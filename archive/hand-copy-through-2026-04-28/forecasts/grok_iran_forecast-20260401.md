**Forecast Question**  
Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each horizon: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
“Return to functional normality” is defined operationally and observably across domains as:  
- **Direct military activity**: Zero confirmed US/Israeli strikes on Iranian targets or Iranian missile/drone/naval attacks on Gulf shipping/assets for ≥30 consecutive days.  
- **Proxy activity**: Hezbollah/Houthi/militia attacks on Gulf or related targets at ≤5 incidents per month (verified via UKMTO/CENTCOM reporting).  
- **Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters**: Sustained transit with <1 confirmed attack per month and US/coalition naval escort presence at pre-war levels.  
- **Commercial transit levels**: Average daily vessel crossings ≥100 (≥80% of pre-war baseline of ~135–138), sustained for ≥30 days (via AIS/tanker-tracker data).  
- **War-risk insurance conditions**: Premiums ≤0.5% of hull value (renewable without special US backstop), full P&I coverage available without force majeure clauses.  
- **Oil and LNG export continuity**: Gulf exports (Saudi, Qatar, UAE, Kuwait, Iraq) at ≥90% of pre-war volumes (IEA/Kpler data); no force majeure declarations; Iranian exports secondary.  
- **Energy market stress and volatility**: Brent crude volatility index ≤ historical peacetime average (~15–20%); no sustained supply shock premiums.  
- **Force posture of major actors**: US/Israeli/Gulf forces at DEFCON-equivalent 4 or below with partial drawdowns; no heightened alerts or mine-laying.  
- **Absence of major new escalation triggers**: No strikes on Kharg Island oil facilities, Ras Tanura, Ras Laffan, or equivalent for ≥60 days.  

A temporary ceasefire or battlefield pause does not qualify. Lower oil prices alone do not qualify. Commercial normalization must lag military pauses by weeks due to insurer and buyer risk aversion.

**Current Situation Snapshot**  
As of 1 April 2026 (~Day 34 since onset), daily transits through the Strait of Hormuz remain suppressed at 5–11 vessels per day (primarily dark/ghost-fleet movements carrying Iranian oil to China under selective Iranian clearance), representing a 92–95% drop from pre-war baseline. Iran continues a selective passage regime, permitting limited sanctioned flows while attacking or denying others; at least 21–25 commercial vessels have been hit since hostilities began. No verified large-scale coalition escorted convoys have executed amid persistent mine/ISR and proxy threats. War-risk premiums remain elevated at 1–10%+ of hull value, with P&I clubs maintaining cancellations or prohibitive repricing; safety concerns (not mere availability) continue to drive reduced traffic. QatarEnergy force majeure on LNG contracts persists with Ras Laffan repairs projected at 3–5 years for 17% of capacity. Oman-mediated indirect US-Iran talks register “encouraging signals” and a reported US 15-point draft, but no public checkpoints, concessions, or framework have materialized; a US ultimatum on power-plant strikes has been postponed five days. Houthi statements indicate potential Bab el-Mandeb activation as a secondary front. Gulf crude exports west of Hormuz show no recovery.

**Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Reference classes (outside-in base rates before case-specific adjustment):  

1. **Iran-Iraq Tanker War (1980–88)**: Relevant for identical chokepoint attacks, insurance spikes, and export halts. Timeline to functional commercial recovery: 6–18+ months post-ceasefire once escorts and premiums aligned. Breaks down because modern coalition naval assets and satellite tracking allow faster threat suppression than in the 1980s. Suggests gradual long-term stabilization only.  

2. **2019–2020 Iranian tanker/Abqaiq attacks**: Relevant for short-duration Hormuz disruptions and temporary premium surges. Recovery: commercial volumes and insurance terms returned to baseline within 1–3 months after de-escalation. Breaks down due to far lower intensity and absence of state-on-state missile exchanges. Suggests modest short-term probabilities if fighting ends abruptly.  

3. **Red Sea Houthi crisis (2023–ongoing as of early 2026)**: Relevant for proxy-driven rerouting, elevated war-risk quotes, and partial export adjustments. Partial normalization: 6–12 months before premiums fell and schedules stabilized. Breaks down because scale was smaller and no direct Iranian state involvement. Suggests commercial recovery trails military calm by months.  

4. **1991 Gulf War**: Relevant for rapid kinetic phase followed by coalition naval clearance. Timeline: 1–3 months to restored transit and insurance norms. Breaks down because current conflict lacks decisive ground component or immediate regime outcome. Suggests faster recovery only under clear military resolution.  

5. **2003 Iraq War opening phase**: Relevant for Gulf-area port and export interruptions during regime-change fighting. Recovery: shipping and volumes normalized in 2–6 months once major combat subsided. Breaks down because Hormuz itself was never closed.
