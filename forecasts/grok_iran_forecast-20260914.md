**1. Forecast Question**  
Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each horizon: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
Functional normality requires simultaneous, observable satisfaction of all of the following for a sustained period (minimum 14 consecutive days for short horizons; 30+ days for longer ones), measured against pre-28 February 2026 baselines:

- Direct military activity: No reciprocal US/Iran or Israel/Iran strikes on military or dual-use targets inside Iran, on Gulf host-nation soil, or on major surface/air assets in the Gulf/Hormuz/Gulf of Oman. No declared restricted or prohibited zones enforced by kinetic means.
- Proxy activity: No significant Houthi, Iraqi militia, or other Iranian-aligned attacks on Gulf energy infrastructure, shipping, or Saudi/UAE/Bahrain/Kuwait territory that produce measurable damage or force majeure declarations.
- Shipping security (Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters): Unrestricted commercial transit under the normal Traffic Separation Scheme (or equivalent mutually accepted routing) without compulsory Iranian permits, fees, “insurance,” or US naval escort/authorization requirements. AIS-visible large commercial vessel transits ≥80% of pre-war daily average (~100+ vessels/day sustained).
- Commercial transit levels: Throughput of crude, products, and LNG through Hormuz recovering to ≥85% of January–February 2026 volumes, with no large stranded-vessel backlog and no systematic dark/spoofed transits required for safety.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Additional war-risk premiums for Hormuz transit returned to ≤2–3× pre-war levels (pre-war baseline ~0.1% of hull value or lower; current multiples reported 15–40×). Capacity freely available without special consortia or government backstops as the dominant mechanism.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf exporters (Saudi, UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Iraq) able to meet contractual volumes without reliance on force-majeure notices, major pipeline diversions as primary routes, or cargo transfers at sea as routine workarounds. Iranian exports flowing under whatever sanctions regime exists without kinetic interdiction as the binding constraint.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent and related markers no longer embedding a persistent Hormuz-risk premium that keeps prices elevated solely due to transit risk (distinct from broader supply/demand). Implied volatility and term-structure contango/backwardation consistent with non-crisis regimes.
- Force posture of major actors: Measurable reduction in forward-deployed US combat power and Iranian anti-access/area-denial posture in the immediate Hormuz approaches relative to peak 2026 levels; no ongoing large-scale carrier/escort operations framed as blockade enforcement.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new strikes on critical energy nodes (East-West pipeline, Kharg, major refineries, LNG trains), no mining campaigns, no major loss of life among seafarers that resets insurance and operator risk models.

A temporary ceasefire, partial traffic recovery under military management, lower oil prices driven by demand destruction or SPR releases, or a battlefield pause without commercial confidence does **not** qualify.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant classes (selected for maritime chokepoint disruption + regional great-power/proxy war characteristics):

- Tanker War (Iran–Iraq, 1980–88, especially 1984–88 peak): Prolonged low-intensity attacks on shipping; traffic never fully normalized until war’s end and subsequent diplomacy. Partial commercial adaptation occurred, but insurance and risk premia stayed elevated for years. Timeline to functional normality measured in years after major combat ended.
- 2019–2021 Gulf tanker attacks / Iranian seizures + max-pressure period: Discrete attacks and seizures produced multi-month insurance spikes and routing caution; recovery to near-normal commercial conditions required sustained absence of incidents plus political de-escalation (months to >1 year).
- Red Sea / Houthi campaign (late 2023–ongoing into 2026 context): Even limited asymmetric attacks produced sharp traffic diversion, extreme insurance rates, and multi-quarter commercial avoidance; full normalization still incomplete years later despite intermittent pauses.
- Suez closures and major canal disruptions (1956, 1967–75): Physical or effective closure produced multi-month to multi-year commercial reorientation; reopening required political settlement plus clearance and confidence rebuilding.
- 1990–91 Gulf War maritime phase and post-ceasefire period: Rapid military victory allowed relatively fast commercial recovery (weeks to a few months for basic transit), but residual risk and force posture lingered. Less relevant because the 2026 conflict features contested control of the waterway itself rather than a clear conventional defeat.
- 2011–12 Strait of Hormuz rhetoric + sanctions episodes: Threats alone moved insurance and pricing; actual low-level incidents produced months of elevated risk.

Base-rate prior (pre-case-specific evidence, blending these classes weighted toward prolonged asymmetric/contested-control cases):  
1 week ≈ 1–3%; 1 month ≈ 5–10%; 3 months ≈ 15–25%; 6 months ≈ 30–40%; 1 year ≈ 45–55%; 18 months ≈ 55–65%. These reflect that commercial and insurance normalization typically lags military pauses by months to years when sovereignty over the waterway is contested and proxies remain active.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**  
Ranked: (1) Durable US–Iran political/security arrangement that settles Hormuz administration and reduces kinetic risk; (2) Sustained absence of attacks on shipping and energy infrastructure long enough for insurers and operators to reprice; (3) Restoration or expansion of bypass capacity (pipelines, alternative loading); (4) Gulf-state consensus that reduces Iranian incentive to use shipping as leverage; (5) Exhaustion or internal Iranian political constraints that favor de-escalation.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**  
Ranked: (1) Contested sovereignty/administration of Hormuz itself (Iranian insistence on control vs US free-navigation/blockade posture); (2) Active proxy and militia options (Houthis, Iraqi groups) that can re-escalate at low cost; (3) Insurance and operator risk models that require long clean periods and political settlement before full capacity returns; (4) Mutual domestic and alliance incentives that reward demonstrating resolve; (5) Second-order effects (pipeline damage, Red Sea pressure, export shortfalls) that keep markets and military postures elevated.

**8. Decomposed Sub-Forecasts** (approximate probabilities of each component being satisfied by horizon; these are inputs, not independent)

- Major sustained reduction in direct US/Iran kinetic activity: 1w ~5–10%; 1m ~15–25%; 3m ~35–45%; 6m ~50–60%; 1y ~60–70%; 18m ~65–75%.  
- Sustained safe commercial transit (high volume, no compulsory permits/escorts): 1w <5%; 1m ~10%; 3m ~25%; 6m ~40%; 1y ~50–55%; 18m ~55–65%.  
- Meaningful insurance normalization (multiples collapsing toward low single digits × baseline): lags transit safety; 1w ~0–2%; 1m ~5%; 3m ~15–20%; 6m ~30–40%; 1y ~45–55%; 18m ~55–65%.  
- Oil/LNG export recovery to near-normal contractual levels without force majeure: 1w ~5%; 1m ~15%; 3m ~30%; 6m ~45%; 1y ~55%; 18m ~60–70%.  
- Proxy restraint: 1w ~10%; 1m ~20%; 3m ~35%; 6m ~45%; 1y ~55%; 18m ~60%.  
- Force drawdown / posture normalization: lags political deal; lower than kinetic reduction.  
- No new major escalation trigger: declines with time as opportunities accumulate.

Gating factors are safe high-volume transit + insurance capacity + absence of new kinetic triggers. Political settlement is the strongest accelerator; its absence keeps the joint probability low.

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Prolonged “no war, no peace” with intermittent kinetic spikes, managed low-volume transit under dual pressure (US and Iranian), elevated insurance, and partial bypass reliance. Traffic recovers modestly in quiet windows but does not reach definition thresholds until a political arrangement addresses Hormuz administration. Normalization, if it occurs, clusters in the 9–18 month window after a deal rather than before one.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
A verified, monitored US–Iran framework (possibly with Omani/Gulf facilitation) that settles routing, removes blockade incentives, and produces 45–60 consecutive clean days. Insurance capacity returns, operators resume, and proxies are reined in. Probability of this path is the main upside driver for the 3–12 month horizons.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Hormuz remains a contested zone with episodic attacks, dual claims of control, active proxies, and structural insurance elevation. Gulf exporters adapt via partial pipelines, floating storage, and demand destruction; global markets reprice a permanent risk premium. Functional normality under the stated definition stays out of reach past 18 months.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Possible over-estimation of normalization: Historical tanker-war and Red Sea cases show commercial and insurance recovery can lag political pauses by years when sovereignty is unresolved; the definition is deliberately demanding and may never be fully met if dual administration becomes the new normal. Possible under-estimation: Economic pain on all parties (Iranian export needs, Gulf fiscal pressure, global inflation sensitivity, US domestic energy-price politics) could force a pragmatic arrangement faster than pure security logic suggests. Load-bearing assumption: that contested control of the waterway itself is the binding constraint rather than residual nuclear or proxy issues. Evidence that would prove the view wrong: sustained high-volume AIS traffic + collapsing war-risk multiples without a formal political deal, or a rapid formal deal that produces immediate commercial recovery.
