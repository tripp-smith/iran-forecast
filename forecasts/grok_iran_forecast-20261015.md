**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each of these horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months. Current date is 15 October 2026 (approximately day 230 of the conflict, which began 28 February 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality requires simultaneous, sustained (minimum 2–4 weeks of continuous performance, not a single spike) achievement of the following observable thresholds across domains. A temporary ceasefire, isolated oil-price dip, or battlefield pause does not meet the standard.

- **Direct military activity**: No ongoing high-tempo kinetic exchanges (US/Israeli strikes on Iranian territory or forces, or Iranian missile/drone barrages targeting Gulf states, US assets, or shipping) at a rate that disrupts commercial operations. Residual low-level incidents possible but not systematic.
- **Proxy activity**: Houthis and other Iran-aligned proxies not conducting systematic attacks on commercial shipping in the Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb or energy infrastructure in the Gulf. Sporadic incidents tolerable if they do not drive sustained insurance or routing responses.
- **Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters**: Official threat assessments (UKMTO, JMIC) at low or moderate; no frequent projectile, mine, or small-boat attacks on commercial vessels; routine transit possible without mandatory special permits from Iranian authorities or continuous naval escort for the majority of traffic.
- **Commercial transit levels**: AIS-visible commercial crossings averaging ≥70% of pre-war baseline (~85/day → ≥60/day sustained), including both inbound and outbound, without large numbers of vessels waiting or AIS-dark.
- **War-risk insurance conditions**: Additional war-risk premiums for Hormuz transit routinely available at ≤1–2% of hull value (or ≤5–10× peacetime baseline of ~0.1–0.25%), with capacity in the commercial market and without heavy reliance on government reinsurance facilities. No widespread 72-hour cancellation notices.
- **Oil and LNG export continuity**: Crude and product flows through Hormuz sustained at ≥80% of pre-war average (~17 million b/d crude equivalent → ≥13–14 million b/d), with LNG cargoes similarly recovering. Gulf-wide exports (including bypasses) near pre-war levels without chronic disruption from attacks or blockades.
- **Energy market stress and volatility**: Brent and refined product prices reflecting supply/demand fundamentals rather than acute attack-driven spikes; realized volatility and the geopolitical risk premium materially reduced from current elevated levels (~$100+ Brent).
- **Force posture of major actors**: Significant drawdown of US naval blockade of Iranian ports and reduction in continuous high-end escort/carrier presence; Iranian IRGC Navy not maintaining high-tempo denial posture or claiming exclusive control requiring permits.
- **Absence of major new escalation triggers**: No new large-scale mine-laying, expansion of blockade, major missile campaigns against Gulf energy infrastructure, or proxy surges that re-elevate the threat level.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot**

As of 15 October 2026 the war continues without an effective ceasefire. A June Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding collapsed in early July; both sides have since exchanged fire, though US strikes on Iranian territory appear limited in recent weeks. President Trump has publicly pledged no US attacks on Iran before the 3 November midterms while maintaining the naval blockade of Iranian ports and building forces (including a third carrier).

Strait of Hormuz commercial traffic is severely restricted. AIS-visible crossings are ~9–12 per day (roughly 10% of the pre-crisis ~85/day baseline), with hundreds of vessels waiting. Recent weeks have seen the highest tempo of attacks on shipping since the war began (multiple double-digit incident days; at least one large container ship sunk; IMO tally exceeding 100 incidents and ~24 seafarer deaths). Iran claims control and requires permits; the US coordinates some escorted or night transits. Oil flows through the strait have been volatile—recovering toward 80% of pre-war levels at times in September/early October before crashing again amid the latest attack surge (Kpler and other trackers report days as low as ~3 million b/d against ~17 million b/d baseline). Brent is trading in the $100–107 range with elevated volatility and a persistent geopolitical premium. War-risk premiums are reported in the 1.5–10% of hull value range (roughly 15–40× peacetime), with capacity strained and government facilities under discussion or limited uptake. Proxies (especially Houthis) remain active against shipping and Saudi/Gulf targets. Force postures on both sides remain elevated.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant reference classes (3–6):

- **1980s Tanker War (Iran–Iraq, ~1984–88)**: Closest maritime analogue. Hundreds of attacks occurred over years; oil continued to flow at reduced volumes with US escorts (Operation Earnest Will). Full commercial normalization followed the UN ceasefire and end of hostilities, not during the fighting. Relevance: same waterway, Iranian asymmetric capabilities, major-power naval involvement. Breakdown: current war includes direct US/Israeli air campaign against Iran and explicit blockade of Iranian ports; Iran’s stake in denying the strait is higher while its own exports are blocked. Suggests partial protected transit possible within months of de-escalation, full normality only after durable end to hostilities (many months to years).
- **Red Sea/Houthi campaign (2023–present)**: Asymmetric attacks on shipping drove large-scale diversion for 1–2+ years. Partial traffic recovery has occurred, but full normality (pre-crisis volumes, low insurance, free routing) has not returned even after years. Relevance: proxy maritime denial, insurance reaction, commercial avoidance. Breakdown: Hormuz has far fewer alternatives and higher energy concentration. Suggests multi-year residual effects even after military pressure.
- **1990–91 Gulf War**: Short, high-intensity conflict with limited sustained maritime disruption to Hormuz itself; rapid commercial recovery after ceasefire and expulsion of Iraqi forces. Relevance: regional war involving Gulf energy. Breakdown: far shorter duration and clearer military outcome. Suggests rapid (weeks–months) recovery once fighting ends.
- **2003 Iraq War**: Limited direct impact on Hormuz transit; commercial recovery measured in weeks after major combat operations. Relevance: US-led campaign in the region. Breakdown: no sustained Iranian denial campaign.
- **Suez Canal closures (1967–75 and shorter modern disruptions)**: Multi-year full closure produced lasting routing changes; shorter blockages recovered faster. Relevance: chokepoint economics. Breakdown: physical vs. war-risk closure.

**Base-rate prior** (before current-case adjustment): Probability of functional normality by horizon roughly 5% (1 week), 10% (1 month), 20% (3 months), 35% (6 months), 55% (1 year), 70% (18 months). These reflect that full multi-domain commercial normalization in a contested Hormuz-type scenario is rare without a durable political/military settlement, and residual insurance and behavioral effects persist.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

Ranked: (1) US political constraint around midterms reducing near-term strike tempo; (2) mutual interest in avoiding total economic collapse (Iran needs revenue pathways eventually; US/Gulf need exports); (3) existence of bypass infrastructure and non-Hormuz export options that reduce absolute leverage; (4) historical pattern that protected or selective transit can resume faster than full normality once attack tempo drops.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

Ranked: (1) Ongoing high-tempo Iranian attacks on shipping and explicit claim of control/permits; (2) absence of durable political settlement and continued US blockade; (3) insurance market pricing and commercial avoidance that lag military pauses; (4) active proxies; (5) residual force postures and risk of post-midterm escalation; (6) second-order effects (refinery damage, inventory draws, buyer caution) that slow commercial recovery even if kinetic activity falls.

Gating factors are shipping security + insurance + transit volumes; military pauses alone are insufficient.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued elevated military and proxy activity through and immediately after US midterms, with intermittent oil-flow recoveries interrupted by attack surges. Commercial traffic and insurance remain severely constrained for months. A political deal or mutual restraint becomes more plausible in H1 2027, enabling gradual protected or selective transit recovery, followed by slower insurance and behavioral normalization. Full multi-domain functional normality is more likely in the 12–18 month window than earlier, contingent on settlement.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Rapid post-midterm deal that ends the blockade, halts Iranian shipping attacks, and restrains proxies. Traffic rebounds above 70% within weeks of compliance; insurance premiums fall sharply as loss experience improves. Probability of this path is low (~10–15% cumulative by 6 months) given current maximalist positions and attack incentives.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Post-midterm US escalation, Iranian mine-laying or sustained high-tempo denial, proxy surge, or breakdown of any talks. Traffic stays depressed, premiums remain extreme, and force postures stay high for 12+ months. This path has material probability (~25–35% that normality is not reached even by 18 months).

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Strongest critique of faster-normalization bias: The definition is demanding (insurance + transit + proxies + force posture simultaneously). Current attack surge and blockade show Iran retains effective denial capacity; commercial actors respond to risk faster than governments. Historical Tanker War lasted years.  
Strongest critique of underestimating normalization: Mutual economic pain is severe; US political incentives to declare success and reduce oil prices are high; partial protected transit has occurred before and could scale if attack tempo drops even without full political settlement. Insurance markets reprice quickly once loss experience improves. Load-bearing assumptions are that Iranian attack incentives remain high while the blockade continues, and that commercial/insurance hysteresis is substantial.
