I will ground the forecast in current reporting on the 2026 Iran War and Hormuz disruption, then build reference-class priors before case-specific updates.

The Gulf is not near functional normality. On 9 October 2026 it is day 222–224 of the Hormuz crisis that began with the closure on 28 February 2026. Direct strikes and shipping attacks resumed after the 17 June memorandum of understanding, war-risk cover is still a gating cost, and AIS-visible commercial transit is roughly 5–12 percent of the pre-crisis baseline.

## 1. Forecast Question

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality, under the operational definition below, by each horizon measured from 9 October 2026: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months.

These are cumulative probabilities that the definition is met by that date, not the probability that the transition occurs inside the interval.

## 2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”

All of the following must hold at the same time, for a continuous 14 days, and without a military escort or ad-hoc waiver regime as the normal mode of commerce.

- Direct military activity. No sustained kinetic strikes by the United States, Israel, or Iran against each other’s forces, bases, or energy infrastructure in the Gulf, Strait of Hormuz, or Gulf of Oman, and no pattern of weekly ship attacks by either side.
- Proxy activity. Houthi, Iraqi militia, and other Iran-linked attacks on Gulf shipping and energy infrastructure are back to sporadic pre-war levels, not a campaign.
- Shipping security. The strait is open to ordinary commercial routing in Omani and Iranian territorial waters without Iranian transit permits, US naval coordination, or a blockade line as the operating system.
- Commercial transit. AIS-visible commercial transits are at least 70 percent of the pre-crisis baseline of about 85 vessels a day (IMF PortWatch), so at least about 60 a day, including tankers and container ships, not only dark-fleet or shuttle movements.
- War-risk insurance. Quoted voyage war-risk for a standard VLCC is below 1 percent of hull value, or under about 4 times the pre-war premium of roughly 0.25 percent. Cover is available from major P&I clubs without extraordinary exclusions.
- Oil and LNG continuity. Combined Gulf crude and condensate exports are within 15 percent of the pre-war level of roughly 20 million barrels a day, moved mainly by ordinary commercial tankers rather than emergency ship-to-ship transfers, AIS-dark runs, and bypass pipelines. LNG cargoes exiting through Hormuz are at least 60 percent of the pre-war monthly rate of about 85–100 cargoes. Structural loss at Ras Laffan is treated as damage, not as normality.
- Energy-market stress. Freight is no longer the dominant component of delivered Gulf crude cost. Brent may be anywhere; price level alone does not count.
- Force posture. The US wartime blockade of Iranian ports has ended or reverted to ordinary sanctions enforcement. Large US naval and air reinforcements tied to the Hormuz fight are drawing down, even if a residual presence remains.
- Escalation triggers. No new major trigger in the prior 30 days: mine campaign, missile salvos on Gulf states, carrier or capital-ship strike, or a fresh nuclear-site strike cycle.

A ceasefire, a lower oil price, or a convoy pause is not normality.

## 3. Current Situation Snapshot

Confirmed or strongly supported as of 9 October 2026:

- Crisis day 222–224 since the 28 February closure. AIS-visible traffic over the past week is about 10 ships a day against 84.8 before the crisis; IMF PortWatch recorded 4 commercial transits on 4 October, about 5 percent of typical. Tanker crossings are a small fraction of the pre-war ~48 a day.
- Attacks are current, not historical. UKMTO has logged at least one incident a day in Hormuz or the Gulf of Aden since 2 October. The Kuwaiti VLCC Kazimah III was hit on 1 October; the Liberian Aframax Lipsi was hit on 4 October. Kpler recorded the lowest visible commodity-vessel count in two months early this week.
- Insurance is the binding constraint. Marsh in July put Gulf war-risk at 3–10 percent of hull versus about 0.25 percent pre-war. Trade-press estimates still put the multiple near 30 times, with VLCC voyage quotes up to about $10 million. Several major clubs have restricted cover.
- The 17 June Islamabad MoU reopened a US-facilitated corridor and produced a traffic peak (JMIC recorded 54 transits on 24 June) but did not settle control of the strait, the blockade, frozen funds, or the nuclear file. Iran still claims permit authority. Washington rejected Tehran’s late-September conditions for a fuller reopening.
- Oil has partially adapted, which is not the same as normalization. Bypass pipelines, Gulf of Oman loadings, and ship-to-ship transfers have lifted regional exports toward pre-war totals on some trackers, while Hormuz crude crossings remain well below baseline and a large volume stays shut in or rerouted. LNG is worse: about 19–21 cargoes exited in September versus 85–100 a month pre-war, and part of Ras Laffan capacity was reported damaged in March.
- The US blockade of Iranian ports remains. Container majors have been rerouting since early March. Houthis are hitting Saudi-linked shipping in the Gulf of Aden. Brent is about $103.

Disputed: exact barrels through the strait (US official figures have run well above independent trackers), whether lanes are mined, and how long Ras Laffan repairs take. Belligerent strike claims, especially IRGC claims of hits on US warships, are discounted where CENTCOM contradicts them.

## 4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior

1. Tanker War, 1984–88, and Operation Earnest Will. Closest maritime analog: multi-year attacks on tankers, mining, reflagging, escorts, and elevated war-risk while the underlying war continued. Functional commercial normalization followed the August 1988 ceasefire, with premiums falling over roughly 12–24 months, then reset by the 1990 invasion of Kuwait. Breaks down because 2026 adds a great-power blockade, a nuclear dispute, and missile reach into Gulf states. Suggests no normality while the war’s political issues are open, then 6–18 months after a real ceasefire.

2. Houthi Red Sea campaign, late 2023–2025. Container lines exited quickly and returned slowly even after attack rates fell. Insurance and routing normalized on a lag of many months, not weeks. Relevant to liner behavior and insurer memory. Breaks down because Hormuz has no equivalent long bypass for Gulf crude and LNG. Suggests 6–18 months after the threat actually drops.

3. 2019 Gulf tanker and Abqaiq attacks. Short spike, rapid commercial recovery, because neither side wanted a war. Relevant only to the speed of insurance and freight repair after a contained incident. Breaks down completely if applied to a seven-month war. Suggests weeks to a couple of months after threat removal, not before.

4. Black Sea shipping under the Russia-Ukraine war, 2022 onward. A corridor can function under fire, but war-risk, escorts, and political risk do not return to baseline while the war continues. Relevant upper bound on “managed abnormality.” Suggests functional normality is unlikely inside an active war.

5. 1990–91 Gulf War. Hormuz itself stayed open; the oil shock was mostly the loss of Kuwaiti and Iraqi supply, which took years to restore. Relevant to export recovery lags and Gulf-state hedging, not to strait closure.

6. Suez closure, 1967–75. Extreme political closure of a chokepoint for eight years. Weak as a base rate, useful as a tail: if Hormuz becomes a sovereignty dispute rather than a wartime tactic, multi-year disruption is possible.

Base-rate prior before this week’s evidence, for a still-active chokepoint war at month seven, using the demanding definition:

- 1 week: 2 percent
- 1 month: 5 percent
- 3 months: 12 percent
- 6 months: 25 percent
- 1 year: 40 percent
- 18 months: 52 percent

The prior already assumes most historical cases do not normalize while fighting and the political dispute continue, and that commercial repair lags any ceasefire.

## 6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization

1. Both sides have already accepted a paper framework (17 June MoU) and have used mediators (Pakistan, Qatar, Oman). A corridor has been run before.
2. Oil-market adaptation — Petroline, ADCOP, Goreh-Jask, Gulf of Oman loadings — reduces the acute price shock and the political cost of delay, which can support a transactional deal even without a strategic settlement.
3. Iran’s economic damage and the US political cost of a long war create a recurring incentive to pause. Traffic did jump within days of the June deal.
4. Insurers in the Tanker War cut rates once a ceasefire held. The pricing mechanism is reversible; it is not a permanent regime shift.
5. Asian buyers still want the barrels and cargoes. Demand pull returns quickly if the security and insurance gates open.

## 7. Key Drivers Against Normalization

1. The political dispute is unresolved: strait jurisdiction, the blockade, frozen funds, the nuclear file, and Israel-Lebanon. The MoU treated symptoms.
2. Hormuz is Iran’s remaining leverage. Attacks in the first week of October show Tehran is still using it, and Washington is still answering with strikes and interdiction.
3. Insurance and liner behavior lag any ceasefire. Red Sea experience says owners do not treat the first quiet fortnight as a regime change.
4. Proxy fronts, especially the Houthis, can keep war-risk maps wide even if US-Iran strikes pause.
5. Physical damage, particularly reported Ras Laffan train losses, can fail the LNG leg of the definition even after the waterway opens.
6. Force posture is still wartime: blockade, carrier presence, and a large assisted-transit apparatus.

Ranked: political settlement and the end of ship attacks are the gates. Insurance, liner return, and LNG repair are the lags. Bypass capacity is an adaptation, not a path to this definition.

Synthesis rule: normality is the joint event. Insurance and unescorted transit are the binding commercial gates; they almost never clear while strikes and the blockade continue. The joint probability is therefore close to the minimum of the insurance and transit lines, with a further haircut because LNG damage and proxy activity can fail the definition even after crude moves. It is not an average.

## 9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon

### 1 week
- Probability: 1%
- Base-rate anchor: 2%
- Current-evidence adjustment: down, because attacks were logged this week and traffic is about one-tenth of normal.
- Core rationale: A 14-day clean window cannot exist inside seven days, and the insurance and transit gates are far from the threshold.
- Main gating factor: ongoing ship attacks and war-risk pricing.
- What would raise this forecast materially: nothing plausible inside seven days can meet the definition.
- What would lower this forecast materially: already near the floor.
- Confidence in this estimate: High

### 1 month
- Probability: 3%
- Base-rate anchor: 5%
- Current-evidence adjustment: down slightly. A surprise deal is possible; commercial repair is not.
- Core rationale: Even a sudden MoU revival would leave insurers, liner networks, and the blockade behind the definition. June showed traffic can rise fast and still fail normality.
- Main gating factor: insurance availability and the blockade, not the headline ceasefire.
- What would raise this forecast materially: a signed deal that lifts the blockade, stops IRGC ship attacks, and brings major clubs back inside two weeks.
- What would lower this forecast materially: another September-style tanker exchange or mining.
- Confidence in this estimate: High

### 3 months
- Probability: 8%
- Base-rate anchor: 12%
- Current-evidence adjustment: down. Late-September diplomacy failed, and October attacks reset insurer memory.
- Core rationale: Three months is enough for one more negotiation cycle and a partial corridor. It is not enough, on the Red Sea and Tanker War lags, for unescorted 70 percent transit plus sub-4× war-risk plus LNG recovery, unless a cleaner political deal lands early in the window.
- Main gating factor: whether Washington and Tehran trade blockade relief for a real shipping stand-down.
- What would raise this forecast materially: a ceasefire that holds 45 days and a published cut in war-risk quotes toward 1 percent of hull.
- What would lower this forecast materially: strikes on Gulf-state energy assets, or a declared Iranian transit-fee law enforced by seizures.
- Confidence in this estimate: Medium

### 6 months
- Probability: 18%
- Base-rate anchor: 25%
- Current-evidence adjustment: down. The war’s issues look more like a grinding leverage contest than a closing file, and the Houthi front widens the risk map.
- Core rationale: Six months can contain a ceasefire plus the start of insurance normalization, as in 1988–89. The base rate is cut because the nuclear and strait-sovereignty disputes are still open and physical LNG damage may independently fail the definition.
- Main gating factor: a durable political pause long enough for clubs and liner operators to change rules.
- What would raise this forecast materially: blockade lifted, 30 days without ship attacks, and two major container lines restoring schedules.
- What would lower this forecast materially: Israeli re-entry strikes or a mine campaign.
- Confidence in this estimate: Medium

### 1 year
- Probability: 35%
- Base-rate anchor: 40%
- Current-evidence adjustment: modestly down. Adaptation reduces urgency; unresolved leverage and damaged infrastructure slow the joint event.
- Core rationale: A year is long enough for war-weariness, a second or third deal attempt, and insurer re-entry if a ceasefire holds for a quarter. It is not long enough to treat normalization as the modal outcome. The joint gate still fails in most paths because proxy activity or LNG repair or a revived blockade can break the definition.
- Main gating factor: conversion of a ceasefire into ordinary commercial rules, not another escorted corridor.
- What would raise this forecast materially: nuclear file parked, blockade ended, and war-risk under 1 percent of hull for a full quarter.
- What would lower this forecast materially: collapse of talks into a blockade-and-attacks equilibrium that both sides find tolerable.
- Confidence in this estimate: Low-medium

### 18 months
- Probability: 48%
- Base-rate anchor: 52%
- Current-evidence adjustment: small downward haircut for structural LNG loss and the chance Hormuz stays a negotiated sovereignty issue.
- Core rationale: By spring 2028 the modal path is a messy political settlement or a frozen conflict with a commercially usable strait, but not yet a high-confidence return to the full definition. The probability stays under 50 percent because the definition requires insurance, unescorted transit, LNG, proxy restraint, and force posture together. Historical chokepoint wars often leave a residual risk premium past 18 months.
- Main gating factor: whether the post-war order removes Iranian veto practice over routing, rather than merely pricing it.
- What would raise this forecast materially: a formal maritime agreement with Oman and Iran, club cover restored, and LNG cargoes sustainably above 60 percent of baseline.
- What would lower this forecast materially: legalized Iranian tolls, permanent US convoy architecture, or unrepaired Qatari trains plus continued Houthi interdiction.
- Confidence in this estimate: Low

## 10. Most Likely Path

Through early 2027 the system stays in managed abnormality: intermittent US-Iran strikes, a thin US-facilitated or Iranian-permitted flow, high war-risk, dark and shuttle tankers, bypass pipelines carrying a large share of crude, LNG far below baseline, and container services mostly absent. A further political pause is more likely than a strategic settlement inside six months. Commercial normality, if it comes, arrives after the pause, not with it — insurers first, owners second, liner networks last. The single most likely state at 18 months is a partially reopened strait that still fails at least one clause of the definition, usually insurance or LNG.

## 11. Faster Normalization Case

A blockade-for-stand-down deal in the next one to two months, enforced against IRGC naval units and accompanied by a 60-day attack-free window, could pull the 6-month probability toward 35 percent and the 1-year probability toward 55 percent. The June traffic spike shows physical transit can recover in days. The 2019 precedent shows insurance can follow a contained political signal. This case requires Gulf states to pressure both patrons, and Iran to judge that leverage has been spent.

## 12. Prolonged Instability Case

If both sides decide the current equilibrium is sustainable — US interdiction of Iranian exports, Iranian harassment of everyone else, Asian buyers using bypass and dark fleets — functional normality can stay below 25 percent at 18 months. Triggers for that path are a mine campaign, a fatal attack on a US warship, Israeli strikes that restart the missile exchange, or an Iranian law authorizing seizures and tolls. The Suez-closure tail is still the wrong base rate, but a politicized strait can outlast the kinetic war.

## 13. Red-Team Critique

The forecast may understate normalization if “functional” is interpreted by markets as “barrels are moving.” Regional export totals have already approached pre-war levels on some days via bypass and ship-to-ship transfer. A risk team that equates that with normality would mark this forecast too low after six months. That reading is rejected here because the question excludes price relief and emergency routing.

The forecast may overstate normalization at one year and 18 months. Insurer and liner return after the Red Sea campaign was slower than a war-weariness model implies, Ras Laffan damage may be multi-year, and a frozen conflict can look stable while failing the joint definition. The 48 percent at 18 months is the softest number in the set. A serious review could defensibly cut it to the high 30s without changing the near-term estimates.

Load-bearing assumptions: the war began in late February 2026 and is still politically open; Iran retains enough naval and missile capability to tax the strait; the US blockade continues until a deal; private insurance will not normalize under weekly attacks.

## 14. Critical Signals to Monitor

- UKMTO incident frequency and whether attacks hit moving ships or only anchored targets.
- IMF PortWatch and AIS daily transits versus the 60-a-day threshold, and the share that is escorted.
- Quoted VLCC war-risk as a percent of hull, and whether Gard, Skuld, NorthStandard, and the London clubs restore cover.
- JMIC or CENTCOM facilitated-transit counts versus unescorted commercial counts.
- Status of the US blockade and any waiver reinstatement.
- QatarEnergy cargo count and Ras Laffan train status.
- Houthi attack rate in the Gulf of Aden.
- Any formal toll, permit, or seizure statute from Tehran, and any Oman-Iran navigation arrangement.
