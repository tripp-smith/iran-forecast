I will ground the forecast in current reporting on the 2026 Iran War and Gulf maritime conditions, then build the probabilities from reference classes and a decomposed evidence ledger.

The Persian Gulf is not functionally normal on day 222 of the Hormuz crisis. Direct and proxy-linked shipping attacks are still occurring, commercial transit is a fraction of the pre-crisis baseline, and war-risk pricing remains a multiple of peacetime. A June memorandum has not produced a durable settlement.

## 1. Forecast Question

What is the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality, under the operational definition below, by each horizon from 7 October 2026: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months?

## 2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”

All eight conditions must hold together for at least 14 consecutive days. A ceasefire, a lower oil price, or a temporary lull is not sufficient.

- Direct military activity: no confirmed kinetic strikes by the United States, Israel, or Iran against each other’s territory, forces, or commercial shipping in the Gulf theater.
- Proxy activity: no confirmed attacks on Gulf energy, port, or shipping targets attributable to IRGC-linked groups.
- Shipping security: no new mining, seizure, or projectile attacks in the Strait of Hormuz, Gulf of Oman approaches, or main Gulf anchorages.
- Commercial transit: AIS-visible commercial crossings at or above 70 percent of the 2025 baseline of about 85 ships a day, so roughly 60 or more a day, and not dependent on military coordination or Iranian permits as the normal mode.
- Insurance: additional hull war-risk premium at or below about 2 times the pre-crisis baseline, with International Group clubs writing Gulf cover without extraordinary cancellation notices. Pre-crisis was roughly 0.1–0.25 percent of hull value per voyage.
- Oil and LNG continuity: combined Gulf crude, products, and LNG export flows at or above 85 percent of the February 2026 baseline, without relying primarily on AIS-dark, sanctioned, or shuttle systems.
- Market stress: the war-specific risk premium and freight distortion largely unwound. A price level alone does not qualify.
- Force posture and triggers: no active blockade of Iranian ports, no active Iranian interdiction posture, no continuous US strike campaign in the Gulf, and no new major trigger such as a capital-ship hit, mass-casualty strike on a Gulf state, formal closure order, or nuclear breakout move.

## 3. Current Situation Snapshot

The war began around 28 February 2026. A 14-point US–Iran memorandum signed around 17 June was meant to pause hostilities and reopen Hormuz. It did not hold. Tit-for-tat strikes resumed within days, including attacks on shipping and on US-linked sites in Gulf states. As of early October, proximity talks and draft proposals to revive the memorandum are still being exchanged, including an Iranian offer to reopen the strait in exchange for lifting a US blockade of Iranian ports. Washington has described earlier versions as rejected. That diplomatic track is real and unresolved, not a settlement.

Maritime conditions remain abnormal. Independent AIS monitors on 7 October put the strait at “restricted,” day 222, with about 8–9 commercial crossings a day over the past week against a pre-crisis baseline near 85, or roughly 10 percent. IMF PortWatch-style counts for early October are even lower on some days. Hundreds of commercial ships are waiting near Fujairah, Sohar, and Gulf anchorages. UKMTO-reported attacks have accelerated: nine in the waterway already in October, about half of September’s combined Hormuz and Gulf total, with reports on 7 October of injured crew, including Indian seafarers, after a tanker attack off Oman. Bloomberg reported Iran increasing tanker attacks even as some oil and gas flow measures rose.

Oil is the partial exception and should not be read as normality. Kpler and related trackers put September Middle East crude exports near 16.3 million barrels a day, just under 80 percent of the February level of about 19.5 million, still about 3.2 million barrels a day short. Those flows use longer voyages, ship-to-ship transfers, dark transits, and a US-facilitated southern corridor in Omani waters. Container lines largely rerouted from March. War-risk pricing is disputed by source and date, but the live range is still extreme: tracker estimates around 15 times peacetime, other situation reports near 30 times, with voyage quotes for large hulls in the millions of dollars. Brent was reported near $101 on 7 October. Mines, residual threat, and insurer behavior remain binding constraints even when barrels move.

Confirmed versus disputed: the existence of a June memorandum, later breakdown, continued shipping attacks, very low AIS-visible transit, and elevated war-risk premia are well supported across Reuters, maritime monitors, and trade press. The exact oil-flow recovery rate and the share of dark traffic are tracker-dependent and should be treated as approximate. Iranian claims of control and permit requirements, and US claims about the scale of escorted transit, conflict; the observable result is a thin, insecure flow, not an open strait.

## 4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior

The Tanker War, 1984–88, is the closest kinetic analogue. Iran and Iraq attacked hundreds of commercial ships over years. Insurance stayed elevated. The US escort operation Earnest Will from 1987 raised throughput but did not restore normality. Commercial confidence returned only after the August 1988 ceasefire, and even then over months. It breaks down because today’s volume, insurance market, and missile and drone threat are larger, and a nuclear file sits behind the maritime fight. It implies little chance of functional normality while attacks continue, and a multi-month lag after a real stop.

The Red Sea and Houthi campaign from late 2023 into 2025 is the closest commercial analogue. Major carriers diverted for more than a year. Naval presence did not restore Suez traffic. Insurance and routing normalized only as the attack rate fell and a political off-ramp appeared. It breaks down because Hormuz has fewer practical alternatives and the chokepoint state is a principal belligerent. It implies 6–18 months after attacks actually stop before the definition here is met.

The 2019 Gulf tanker and Abqaiq crisis was short. Premia spiked and eased within weeks to a few months once the acute phase passed. It breaks down because it was not a general war and did not include a blockade or sustained mining threat. It is an upper bound on speed only if the current war ends cleanly.

The 1990–91 Gulf War kept Hormuz open and commercial recovery after Desert Storm was relatively fast. It breaks down because Iran was not the maritime belligerent closing the strait. It is a weak prior for this case.

The Black Sea grain corridor, 2022–23, shows an insurance-backed, politically fragile corridor can move cargo without equaling peacetime normality. It breaks down because volumes and the alternative geography differ. It supports treating escorted or politically licensed transit as not normal.

Outside-in prior, before this week’s evidence, for functional normality by each horizon: 1 week 2 percent; 1 month 6 percent; 3 months 12 percent; 6 months 22 percent; 1 year 40 percent; 18 months 55 percent. The prior assumes wars involving the chokepoint state itself rarely produce commercial normality inside a quarter, and that even after a ceasefire the insurance and carrier lag is measured in months.

## 6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization

Ranked: mutual incentive to stop an economically costly war, visible in repeated attempts to revive the June memorandum; partial physical oil recovery, which shows the strait is not physically sealed; Gulf states’ interest in de-escalation after strikes on their territory; US political sensitivity to fuel prices ahead of midterms; insurer willingness to return capacity quickly once a real attack pause is observed, as briefly seen after the June deal.

## 7. Key Drivers Against Normalization

Ranked: Hormuz remains Iran’s principal leverage, so Tehran has a structural reason to keep threat and intermittent attacks alive until a political price is paid; the attack series is current, not historical, with a visible acceleration in early October; insurance, not naval escort, is the binding constraint, and premia reprice on incidents within hours; force postures are still wartime, including a US blockade claim and Iranian permit claims; proxy and Lebanon-Israel dynamics can break a Gulf pause from outside; mine and corridor risk clears slowly; buyers and carriers will not treat a two-week lull as a regime change.

## 10. Most Likely Path

Episodic attacks and talks continue through late 2026. Oil moves in a reduced, expensive, partly dark system at roughly 60–80 percent of pre-war volumes. A more durable pause is more plausible in the first half of 2027 than this quarter, but insurance and carrier behavior trail any military pause by one to three months. The modal state at 12–18 months is a politically managed, still-premium strait, not the definition above. Functional normality is a plausible outcome by 18 months, not the central one.

## 11. Faster Normalization Case

A revived memorandum holds, attacks stop for 45 days, mines are declared cleared in the Omani corridor, and hull premia fall toward 0.5 percent. Gulf states press both sides. Carriers return in stages. That path can meet the definition around six to nine months. It requires Iran to trade immediate Hormuz leverage for sanctions and blockade relief, which it has not yet done on enforceable terms. Probability mass on this path is real but minority, on the order of one in five by month six and higher thereafter.

## 12. Prolonged Instability Case

Talks fail, the blockade remains, and Iran keeps a tanker-for-tanker doctrine. Monthly incidents hold premia in double-digit multiples. Transit oscillates between 5 and 30 percent of normal on AIS. A Lebanon or Iraq shock restarts direct strikes. This path can still export a large share of oil and still fail every commercial clause of the definition through 18 months. It is the main reason the 18-month probability stays below 50 percent.

## 13. Red-Team Critique

The forecast may understate normalization if dark and escorted flows are already closer to a workable system than AIS implies, and if insurers reprice faster than the Red Sea analogue once a pause is credible. The June premium drop shows that speed. It may overstate later normalization by treating war fatigue as likely to produce a bargain. Iran’s leverage rises when the strait is insecure, and US domestic pressure cuts both ways: it can force a deal or another round of strikes. The load-bearing assumption is that insurance and ordinary transit, not barrels moved, define the target. If a user substituted “oil is flowing,” these probabilities would be too low.

## 14. Critical Signals to Monitor

UKMTO incident frequency and casualties. AIS and PortWatch daily transits versus the 60-a-day threshold. Quoted hull war-risk as a percent of value. Whether the US blockade and Iranian permit regime are formally lifted. Mine-clearance statements from Oman, the US, or the IMO. Gulf-state strike claims. Hezbollah-Israel incidents tied by either side to the Gulf file. Any published text of a revived memorandum.
