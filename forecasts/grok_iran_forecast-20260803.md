**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each of these horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

“Return to functional normality” requires simultaneous satisfaction of all of the following observable conditions for a sustained period (not a single day or temporary pause):

- Direct military activity: No sustained or recurrent US/Israel–Iran kinetic exchanges (missile, drone, air, or naval strikes) targeting military infrastructure, leadership, or energy facilities inside Iran or against US/Coalition bases in the Gulf for at least 30 consecutive days.
- Proxy activity: No significant activation or major attacks by Iranian-aligned proxies (Hezbollah, Iraqi militias, Houthis) against Israel, Gulf states, or commercial shipping that produce material damage or casualties.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Absence of attacks, boardings, mine incidents, or deliberate harassment of commercial vessels; no active IRGC “traffic separation” enforcement that requires coordination with Iranian authorities as a condition of safe passage; UKMTO/JMIC threat level no higher than “substantial” on a sustained basis.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily two-way commercial transits (all vessel types) averaging ≥70% of the pre-28 February 2026 baseline (~88–130 vessels/day depending on source) for a rolling 14-day period, with tanker and LNG shares recovering proportionally.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk additional premiums for Hormuz transit ≤1.0% of hull value (pre-war baseline ~0.1–0.25%) and widely available without selective capacity rationing or heavy reliance on government reinsurance facilities.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Aggregate crude + condensate + LNG flows through Hormuz restored to ≥80% of pre-war volumes on a sustained basis, without major rerouting dependence on Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb or overland alternatives as the primary mode.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent front-month volatility normalized (realized 30-day vol not persistently elevated relative to 2024–25 averages) and no persistent risk premium attributable to Hormuz closure risk exceeding ~$5–7/bbl.
- Force posture of major actors: Material drawdown or de-alert of surge US naval and air assets specifically deployed for the Iran campaign; Iranian coastal anti-ship missile/drone/radar posture not held at wartime readiness levels.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No unresolved nuclear “breakout” crisis, leadership succession crisis, or formal Iranian claim of administrative control over the Strait that is actively enforced against international shipping.

A temporary ceasefire, partial reopening under Iranian route control, lower oil prices driven by demand destruction, or a battlefield pause without commercial and insurance normalization does **not** meet the definition.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant historical cases (Gulf/maritime chokepoint/regional war):

1. **Tanker War (Iran–Iraq, 1984–88)**: Multi-year attacks on shipping; traffic never fully stopped but insurance and risk premiums elevated for years. Partial normalization required end of land war + international escorts. Suggests multi-year commercial lag after kinetic peak.
2. **1990–91 Gulf War / Kuwait liberation**: Rapid military victory followed by relatively quick shipping restoration (months), but residual mine threat and insurance lag. Breaks down because Iraq lacked Iran’s geographic control of Hormuz and asymmetric capabilities.
3. **Houthi Red Sea campaign (2023–25)**: Sustained low-intensity attacks produced large traffic diversion, elevated war risk, and incomplete recovery even after partial pauses. Commercial confidence lagged kinetic intensity by many months.
4. **1987–88 US Operation Earnest Will / Bridgeton mining**: Escorts restored traffic relatively quickly once political will was present, but required sustained naval commitment.
5. **Post-2003 Iraq and post-2011 Libya energy/shipping recovery**: Security and commercial normalization lagged formal end of major combat by 12–36+ months.
6. **Iranian mine-laying and harassment episodes (1980s, 2019)**: Short, sharp spikes resolved in weeks to a few months once deterrence or diplomacy restored, but only when Iran chose restraint.

**Base-rate prior** (pre-evidence adjustment, probability of meeting the full operational definition):

- 1 week: <1%
- 1 month: 2–5%
- 3 months: 8–15%
- 6 months: 15–25%
- 1 year: 25–40%
- 18 months: 35–55%

These are deliberately conservative because the definition is multi-domain and demanding; historical chokepoint recoveries rarely achieve simultaneous military, commercial, insurance, and political normalization on short timelines when the controlling actor retains asymmetric leverage.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

Ranked: (1) Mutual economic pain and desire to avoid broader energy shock; (2) US domestic/political preference for deal over prolonged high-tempo operations; (3) Gulf Arab states’ interest in restored flows and stability; (4) Residual Iranian conventional degradation reducing appetite for full-scale escalation; (5) Third-party mediation channels (Oman, Qatar, Pakistan, others).

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

Ranked: (1) Iranian strategic incentive to retain Hormuz leverage as core residual deterrent after leadership and conventional losses; (2) Ambiguity and enforcement problems in any written understanding on Strait administration; (3) Domestic Iranian hardline constraints and succession politics; (4) Israeli interests and potential independent action; (5) Insurance and shipping industry risk aversion that outlasts kinetic pauses; (6) Proxy residual capacity and potential for spoiler attacks.

**9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon**

**1 Week**  
- Probability: 1%  
- Base-rate anchor: <1%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Near-zero; active disruption and no sudden resolution mechanism visible.  
- Core rationale: Even a perfect diplomatic announcement cannot clear mines, restore insurer confidence, or rebuild transit volumes in days.  
- Main gating factor: Physical and commercial lag.  
- What would raise: Immediate verified demining + joint US–Iran–Oman safe-passage regime announced and implemented without incident.  
- What would lower: Further vessel attacks or major kinetic exchange.  
- Confidence: High  

**1 Month**  
- Probability: 5%  
- Base-rate anchor: 2–5%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight upward from pure base rate only if talks produce a more durable text; still low.  
- Core rationale: Historical pauses have produced temporary traffic upticks that reverse on the next incident. Insurance and volume recovery require weeks of clean operations.  
- Main gating factor: Credible, enforceable Strait regime accepted by insurers and major carriers.  
- What would raise: 3+ weeks of incident-free transit at rising volumes + premium collapse.  
- What would lower: Another MOU breakdown or proxy activation.  
- Confidence: Medium-High  

**3 Months**  
- Probability: 15%  
- Base-rate anchor: 8–15%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Modest upward for economic incentives; downward for Iranian leverage retention and repeated prior failures.  
- Core rationale: Possible if a more robust political settlement holds and is stress-tested; still requires simultaneous commercial confidence recovery.  
- Main gating factor: Iranian willingness to relinquish or dilute operational control claims.  
- What would raise: Verified sustained safe transit + insurance market re-entry + force posture signals.  
- What would lower: Leadership politics in Tehran or Israeli independent action.  
- Confidence: Medium  

**6 Months**  
- Probability: 25%  
- Base-rate anchor: 15–25%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Balanced; economic pressure helps, but institutionalization of Iranian Strait claims and insurance memory work against.  
- Core rationale: Time allows for demining, gradual traffic recovery, and possible political settlement, but the multi-domain definition remains demanding.  
- Main gating factor: Durable political settlement that survives domestic politics on both sides.  
- What would raise: Clear US–Iran final agreement covering nuclear, sanctions, and navigation + clean operational record.  
- What would lower: Prolonged low-intensity harassment or new nuclear crisis.  
- Confidence: Medium  

**1 Year**  
- Probability: 40%  
- Base-rate anchor: 25–40%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight upward for cumulative economic and political costs of prolonged disruption.  
- Core rationale: Most historical analogues show partial recovery by this horizon if major combat ends, but full multi-domain normality (especially insurance and zero-harassment transit) often takes longer when the geographic actor retains leverage.  
- Main gating factor: Whether Iran decides residual Hormuz leverage is worth the ongoing economic cost.  
- What would raise: Regime consolidation around a deal or clear conventional deterrence restoration that reduces Iranian need for asymmetric tools.  
- What would lower: Entrenchment of dual-route or Iranian-administered regime that markets reject.  
- Confidence: Medium  

**18 Months**  
- Probability: 50%  
- Base-rate anchor: 35–55%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Anchored near center of base-rate range.  
- Core rationale: Sufficient time for political resolution, physical clearance, and market relearning, yet structural Iranian interest in retaining a veto over Gulf energy flows remains a durable obstacle. Long-horizon certainty is low.  
- Main gating factor: Strategic choice in Tehran about the value of permanent versus temporary leverage.  
- What would raise: Broad regional security architecture that includes Gulf states and reduces Iranian isolation.  
- What would lower: Successful Iranian institutionalization of de-facto control accepted (or tolerated) by major buyers.  
- Confidence: Low-Medium  

**10. Most Likely Path**

Fragile, on-off diplomacy produces intermittent traffic recoveries that repeatedly stall on Iranian enforcement actions or mutual accusations of violation. Transit stabilizes at 20–40% of pre-war levels for extended periods. Insurance remains elevated. Full multi-domain normality is deferred beyond 12 months for most paths; a durable settlement eventually emerges under economic pressure but leaves residual Iranian influence over the Strait that markets partially discount rather than fully normalize.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

A high-level political bargain (nuclear restraints + sanctions relief + explicit free-navigation language with verification) holds for 60–90 days without kinetic breach. Rapid demining, joint monitoring, and Gulf-state buy-in allow volumes and insurance to recover faster than base rate. Probability of this path: low-to-moderate (~15–20% weight).

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Iran prioritizes long-term leverage over short-term economic relief; low-intensity harassment and dual-route enforcement become the new normal. Insurance and major Western carriers remain cautious; shadow and Asian fleets dominate residual traffic. Normalization delayed well beyond 18 months. Probability weight: meaningful (~30–40%).

**13. Red-Team Critique**

The forecast may **overestimate** near- and medium-term probabilities by under-weighting the stickiness of commercial risk aversion and the domestic Iranian incentives to retain Hormuz as residual power. It may **underestimate** longer-term probabilities if cumulative economic damage forces faster Iranian compromise than historical analogues suggest. Load-bearing assumptions include: (a) no sudden regime collapse or major leadership change that resets Iranian strategy, and (b) continued US willingness to sustain elevated presence rather than accept a de-facto Iranian administrative role. Evidence that would most falsify the view: sustained 14-day transit averages above 70% with premiums collapsing below 1% while kinetic activity remains zero.
