**1. Forecast Question**  
Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each horizon: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
Functional normality requires all of the following to hold simultaneously for a sustained period of at least 30 consecutive days, with no major reversion:

- **Direct military activity**: No significant kinetic strikes or major attacks by the US, Israel, Iran, or their forces against military, energy, or commercial targets in the Persian Gulf region.
- **Proxy activity**: Sustained restraint by Houthis and other Iran-linked proxies from major attacks on shipping, Gulf infrastructure, or related targets.
- **Shipping security (Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters)**: Waters free of active mine threats, effective interdiction, or attacks; vessels able to transit without routine dependence on US naval facilitation or special protected corridors as the primary mode.
- **Commercial transit levels**: Combined daily vessel transits through Hormuz at ≥80% of the pre-war average (roughly 70–100+ ships/day equivalent, depending on exact baseline of ~100–135 crossings/day).
- **War-risk insurance conditions**: Additional premiums for Hormuz transit generally ≤1% of hull value and widely available from private markets, without primary reliance on special government reinsurance facilities.
- **Oil and LNG export continuity**: Gulf crude and product exports (including Iranian volumes if the blockade ends) at ≥90% of pre-war potential; Qatari LNG flows at ≥70–80% of remaining undamaged capacity (accounting for the ~17% long-term loss from damaged trains at Ras Laffan, with repairs estimated at ~3 years).
- **Energy market stress and volatility**: Normalized freight rates and war premiums; no extreme, disruption-driven spikes in Brent or related markets; low elevated volatility attributable to Gulf risks.
- **Force posture of major actors**: Reduced US carrier presence (toward 1 or fewer in the region); Iranian IRGC Navy not actively interdicting or mining; no US naval blockade of Iranian traffic.
- **Absence of major new escalation triggers**: No large-scale attacks, renewed mine campaigns, blockade enforcement, or political shocks that re-close the strait or re-elevate risks.

A temporary ceasefire, adaptive oil flows via pipelines/shuttle tankers/STS transfers, or lower oil prices alone do not constitute normality. The definition distinguishes military pauses from commercial and insurance normalization.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot**  
As of 17 November 2026 (approximately day 263), the conflict that began with US-Israeli strikes on 28 February 2026 remains unresolved. A June Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding for a 60-day negotiating window lapsed in August without a lasting deal; low-level exchanges continue. Recent days have seen Iranian drone attacks claimed against Bahrain (intercepted), IRGC/Navy claims of tanker mine strikes in/near Hormuz (some unverified; UKMTO confirmed at least one projectile attack), and the arrival of a third US aircraft carrier (USS Theodore Roosevelt) joining two others. Iranian oil exports are effectively zero under US blockade. War-risk premiums remain elevated (reports in the 7–9% range of hull value recently, far above pre-war ~0.25%). Brent trades near $92. Diplomatic mediation continues (Oman, Pakistan, others); Iranian FM Araghchi is in Muscat. Proxies (Houthis) remain active, with reported advances near Bab al-Mandeb. US casualties stand at 23 (Pentagon figures); Iranian casualties are in the thousands. Force postures are elevated; no permanent settlement exists.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant classes:  
- **Tanker War (1984–1988)**: Prolonged maritime attacks during the Iran-Iraq War. Relevant for Gulf shipping disruption and US escort involvement (Earnest Will). Ended with broader ceasefire; shipping recovered thereafter. Suggests multi-year timelines if underlying war persists. Breaks down: different political context, no modern insurance/blockade dynamics or nuclear issues.  
- **2019–2020 Gulf tanker incidents**: Short spikes of attacks on shipping. Rapid de-escalation without sustained closure. Suggests quick recovery possible under limited escalation. Breaks down: far smaller scale than 2026 war.  
- **Red Sea Houthi campaign (post-2023)**: Partial, multi-year disruption with rerouting and elevated insurance, without full normality. Suggests lingering commercial effects. Breaks down: not a full strait closure or major power war.  
- **Post-ceasefire recoveries in chokepoints (historical Suez, limited Tanker War aftermath)**: Weeks to months once hostilities fully cease and mines cleared.  
- **US blockade/sanctions episodes**: Can persist independently of kinetic fighting if political conditions unmet.

Base-rate prior (pre-current-evidence adjustment, weighted toward protracted maritime/Gulf cases):  
1 week ~1%; 1 month ~4%; 3 months ~10%; 6 months ~18%; 1 year ~35%; 18 months ~50%. These reflect that full commercial/insurance normalization typically lags military pauses and requires durable political resolution.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**  
- Diplomatic channels remain open with active mediators (Oman, Pakistan).  
- Adaptive oil export recovery demonstrates commercial resilience under partial security.  
- Economic pressure on Iran and US domestic costs of prolonged deployment.  
- Historical pattern that full hostilities end with negotiated outcomes rather than total victory.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**  
- Unresolved core issues (blockade, sanctions, nuclear inspections, force posture, Iranian conditions).  
- Recent escalation and high US force posture (three carriers).  
- Elevated insurance costs and shipper risk aversion; persistent attacks/threats.  
- Structural LNG damage and force majeure.  
- Proxy activity and mine/threat risks.  
- Iran’s demonstrated capacity to disrupt Hormuz even at reduced levels.  
Ranked by importance: political settlement of war terms > physical security/mine clearance and attack cessation > insurance market confidence > LNG/export logistics recovery.

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Protracted low-intensity conflict with intermittent diplomacy. Adaptive oil flows continue via protected routes and pipelines; full Hormuz commercial normalization and insurance recovery lag a durable political settlement (ending blockade and core disputes). LNG remains constrained by physical damage for years. Probabilities rise gradually as incentives for settlement accumulate.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
A breakthrough mediated deal (Oman/Pakistan channel) within 1–3 months that ends the blockade, provides security guarantees, and enables force drawdown. Insurance markets reprice quickly on verified calm; transits recover within weeks-months of security. Probability of this path is modest given current demands and recent escalation.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Stalemate persists with periodic escalations, proxy activity, and insurance remaining elevated. Oil adapts but remains costly; LNG disruption continues. Hormuz stays restricted for 18+ months. This aligns with Tanker War and Red Sea reference classes if political resolution fails.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Strongest case for faster normalization: Economic pressure and mediation could produce a limited deal sooner than base rates suggest; oil adaptations already show resilience. Strongest case for prolonged instability: Core issues (sanctions, nuclear, blockade) are zero-sum; insurance markets are slow to recover after losses; structural LNG damage and mine risks persist. I may be overestimating 1-year/18-month odds if the conflict freezes into a “neither peace nor war” pattern with ongoing disruption. I may be underestimating short-term odds if a quiet period is misread as progress. Load-bearing assumptions: that a political settlement is achievable within 18 months and that insurance follows security rather than lagging indefinitely.
