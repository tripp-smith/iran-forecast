**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by the horizons of 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months (from ~27 September 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality requires simultaneous satisfaction of these observable, multi-domain criteria (none alone is sufficient; a temporary ceasefire, price dip, or partial escorted transit does not qualify):

- Direct military activity: No sustained strikes by US/coalition forces on Iranian territory/coastal assets or by Iran on US/coalition bases, naval assets, or Gulf Arab energy infrastructure for a sustained period (weeks, not days).
- Proxy activity: Material reduction in Houthi and other Iranian-aligned attacks on shipping, Saudi/UAE energy facilities, or Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb lanes relative to mid-2026 peaks.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Absence of successful attacks, boardings, mine incidents, or turn-back orders on commercial vessels; UKMTO/JMIC risk assessments no longer at critical/elevated for routine commercial traffic.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily commodity-vessel transits through Hormuz sustained at ≥70–80% of pre-war baseline (~60–85 vessels/day range, depending on source) for multiple consecutive weeks, with AIS-visible traffic and no large stranded fleet.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Premiums for Hormuz transit back near pre-war levels (multiples of 1–3× rather than 20–40×+), with major underwriters and P&I clubs offering cover without prohibitive conditions or exclusions.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Persian Gulf crude + condensate + LNG export volumes restored to ≥85–90% of pre-war (~20–23 mb/d oil-equivalent range), with East-West pipeline and other bypasses operating normally and no chronic ship-to-ship transfer dependence.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent/WTI no longer embedding a large persistent Hormuz risk premium; volatility metrics and contango/backwardation consistent with ordinary supply-demand rather than chokepoint crisis.
- Force posture of major actors: No large-scale US naval blockade of Iranian ports still in force; Iranian forces not actively enforcing unilateral transit regimes or fee schemes; Gulf Arab states not under elevated defensive alert tied to Iranian missile/drone threat.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new large-scale kinetic exchanges, nuclear-related escalations, or third-party interventions that re-close the waterway or re-spike insurance/shipping risk.

Normality is commercial and operational, not merely the absence of headline fighting.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant classes (3–6):

1. **1980–88 Tanker War (Iran-Iraq)**: Multi-year attacks on shipping in the Gulf/Hormuz approaches. Traffic never fully stopped but risk remained elevated for years; insurance spiked and partial commercial adaptation occurred within months, full normalization only after ceasefire + years of residual risk. Suggests multi-year functional disruption even after major combat ends. Breaks down because both sides needed oil revenue and neither fully closed the strait.
2. **2019–2020 tanker attacks / 2019 Aramco / Soleimani period**: Discrete spikes followed by rapid (weeks–months) partial recovery of traffic and insurance once kinetic intensity fell, but residual elevated risk and force posture persisted. Suggests 1–6 months for partial commercial recovery after de-escalation. Breaks down because no full-scale war or sustained blockade.
3. **Red Sea / Bab el-Mandeb Houthi campaign (2023–present, ongoing into 2026)**: Sustained proxy attacks produce multi-year depression of traffic, extreme insurance, and rerouting even without direct great-power war. Normalization requires either sustained military suppression or durable political deal; partial recovery can occur in months but full return is slow. Highly relevant for proxy and insurance components.
4. **Suez Canal closures (1956, 1967–75)**: Full closures lasted months to years; commercial recovery after reopening took weeks to months once security was credible. Suggests that once physical and insurance barriers lift, traffic can rebound relatively fast. Breaks down because Hormuz is not a single lock system and Iran retains asymmetric denial options.
5. **2024–25 limited Iran-Israel exchanges and prior Gulf crises**: Short kinetic cycles produce temporary spikes that decay in weeks if not reinforced. Suggests low probability of rapid full normalization without a durable political settlement.

Base-rate prior (pre-current-evidence adjustment, probability of meeting the full operational definition):
- 1 week: 1–3%
- 1 month: 5–10%
- 3 months: 15–25%
- 6 months: 30–40%
- 1 year: 45–55%
- 18 months: 55–65%

These reflect that commercial/insurance normalization systematically lags military pauses, and durable political settlements in the Gulf after major conflict are historically slow.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

- Mutual economic pain (Iranian revenue squeeze, elevated global energy prices, Gulf Arab exposure).
- Existence of mediation channels (Qatar, Oman, Pakistan, UNGA contacts).
- US domestic political calendar (midterms) creating incentive for a pause or deal rather than open-ended attrition.
- Historical pattern that commercial traffic rebounds once credible security + insurance normalize.
- Limited remaining Iranian anti-shipping capacity after repeated US strikes.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

- Core political demands remain far apart (blockade lift, asset release, nuclear issues, “all fronts” ceasefire vs. US insistence on Iranian restraint and non-reconstitution).
- Iranian regime survival incentives and IRGC institutional interest in retaining Hormuz leverage.
- Proxy (Houthi) autonomy and residual capacity.
- Insurance markets and shipowners require multi-week sustained safety, not declarations.
- Risk of post-midterm US kinetic resumption or Iranian response.
- Second-order effects: once rerouting, STS transfers, and elevated premiums are locked in, reversal is sticky.

Ranked importance: political gap and insurance/shipping confidence are the highest barriers; pure military pause is secondary.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued limited kinetic exchanges and diplomatic stalemate through the US midterms, followed by either a constrained pause or renewed limited pressure. Commercial traffic remains suppressed and insurance elevated for at least several more months. Gradual partial recovery of volumes via escorted or high-premium voyages, with full multi-domain normality more likely only after a clearer political equilibrium or exhaustion of both sides’ willingness to sustain costs. Proxy activity decays slowly.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

A post-midterm US decision to prioritize energy-market relief, combined with a mediated package that freezes direct strikes, lifts the blockade in stages, and produces a verifiable multi-week safety record. Insurance markets respond within weeks; traffic rebounds toward 70%+; proxies stand down under Iranian direction. Probability of this path materializing is low but non-zero (~15–20% chance it becomes the dominant trajectory within 6 months).

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Political gap persists; intermittent strikes and vessel incidents continue; Houthi or other proxies keep residual capacity; insurance remains prohibitive; Gulf states hedge further; energy markets permanently reprice a higher Hormuz risk. Functional normality delayed beyond 18 months. This path is consistent with the Tanker War and multi-year Red Sea experience.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Possible over-estimation of longer-horizon probabilities: commercial and insurance normalization may prove stickier than historical analogies if the political conflict remains unresolved and Iran retains denial options. The operational definition is deliberately demanding; markets and shipowners may accept a “new normal” of higher risk that still fails the criteria. Possible under-estimation of near-to-medium term: mutual economic pain and US political incentives could produce a faster pause than base rates imply. Load-bearing assumptions include continued Iranian regime coherence and absence of a sudden, decisive military or political shock.
