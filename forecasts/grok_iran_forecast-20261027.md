**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each of these horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality requires simultaneous, sustained satisfaction of the following observable conditions for at least 30 consecutive days (or clear trajectory toward it with high confidence of durability):

- Direct military activity between primary belligerents (US/Israel and Iran) reduced to near-zero kinetic exchanges; no active enforcement of a naval blockade against non-Iranian commercial traffic.
- Proxy activity (Houthis, Iraqi militias, Hezbollah, and equivalents) limited to residual low-level incidents that do not systematically disrupt shipping or energy infrastructure in the Gulf or approaches.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: threat level downgraded from severe; absence of systematic attacks, mining campaigns, or requirement for mandatory US escorts/AIS-dark operations; commercial vessels able to transit on normal routing with routine (not wartime) risk mitigation.
- Commercial transit levels: AIS-visible commercial transits recover to ≥70–80% of pre-war baseline (~60–70/day average sustained), with total effective throughput (including any residual dark traffic) near pre-war volumes.
- War-risk insurance conditions: additional premiums fall to ≤1–2% of hull value (or <5–10× peacetime rates of ~0.15–0.25%), with broad market availability, no mass cancellations, and Joint War Committee listed-area status normalized or low-premium.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: non-Iranian Gulf crude exports at or above 90% of pre-war volumes via standard routes (not primarily dark shuttles or emergency pipeline diversion); Iranian exports resume under whatever post-conflict sanctions or agreement regime applies; LNG flows without major structural rerouting.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent crude within roughly 20–30% of pre-war levels (~$70–90 range rather than sustained $110+), with realized and implied volatility returned to pre-conflict norms and no ongoing emergency stockpile releases or extreme freight rate spikes.
- Force posture of major actors: US/Coalition naval and air presence reduced from surge/escort levels (no sustained carrier strike group or MEU commitment solely for Hormuz protection); Iranian IRGC naval posture defensive/routine without claims of exclusive control or transit-permit requirements.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: no significant new attacks, nuclear breakthroughs, regime-change operations, or proxy activations that restart kinetic phases.

Temporary ceasefires, partial oil-flow recoveries via protected dark shuttles, or lower prices alone do not meet the definition. Commercial and insurance normalization must lag military pauses and be durable.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant historical cases:

- **1980s Tanker War (Iran-Iraq, ~1984–1988)**: Prolonged attacks on shipping (hundreds of incidents). US escorts (Operation Earnest Will, 1987–88) allowed continued flows at elevated cost and risk. Full normalization followed the August 1988 ceasefire within roughly 3–6 months for commercial traffic and insurance, though residual risk and presence lingered. Relevant for maritime coercion and escort models; breaks down because the 2026 case involves direct great-power strikes on Iran, nuclear issues, and proxies, not just bilateral Iran-Iraq tanker targeting. Suggests partial stabilization 3–9 months post-military pause, full commercial 6–12 months.
- **1990–91 Gulf War / post-Desert Storm shipping**: Rapid military resolution led to quick commercial recovery (weeks to a few months). Less relevant for a protracted, multi-domain war with Iranian capacity to sustain low-level maritime disruption. Suggests faster end-state once major combat ends.
- **Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb Houthi crisis (2023–2025)**: Sustained partial disruption with escorts and Cape rerouting; traffic never fully normalized for 12–18+ months despite no full-scale war. Highly relevant for insurance lag, commercial adaptation, and proxy persistence. Suggests 12–24 months for functional normality even after peak threat declines.
- **2019–2020 Gulf tanker incidents and Abqaiq**: Short-lived spikes resolved in weeks to a couple of months with no sustained war. Less relevant for intensity and duration. Suggests rapid recovery after isolated incidents.
- **Ukraine Black Sea grain corridor and related shipping (2022–present)**: Protected corridors enabled partial recovery; full normality remains incomplete years later due to residual risk. Relevant for protected-transit models. Suggests incomplete normalization beyond 12–18 months under ongoing threat.

Base-rate prior (before case-specific updating), reflecting the demanding operational definition and typical lag between military pause and commercial/insurance normalization: 1 week 2–5%; 1 month 5–10%; 3 months 15–25%; 6 months 30–45%; 1 year 50–65%; 18 months 60–75%. Long-horizon figures remain modest because historical chokepoint wars show persistent risk premia, force postures, and incomplete traffic recovery even after formal pauses.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

Ranked: (1) Economic and political incentives for all sides (Iran needs revenue; Gulf states and Asian buyers need volume; US political costs of prolonged disruption); (2) demonstrated operational adaptation (dark shuttles + pipelines already restoring much oil volume); (3) active regional mediation; (4) fatigue after 8 months and election timing that may favor de-escalation windows; (5) historical pattern that protected corridors eventually allow partial commercial recovery.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

Ranked: (1) Iran’s residual capacity and demonstrated willingness to attack shipping to retain leverage over Hormuz and extract concessions (nuclear, sanctions, “new realities”); (2) post-election risk of renewed major US/Israeli strikes; (3) insurance market conservatism and lag (premiums remain elevated even during quieter periods); (4) proxy persistence (Houthis, possible Iraqi/Hezbollah reactivation); (5) force-posture inertia and mutual distrust preventing rapid drawdown or full reopening; (6) absence of a comprehensive political settlement addressing nuclear and Strait control issues.

Insurance, safe unrestricted transit, and force posture are typically the slowest; oil volume can recover faster via workarounds.

**10. Most Likely Path**

A post-election period of heightened risk (possible limited strikes or intensified maritime pressure) followed by a fragile freeze or limited arrangement by early-to-mid 2027. Oil volumes continue via adapted routes at elevated cost; AIS-visible traffic recovers slowly; insurance premiums decline only gradually. Full multi-domain normality remains incomplete at 12–18 months, with residual risk premia, selective escorts or dark operations, and proxy activity. Probability mass concentrates on partial, managed recovery rather than clean return to pre-war conditions.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Strongest case: Rapid post-election diplomacy produces a durable reopening formula; Iran prioritizes revenue and accepts constrained Hormuz status; US draws down surge forces; premiums collapse within 60–90 days of verified quiet; AIS traffic rebounds above 70%. This path is possible if economic pain and regional mediation outweigh leverage calculations. It would push 6-month probability above 55% and 1-year above 75%.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Strongest case: Post-election strikes restart a high-intensity phase; Iran sustains or escalates maritime attacks and proxy activation; no political settlement on nuclear or Strait issues; insurance remains punitive; traffic stays depressed with permanent workarounds. Reference-class Red Sea and Tanker War tails support this. It would keep 6-month probability below 20% and 18-month below 45%.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

The forecast may overestimate normalization speed by underweighting Iran’s demonstrated ability to impose costs at low intensity and the conservatism of insurance markets (which lagged even the June MOU). It may underestimate normalization if oil-volume adaptation already signals that commercial actors will accept elevated risk faster than the definition assumes, or if election outcomes create a strong de-escalation window. Load-bearing assumptions are that residual IRGC maritime capability persists and that insurance/force posture lag military pauses by months. Evidence of a verified multi-month attack-free period with rising AIS traffic would force an upward revision; a new major strike campaign would force a downward one.
