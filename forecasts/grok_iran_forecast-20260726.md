**1. Forecast Question**  
Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each horizon: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
Functional normality requires simultaneous satisfaction of all of the following observable conditions for a sustained period (not a single-day snapshot):

- Direct military activity: No sustained reciprocal strikes by US/Iran forces on each other’s territory, forces, or critical infrastructure in the Gulf region. Isolated residual incidents do not disqualify if frequency is near pre-war baseline.
- Proxy activity: No significant Houthi, Iraqi militia, or Hezbollah attacks on shipping, energy infrastructure, or Gulf-state targets linked to the conflict.
- Shipping security (Strait of Hormuz and approaches): No confirmed kinetic attacks on commercial vessels for ≥14 consecutive days; UKMTO/JMIC threat level at or below “moderate”; no active Iranian requirement for mandatory IRGC coordination or tolls that deters non-Iranian traffic.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits ≥80% of pre-war baseline (~110+ of the historical ~130–138 average), sustained over ≥7 days, with balanced inbound/outbound and significant non-Iranian/non-sanctioned tonnage.
- War-risk insurance: Additional war-risk premiums for Hormuz transit at or near pre-war levels (or ≤2–3× pre-war if still elevated by residual risk); major underwriters willing to quote freely without special exclusions.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf exporters (Saudi, UAE, Kuwait, Qatar, Iraq) and residual Iranian flows operating at ≥85% of pre-war effective capacity through Hormuz or viable alternatives, without systemic diversion or prolonged loading halts.
- Energy market stress: Brent volatility and risk premium consistent with ordinary geopolitical risk (not the elevated levels seen during active blockade/strike cycles); no persistent multi-week backwardation driven primarily by Hormuz risk.
- Force posture: Major actors (US 5th Fleet, Iranian coastal forces, Gulf states) have drawn down from peak wartime alert postures; no large-scale new deployments signaling imminent major escalation.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No fresh closure declarations, large-scale mining, mass casualty strikes on Gulf infrastructure, or formal reimposition of total blockades that reverse prior gains.

A temporary ceasefire, lower oil prices alone, or a battlefield pause without commercial recovery does **not** meet the standard.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant historical cases (Gulf/chokepoint/maritime disruption/regional wars):

1. **Iran–Iraq War tanker war phase (1980s)**: Multi-year attacks on shipping; traffic adapted but never fully “normal” under sustained threat; full commercial confidence required years after ceasefire. Timeline to partial stabilization: 1–3+ years post-major fighting. Breaks down because today’s precision strike capabilities and global insurance markets react faster, but political will to keep the strait contested is similar.
2. **2019–2021 Iran tanker attacks / tanker seizures / mine incidents**: Episodic rather than continuous; traffic recovered in weeks to months once kinetic intensity fell, but residual risk premiums and caution persisted. Timeline to functional normality: 3–9 months after last major incident cluster. Breaks down because 2026 involves sustained US–Iran reciprocal campaigns and explicit control contest.
3. **Houthi Red Sea campaign (2023–2025+)**: Significant diversion of traffic; insurance spikes; partial recovery only after sustained multinational pressure and Houthi restraint. Full commercial normalization incomplete even after years. Timeline: 6–18+ months for high-confidence flows. Highly relevant for proxy/insurance dynamics.
4. **1990–91 Gulf War / post-liberation Kuwait**: Rapid military victory produced relatively fast commercial recovery (months) once fighting ended and mines were cleared. Breaks down: decisive conventional victory vs. ongoing asymmetric contest with Iran.
5. **Strait of Hormuz crisis episodes (e.g., 2011–12 threats, 2019)**: Markets and shipping priced high risk quickly but recovered in weeks–months when threats remained rhetorical or limited. Less relevant once kinetic threshold is crossed repeatedly.
6. **Broader post-conflict chokepoint recoveries (Suez after 1956/1967/1973, temporary closures)**: Political settlement + physical clearance often required 3–12 months for full commercial confidence.

**Base-rate prior (before case-specific update)** for functional normality under the strict multi-domain definition above, for a major Gulf kinetic contest involving Iran:
- 1 week: ~1–3%
- 1 month: ~5–10%
- 3 months: ~15–25%
- 6 months: ~30–45%
- 1 year: ~50–65%
- 18 months: ~60–75%

These reflect that commercial/insurance normalization lags military pauses, and Iran has repeatedly treated Hormuz control as core leverage.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Mutual exhaustion / political decision by US and Iran leadership that continued kinetic contest is too costly relative to gains (most important).  
2. Durable, verifiable understanding on Hormuz transit rules that both sides accept (no unilateral Iranian control + no permanent US blockade).  
3. Sustained absence of ship attacks + successful multinational escort/monitoring that rebuilds insurer and master confidence.  
4. Proxy restraint (especially Houthis) and Gulf-state political stability.  
5. Physical clearance of residual threats and gradual force posture de-escalation.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Iran’s demonstrated willingness to use force and administrative claims to retain Hormuz leverage as core strategic asset.  
2. US determination not to accept Iranian control or tolls, backed by repeated strikes.  
3. Second-order proxy activation (Houthis) that expands the problem set beyond Hormuz.  
4. Insurance markets and commercial operators requiring long “clean” periods before restoring volume and pricing.  
5. Domestic political incentives on both sides that reward toughness over rapid compromise.  
6. Risk of new triggers (mass-casualty incident, infrastructure hit on major Gulf exporter, nuclear-related escalation).

**8. Decomposed Sub-Forecasts** (approximate probabilities of each component being sufficiently satisfied)

- Major reduction in direct US–Iran strikes: 1w <5%; 1m 15%; 3m 40%; 6m 55%; 1y 70%; 18m 75%.  
- Sustained safe commercial transit (no attacks + volume recovery): 1w <2%; 1m 8%; 3m 25%; 6m 40%; 1y 55%; 18m 65%.  
- Meaningful insurance normalization: lags transit recovery by weeks–months; lower than transit numbers above.  
- Oil/LNG export recovery to near-pre-war effective levels: similar to or slightly higher than transit (alternative routes help somewhat).  
- Proxy restraint: 1w low; rises more slowly than direct strikes due to Houthi autonomy and Iranian leverage.  
- Force drawdown + no major new trigger: correlated with strike reduction but slower.

Gating factors are (a) end of reciprocal kinetic campaign and (b) commercial/insurance confidence, which historically lag military pauses.

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Continued low-to-medium intensity reciprocal strikes and ship interdiction for weeks to a few months, with traffic remaining heavily suppressed. Eventual political exhaustion or external mediation produces a new pause or limited understanding. Commercial recovery then lags by weeks to months as insurers and operators demand clean periods. Proxy activity (Houthis) adds friction. Functional normality, if achieved, most likely clusters in the 6–18 month window rather than earlier.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Rapid mutual recognition that further kinetic exchanges yield diminishing returns; renewed high-level understanding with verifiable Hormuz rules, multinational monitoring, and proxy de-activation. Traffic rebounds within weeks under escort; insurance normalizes within 1–2 months. Probability of this path is low given current positions but not zero.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Iran successfully maintains de-facto leverage through intermittent attacks and administrative claims; US continues periodic strikes and blockade measures; Houthis expand Red Sea pressure; Gulf states face repeated infrastructure or shipping incidents. Commercial operators and insurers treat the region as semi-permanent high-risk. Functional normality delayed beyond 18 months.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Possible overestimation of longer-horizon probabilities: Iran’s strategic culture treats Hormuz as non-negotiable leverage; US domestic and alliance politics may prevent acceptance of any Iranian administrative role. Insurance markets can remain elevated for years (Red Sea precedent). Possible underestimation of short/medium horizons: markets and operators adapted surprisingly quickly in some past Gulf episodes once kinetic intensity dropped, and alternative export routes + strategic stocks can reduce pressure for immediate full Hormuz normality. Load-bearing assumptions include continued US willingness to strike and Iranian capacity/willingness to contest despite losses.
