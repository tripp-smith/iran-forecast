**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each of these horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

“Functional normality” requires simultaneous, sustained (not fleeting) recovery across domains. It is not a temporary military pause, lower oil prices alone, or partial commercial recovery under wartime conditions. Observable criteria:

- Direct military activity: Near-zero exchanges of fire between US/Israel and Iran for a sustained multi-week period (no large-scale strikes, missile/drone campaigns, or naval engagements).
- Proxy activity: Houthi and other Iranian-aligned forces restrained to low/pre-war levels; no sustained attacks on Gulf energy infrastructure, ports, airports, or shipping beyond the immediate Yemen theater.
- Shipping security (Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters): Attack rate on commercial vessels drops to near-zero (<1–2 significant incidents per month) for a sustained period. No active mine threat or enforced “authorization” regime by Iran that deters traffic.
- Commercial transit levels: Consistent ≥80–90% of pre-war volumes for oil, LNG, products, and containers through Hormuz (and overall Gulf export continuity via all routes).
- War-risk insurance: Premiums normalize to near-pre-war or modestly elevated levels (roughly ≤2–3× baseline, not the current 8–30× range that is prohibitive for most operators). Cover widely available without extreme restrictions.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf producers (Saudi, UAE, Iraq, Kuwait, Qatar, and ideally Iran) sustain near-normal export rates without major wartime disruption or reliance on temporary workarounds (dark AIS shuttles, forced STS, etc.).
- Energy market stress and volatility: War-risk premium largely removed; prices and volatility driven primarily by fundamentals rather than acute chokepoint fear.
- Force posture of major actors: US (and partner) naval/air surge posture drawn down from wartime levels; no permanent high-alert blockade or escort regime required for routine traffic.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No major new attacks on energy infrastructure, nuclear-related escalations, full attempted closure of Hormuz, or expansion that resets commercial confidence.

A temporary ceasefire or partial oil-flow rebound under high insurance and US facilitation does not qualify.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot**

As of 17 October 2026 (roughly Day 230–231 of the conflict that began ~28 February 2026):

- Direct US strikes on Iran are paused until after the 3 November midterms (per Trump statements), while the US naval blockade of Iranian ports continues. Two US carriers are operating in the Arabian Sea. Talks continue but remain stalled; the June MoU expired and hostilities resumed.
- Iranian (IRGC) attacks on commercial shipping in/near Hormuz are at or near the highest tempo of the war (dozens of incidents in recent weeks; ships sunk; claims of mines and projectiles; UKMTO and other reports confirm high attack rates). Iran asserts control and “authorization” requirements; US/CENTCOM contests this and facilitates transits.
- Oil flows through Hormuz are highly volatile: Kpler and other data show 7-day averages often 30–40%+ below pre-war (~17 mb/d) baselines for the strait itself (with daily swings from very low to higher), though total Middle East exports (including bypass pipelines and Gulf of Oman STS) have recovered more substantially at times. Brent crude is in the ~$105–110 range (pre-war ~$70–75).
- War-risk insurance remains extremely elevated (reports of 8–30× peacetime rates; percentages of hull value in the multi-percent range per voyage or period; cover expensive and capacity-constrained). Many operators avoid or minimize exposure.
- Proxies: Houthis have escalated attacks on Saudi airports, infrastructure, and related targets; Yemen front active.
- Force posture remains elevated; commercial normality is not present.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant classes:

- **1980s Tanker War (Iran-Iraq, ~1984–1988)**: Multi-year campaign of attacks on tankers; US Operation Earnest Will escorts; shipping continued with disruption but never fully halted; oil flows reduced but not collapsed long-term. Ended with political ceasefire. Suggests multi-year timelines if conflict persists; commercial continuity possible under escorts but full normality requires war end. Breakdown: Scale of 2026 disruption (insurance collapse, oil price impact, mine claims) is larger relative to global flows.
- **Red Sea/Houthi campaign (2023–present, overlapping)**: Sustained multi-year disruption; large rerouting; insurance remained elevated long after partial pauses; full commercial recovery lagged. Suggests 6–18+ months lag even after attack frequency drops. Breakdown: Hormuz is a single critical chokepoint with fewer easy alternatives.
- **1991 Gulf War**: Short, intense conflict; rapid post-ceasefire shipping and energy recovery (weeks to a few months). Suggests fast recovery once decisive military/political end occurs. Breakdown: Current conflict is more protracted and hybrid.
- **Shorter Hormuz incidents (2019 tanker attacks, 2012 threats, etc.)**: Brief spikes in risk; insurance and traffic recovered in weeks to a few months once attacks stopped. Suggests rapid commercial response if kinetic activity truly ceases.
- **Protracted proxy/maritime hybrid cases (e.g., various Yemen or Red Sea phases)**: Insurance and operator behavior normalize more slowly than pure military pauses.

**Base-rate prior** (before current-case adjustment; rough probabilities of meeting the full operational definition by horizon):

- 1 week: ~5%
- 1 month: ~15%
- 3 months: ~30%
- 6 months: ~45%
- 1 year: ~60%
- 18 months: ~70%

These reflect that full multi-domain commercial/insurance/force normality typically lags pure military pauses by months, and that chokepoint wars often persist or leave residual risk.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

1. US political incentive to de-escalate or freeze post-midterms (or claim progress).
2. Iranian economic pressure from blockade (limited oil export revenue).
3. Existence of workarounds (pipelines, STS, dark transit facilitation) that have already enabled partial flows.
4. Mutual interest in avoiding uncontrolled escalation or total Hormuz closure.
5. Historical pattern that commercial actors respond once sustained attack rates drop.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

1. Iranian incentive and demonstrated ability to keep pressure on shipping/oil prices (especially pre- and post-US elections).
2. Extremely elevated insurance and operator risk aversion (lags kinetic pauses by months).
3. Active proxy front (Houthis) expanding risk surface.
4. Failed prior ceasefire attempt and deep disagreements over Hormuz control, sanctions, nuclear issues.
5. Residual mine/threat perceptions and force postures that sustain risk premium.
6. Volatility of flows even under facilitation; second-order effects on refineries, Asian buyers, etc.

**8. Decomposed Sub-Forecasts**

Illustrative probabilities of key components by horizon (these are not independent; insurance and transit are tightly linked to attack rates):

- Major reduction in direct strikes: High by 1–3 months if pause holds post-midterms (~60–80%), but fragile.
- Sustained safe commercial transit (≥80–90% volumes, low attack rate): Low near-term (10–20% at 1 month); rises to 40–60% by 6–12 months only if attacks truly cease.
- Meaningful insurance normalization (≤2–3×): Lags transit; 5–15% at 1–3 months; 30–50% by 6–12 months.
- Oil/LNG export recovery to near-normal: Partial already via workarounds; full consistent recovery 30–50% by 3–6 months if security improves.
- Proxy restraint: Lower (Houthi activity has independent drivers); 20–40% sustained by 6 months.
- Force drawdown: Slow (30–50% by 6–12 months).
- No new major escalation trigger: Medium (50–70% across horizons, but downside skew).

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued contested maritime environment through the US midterms and immediate aftermath, with volatile oil flows under US facilitation, high insurance, and intermittent Iranian attacks. Partial commercial recovery possible by 3–6 months if kinetic intensity drops, but full multi-domain normality (especially insurance and force posture) more likely in the 6–18 month window absent a decisive political settlement. Proxy activity (Houthis) remains a drag.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Rapid post-midterm deal or mutual freeze that produces multi-week zero-attack period. Insurers reprice within weeks; operators return; flows stabilize above 80–90%. US draws down escorts. Probabilities rise sharply (e.g., 40%+ at 1 month, 70%+ at 6 months). Requires Iranian economic pressure to dominate and US willingness to trade on sanctions/Hormuz issues.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Post-midterm US strikes or Iranian maritime campaign intensifies to keep prices high. Insurance stays extreme; operators minimize exposure; flows remain volatile and dependent on facilitation/bypasses. Houthi activity expands. Normality delayed beyond 18 months (probabilities fall to 20–40% at 1 year). Load-bearing assumption is Iranian ability and will to sustain low-cost disruption.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

I may be underestimating near-term normalization if US facilitation and bypasses already constitute a “new normal” that markets accept, or if a quiet deal emerges quickly. I may be overestimating longer-term recovery if Iranian maritime capabilities prove more resilient than expected or if residual mine/threat perceptions and insurance hysteresis prove stronger than historical cases. The operational definition is deliberately strict; looser commercial metrics would show higher probabilities earlier. Evidence on exact current insurance multiples and precise flow volumes remains somewhat noisy.
