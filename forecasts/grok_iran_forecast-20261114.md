**Forecast Question**  
Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each horizon: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
Functional normality requires *all* of the following to hold simultaneously and be sustained for at least 30 consecutive days (not a single data point or temporary pause):

- Direct military activity: No significant kinetic exchanges (strikes, drone/missile launches, or engagements causing damage or casualties) between US/Israeli forces and Iranian forces. Rare isolated incidents are tolerable only if they do not alter risk perceptions.
- Proxy activity: Houthi and other Iran-linked proxy attacks on Gulf shipping, ports, pipelines, or energy infrastructure reduced to pre-February 2026 baseline (sporadic and non-systematic, not a sustained campaign).
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Unrestricted commercial transit without Iranian permits, fees, route designations, or de facto denial. No active mine threats, projectile/drone campaigns, or requirement for military escorts/special routing for routine commercial vessels.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits (AIS-visible plus reliable dark-fleet estimates) at ≥80% of the pre-war average (roughly 100–130+ vessels per day depending on definition; tanker + cargo baseline around 70–80).
- War-risk insurance conditions: Additional war-risk premiums ≤1% of hull value (near the pre-war 0.15–0.25% range). Joint War Committee (JWC) listed-area status substantially reduced or lifted. Capacity available without extreme gating.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Oil flows *through* Hormuz ≥90% of pre-war levels (~15–17 mbd range) without heavy structural dependence on bypass pipelines or ship-to-ship transfers outside normal patterns. LNG flows from Qatar/UAE at normal volumes and costs.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent risk premium attributable to Gulf disruption ≤$5–10/bbl on a sustained basis; no prolonged elevated freight or insurance costs distorting flows.
- Force posture of major actors: Material drawdown from wartime levels for US naval/air forces in the region, Iranian IRGC Navy posture, and Gulf state heightened readiness. End of active US blockade of Iranian ports and Iranian effective control claims over the strait.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new attacks on critical energy infrastructure, nuclear-related escalations, or direct involvement of additional Gulf states in combat.

A temporary ceasefire, a drop in oil prices alone, partial oil-flow recovery via workarounds, or a battlefield pause does *not* constitute normality. The definition is multi-domain and commercial-first because shipping behavior, insurer pricing, and exporter confidence lag military events and are the binding constraints for energy markets.

**Current Situation Snapshot** (as of ~14 November 2026, ~Day 260)  
The war began 28 February 2026 with US-Israeli strikes (including the killing of Ali Khamenei). Iran retaliated against Israel and Gulf states. A June 2026 Islamabad MOU for 60-day talks and partial reopening collapsed around 8 July; limited exchanges and a US naval blockade of Iranian ports resumed. Major combat has largely subsided, but tit-for-tat continues (recent US strikes on sites such as Sirik/Qeshm; IRGC claims of strikes on Bahrain radar and multiple tanker attacks, some disputed or unverified by independent sources). Trump has described major renewed offensive as “highly unlikely” pending a strong agreement and noted the US “won,” while also seeking “total victory”; US midterms (early November) produced Democratic gains in Congress. Iran maintains leverage via Hormuz restrictions and lists conditions (sanctions relief, blockade lift, asset release, etc.).

Shipping: Strait “effectively closed” to normal traffic. Daily transits remain far below pre-war (recent Windward readings ~10 in 24 hours or low double-digits averages; some trackers show higher US-facilitated or weekly figures still ~65% below normal). Many vessels run dark. Oil flows *through* Hormuz have recovered to roughly 11–12 mbd range in recent weeks/months (IEA and Kpler-linked figures; ~65–82% of pre-war), with total Gulf exports (including pipelines) higher (around 15–18 mbd or 74–82% of pre-war). Significant reliance on Saudi East-West pipeline, UAE routes, and ship-to-ship transfers. Attacks continue (IMO cumulative incidents ~130–136 with ~25 seafarer deaths; multiple November tanker hits by projectile/mine claims). War-risk premiums remain extremely elevated (~6–10% of hull value; recent figures around 9%). Brent trades ~$95–96 (pre-war ~$72; war peak ~$126). Houthis continue attacks on Saudi targets. No formal settlement; force postures remain elevated. Evidence quality is mixed: commercial trackers (Kpler, Windward, Lloyd’s List, IEA) are higher-credibility for flows/transits than belligerent claims.

**Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
- **1980s Tanker War (Iran-Iraq, 1984–88)**: Sustained attacks on shipping for years; traffic continued under escorts but with high risk. Full normalization only after 1988 ceasefire. Relevant for prolonged maritime coercion by Iran. Breaks down: no full-scale US/Israeli invasion or leadership decapitation; Iraq was the primary aggressor early. Suggests multi-year disruption possible without settlement; partial commercial adaptation in 6–18 months.
- **1990–91 Gulf War**: Decisive US intervention; Hormuz never closed. Rapid post-ceasefire recovery of shipping and exports. Relevant for major-power involvement and oil-market impact. Breaks down: different geography, no Iranian control of the strait, shorter high-intensity phase. Suggests 1–6 months to functional commercial recovery if hostilities end cleanly.
- **Houthi Red Sea/Bab al-Mandeb campaign (2023–2025)**: Prolonged insurance elevation, traffic diversion (Suez collapse), and slow recovery even after claimed ceasefires because risk perception and JWC listings lagged. Relevant for asymmetric maritime disruption and insurer caution. Breaks down: Hormuz has fewer easy bypasses for volume. Suggests 6–18+ months for insurance and traffic normalization.
- **2019–2020 Iran-linked Gulf tanker attacks**: Short spikes followed by relatively quick de-escalation and traffic recovery once political pressure eased. Relevant for limited kinetic maritime actions. Breaks down: scale of 2026 conflict is larger. Suggests weeks to a few months for limited incidents.
- **Other chokepoint/post-conflict recoveries**: Rare full closures; recovery typically tracks political settlement more than pure military pause.

Base-rate prior (outside-in, before case-specific evidence): Probability of full functional normality is low in the near term because commercial and insurance lags are structural. Rough prior: 1 week ~1–2%; 1 month ~5%; 3 months ~15%; 6 months ~30%; 1 year ~50%; 18 months ~65%. These reflect historical patterns where military pauses precede commercial normality by months, and unresolved political claims sustain risk premiums.

**Key Drivers Toward Normalization**  
1. Oil-flow adaptation (pipelines, selective US-facilitated transits, larger tankers) already at substantial recovery—reduces immediate economic pressure.  
2. Political fatigue and US midterm results may constrain major new US offensives.  
3. High freight rates and selective willingness to accept risk allow some volume.  
4. Historical pattern that pure military pauses eventually allow commercial creep if attacks drop.

**Key Drivers Against Normalization**  
1. Persistent attacks and mine/projectile risk keep insurers and shipowners gated (highest-ranked).  
2. Unresolved Iranian conditions and dual blockades sustain effective denial.  
3. Insurance pricing inertia and JWC listing (risk perception lags incidents).  
4. Proxy activity and elevated force postures create escalation pathways.  
5. Gap between oil-volume recovery and normal traffic/insurance conditions.

**Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon**  
Synthesis treats insurance normalization + unrestricted safe transit as the primary gating factors. Oil-flow recovery alone is insufficient (already partially achieved via workarounds). Probabilities are not simple averages; they reflect the joint requirement and the lag of commercial/insurance domains behind military pauses. Long-horizon figures stay below 80% because historical maritime cases show residual risk premiums and political unresolved issues can persist.

**1 Week**  
- Probability: 1%  
- Base-rate anchor: Near-zero for full multi-domain recovery in days.  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slightly upward from pure zero because some oil flows exist, but attacks and premiums remain elevated.  
- Core rationale: Ongoing incidents, high premiums, low transits, and no settlement make simultaneous satisfaction of all criteria impossible.  
- Main gating factor: Insurance and shipping security.  
- What would raise this forecast materially: Immediate verified ceasefire on shipping + premium collapse (highly improbable).  
- What would lower this forecast materially: Major new attack wave or escalation.  
- Confidence in this estimate: High  

**1 Month**

- Core rationale: One month is too short for insurance markets to reprice or shipowners to treat the strait as normal; force postures and Iranian conditions remain.  
- Main gating factor: War-risk insurance and transit security.  
- What would raise this forecast materially: Formal reopening agreement with verified mine clearance and attack cessation.  
- What would lower this forecast materially: Renewed major strikes or mine-seeding campaign.  
- Confidence in this estimate: High

**Most Likely Path**  
Partial oil-flow recovery continues via a mix of selective Hormuz transits (often dark or facilitated), pipelines, and ship-to-ship transfers. Direct major combat stays limited. Attacks on shipping occur at reduced but non-zero frequency. Insurance remains elevated (several percent). Full functional normality is delayed until a political arrangement addresses Iranian conditions and produces sustained incident-free transit. Most probable window for crossing the definition threshold is 9–18 months, contingent on no major escalation.

**Faster Normalization Case**  
A verified deal within 1–3 months that ends attacks, lifts blockades, clears mines, and produces JWC relief could push 6-month probability above 50% and 1-year above 70%. Triggers: rapid diplomatic breakthrough, Iranian domestic pressure, or US political decision to prioritize energy stability.

**Prolonged Instability Case**  
Low-level maritime campaign continues indefinitely (as in parts of the Tanker War), insurance stays gated, and traffic normalizes only partially. Probability of full normality by 18 months could fall below 40%. Triggers: renewed major strikes, successful Iranian mine-seeding, or collapse of any talks.

**Red-Team Critique**  
Strongest case that I overestimate normalization: Iran has clear incentives and demonstrated capability to keep Hormuz as leverage; insurance markets are slow to reprice even after incidents drop (Red Sea precedent); current attack tempo shows resilience. Strongest case that I underestimate it: Oil-flow adaptation is already advanced, major-combat phase has subsided, and economic costs on all sides create pressure for de facto arrangements faster than formal deals. Load-bearing assumptions: that commercial/insurance lags dominate, that Iranian conditions are not easily met, and that no sudden decisive military or political shock occurs. Evidence that would prove the view wrong: sustained multi-week period of zero incidents + premium collapse + transit recovery above 80%, or conversely a major escalation that freezes recovery.
