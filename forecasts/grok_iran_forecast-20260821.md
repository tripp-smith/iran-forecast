**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by the horizons of 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months (from 21 August 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

“Return to functional normality” requires simultaneous satisfaction of all the following observable conditions for a sustained period (not a single day or temporary pause):

- Direct military activity: No US/Israeli/Iranian kinetic strikes on military, nuclear, energy, or shipping-related targets inside Iran, Gulf states, or the maritime domain for ≥14 consecutive days; no major new force deployments or alerts indicating imminent large-scale operations.
- Proxy activity: Houthis, Iraqi militias, Hezbollah, and other Iran-aligned groups show no sustained attacks on commercial shipping, energy infrastructure, or US/partner forces beyond pre-war baseline levels.
- Shipping security (Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters): Zero confirmed projectile/mine/boarding incidents against commercial vessels for ≥21 consecutive days; UKMTO/JMIA risk assessments at “moderate” or lower; no active minefields or enforced Iranian “controlled routes” with fees or mandatory coordination.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits through Hormuz ≥80% of pre-war average (~110–130+ ships/day, including VLCCs and LNG carriers), sustained for ≥14 days, with AIS data and Kpler/equivalent trackers confirming normal routing (not exclusively dark or Iranian-coast hugging under coercion).
- War-risk insurance: Hull war-risk premiums ≤2–3% (pre-war range or modestly elevated), with major insurers and P&I clubs offering coverage without prohibitive exclusions or surcharges that effectively price out most operators.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf producers (Saudi, UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Iraq, Iran if relevant) exporting at ≥90% of pre-war volumes via Hormuz or confirmed alternative routes without material diversion or stockpile buildup; no widespread port/refinery shutdowns due to security.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent crude sustained below $80 (or within 10% of contemporaneous non-Gulf-driven fundamentals) without Hormuz-risk premium dominating; implied volatility and crack spreads not indicating acute supply-fear regime.
- Force posture of major actors: US CENTCOM and partner naval/air assets reduced from wartime surge levels toward pre-February 2026 posture; Iranian IRGC Navy and coastal missile/drone forces not in heightened alert or actively interdicting traffic.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No leadership assassinations, nuclear breakout signals, large-scale proxy offensives, or formal declarations that reopen the conflict cycle.

A temporary ceasefire, partial traffic recovery under escort, lower oil prices from demand destruction, or battlefield pause alone does **not** meet the definition. All domains must be concurrently functional and commercially viable without continuous military escort or coercion.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant reference classes (selected for chokepoint disruption + regional great-power/proxy war elements):

1. **Tanker War phase of Iran-Iraq War (1984–1988)**: Prolonged attacks on shipping in Gulf/Hormuz. Traffic never fully normalized until ceasefire; insurance remained elevated; functional commercial normality took 1–3 years post-ceasefire with residual risk. Relevant for asymmetric maritime interdiction. Breaks down: no superpower direct combatant role at this intensity; oil market structure different.
2. **1990–1991 Gulf War (Iraq invasion of Kuwait + Desert Storm)**: Rapid coalition victory, but shipping/insurance normalization lagged 6–18 months due to mines, residual threats, and political uncertainty. Relevant for rapid kinetic resolution followed by slow commercial recovery. Breaks down: clear territorial aggressor defeat; no enduring Iranian-style regime survival with residual missile/proxy capacity.
3. **Houthi Red Sea campaign (2023–ongoing as of 2026 baseline)**: Persistent low-level attacks forced major rerouting, insurance spikes, and incomplete recovery even after months of US/UK strikes. Normalization still incomplete after 2+ years. Relevant for proxy persistence and insurance stickiness. Breaks down: secondary chokepoint; less direct state-on-state intensity.
4. **Suez Canal closures (1956, 1967–1975)**: Political/military closures lasted months to years; full commercial normality required political settlement + demining/clearance. Relevant for chokepoint political control disputes. Breaks down: narrower waterway, different actors.
5. **Post-2003 Iraq + residual Gulf maritime risk periods**: Insurance and transit recovered over 12–24 months after major combat, with persistent elevated premiums during instability spikes.
6. **2024–2025 limited Iran-Israel exchanges + prior Hormuz threats**: Short spikes resolved in weeks, but never reached full multi-domain war intensity.

Base-rate prior (probability of meeting the full multi-domain definition, before case-specific updates):

- 1 week: 1–3%
- 1 month: 5–10%
- 3 months: 15–25%
- 6 months: 30–40%
- 1 year: 45–55%
- 18 months: 55–65%

These reflect that pure military pauses occur faster than insurance, transit volume, and force-posture normalization. Historical chokepoint recoveries under residual hostility typically require 6–24 months for commercial functionality.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization** (ranked)

1. Mutual economic exhaustion and US domestic political pressure for lower energy prices (highest weight).
2. Sustained pause in direct strikes creating space for third-party mediation (Oman/Qatar).
3. Iranian recognition that maximalist Hormuz-control demands are unsustainable under prolonged blockade + secondary sanctions.
4. Gulf Arab states’ preference for commercial recovery over prolonged confrontation.
5. Residual US military superiority enabling credible deterrence without continuous kinetic action.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization** (ranked)

1. Iranian regime survival imperative and insistence on formal recognition of Hormuz authority/control (highest weight; existential for residual power projection).
2. Stickiness of war-risk insurance and shipper risk aversion (once elevated, slow to reprice even after kinetic pause).
3. US political commitment to “maximum pressure” and refusal of Iranian core demands (blockade lift, sanctions relief, compensation).
4. Proxy autonomy and residual Iranian missile/drone stocks enabling low-cost disruption.
5. Absence of a comprehensive political settlement addressing nuclear, regional, and maritime issues simultaneously.
6. Cumulative damage, leadership changes inside Iran, and mutual distrust after multiple collapsed understandings.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Protracted “no war, no peace.” Intermittent low-level Iranian interdiction or proxy actions keep insurance elevated and volumes suppressed. Occasional tactical pauses and limited technical arrangements (Oman channel) produce partial traffic recovery under high risk premiums. Full multi-domain normality remains elusive until a broader political package addresses Iranian residual capabilities, sanctions, and maritime regime. Energy markets adapt via alternative supply and demand destruction; Gulf states invest in redundancy. Probability mass concentrates on partial recovery by 6–12 months without crossing the strict operational threshold until closer to 18 months.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Decisive Iranian economic collapse or leadership pragmatism leads to acceptance of free navigation + limited sanctions relief within 1–3 months. Rapid mine clearance, insurer confidence restoration, and US force drawdown follow. Requires Iranian abandonment of maximalist Hormuz-control claims—low but non-zero probability under extreme pressure.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Iran successfully adapts to sanctions, retains sufficient missile/drone/proxy capacity for intermittent disruption, and extracts de-facto recognition of influence over Hormuz. US maintains blockade and secondary sanctions indefinitely. Insurance and shipper behavior lock in elevated risk pricing for 18+ months. Nuclear ambiguity or proxy expansion provides recurring triggers.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Possible overestimation of normalization: The operational definition is deliberately strict; historical insurance and commercial confidence recoveries after multi-month closures with residual hostility are slower than kinetic pauses. Iranian regime survival incentives and US political commitment to maximum pressure may lock the system into managed conflict longer than attrition models suggest. Possible underestimation: Markets and shippers are adaptive; dark fleet + alternative routes + eventual fatigue could produce functional (if imperfect) normality faster than pure political analysis implies. Load-bearing assumption at risk: that Iranian maximalist demands are non-negotiable under prolonged economic isolation.
