**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by the horizons of 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months (from 17 September 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

“Return to functional normality” requires simultaneous satisfaction of all of the following observable conditions for a sustained period (not a single-day snapshot or temporary pause):

- Direct military activity: No sustained US–Iran or coalition–Iran kinetic exchanges (strikes on military targets, coastal infrastructure, or vessels) at a rate exceeding sporadic, isolated incidents that are contained without reciprocal escalation.
- Proxy activity: Material restraint by Iran-aligned proxies (Houthis, Iraqi militias, Hezbollah remnants) such that attacks on Gulf shipping, energy infrastructure, or partner states do not produce new closures or major insurance spikes.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Free, unrestricted commercial transit without requirement for Iranian permits/tolls, mandatory IRGC coordination, or exclusive reliance on Iranian-controlled northern routes; no active mining, systematic drone/missile attacks on merchant vessels, or effective exclusion zones that force dark transits or escorts as the dominant mode.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily two-way vessel transits averaging ≥70–80% of pre-war baseline (~85 vessels/day per IMF PortWatch/Kpler-type trackers) for at least 14 consecutive days, with a representative mix of tankers, bulk, and container vessels from diverse flags (not dominated by Iranian/Chinese/shadow-fleet traffic).
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums for Hormuz transit at ≤2–3× pre-war baseline (pre-war ~0.1–0.25% of hull value) and readily available from major underwriters without mass cancellations or capacity rationing; cargo war-risk cover similarly normalized.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf producers (Saudi, UAE, Kuwait, Iraq, Qatar) exporting at ≥85–90% of pre-war volumes via Hormuz or viable bypasses without sustained force majeure; Iranian exports no longer subject to active US naval blockade that prevents meaningful loadings from reaching buyers.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent crude no longer reflecting a persistent Hormuz risk premium that keeps prices structurally elevated and volatile solely due to chokepoint insecurity (distinct from broader macro factors); no repeated spikes triggered by single transit incidents.
- Force posture of major actors: Material drawdown or stabilization of elevated US/coalition naval and air presence in the Gulf/Hormuz approaches to levels consistent with routine presence rather than active blockade/escort operations; Iranian coastal forces not in continuous high-alert interdiction posture.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new leadership assassinations, large-scale attacks on energy terminals/pipelines, formal re-declaration of closure, or introduction of new toll/permit regimes that reset commercial confidence.

A temporary ceasefire, partial traffic recovery under escort, lower oil prices from demand destruction, or battlefield pause alone does **not** meet the definition. Commercial and insurance normalization are gating factors; military de-escalation is necessary but insufficient.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant historical reference classes (3–6):

1. **Iran–Iraq Tanker War (1984–1988)**: Prolonged anti-shipping campaign in Gulf/Hormuz approaches with Western naval escorts (Operation Earnest Will). Relevant because of Iranian asymmetric tactics, insurance spikes, and chokepoint focus. Breaks down because it was bilateral Iran–Iraq with less direct great-power kinetic involvement and no full formal “closure.” Suggests multi-year partial disruption with gradual commercial recovery only after major political settlement (years, not months).

2. **2019 Gulf shipping crisis (tanker attacks, seizures, limited mining threats)**: Short, intense insurance and transit disruption after attacks attributed to Iran. Relevant for insurance pricing dynamics and rapid commercial caution. Breaks down because no full-scale war or sustained blockade. Suggests 3–6 months for meaningful insurance/traffic recovery once kinetic incidents stop.

3. **Red Sea/Houthi disruption (late 2023–ongoing into 2026 context)**: Proxy attacks on shipping leading to major rerouting, insurance hikes, and partial recovery only after sustained restraint + naval presence. Relevant for proxy persistence and commercial lag behind military pauses. Breaks down because alternative routes (Cape) exist and volume is lower than Hormuz energy flows. Suggests 6–18 months for functional normalization even after de-escalation signals.

4. **1990–91 Gulf War / post-Kuwait invasion energy recovery**: Major supply disruption followed by relatively rapid physical recovery once combat ended, but insurance and commercial confidence lagged. Relevant for scale of energy impact. Breaks down because clear battlefield victory and no sustained Iranian control claims over the strait.

5. **Post-ceasefire commercial reopening lags in other chokepoints (Suez 1956/1973 analogs, limited modern data)**: Political agreements often precede insurance and operator confidence by weeks to many months.

Base-rate prior (pre-current-evidence adjustment, averaged across classes weighted toward prolonged asymmetric Gulf cases): Probability of functional normality (per definition) by horizon roughly 1 week <5%; 1 month 8–12%; 3 months 20–30%; 6 months 35–45%; 1 year 50–60%; 18 months 60–70%. These reflect that military pauses occur faster than insurance/commercial normalization, and full multi-domain recovery historically lags political announcements by months to years.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

Ranked by importance:
1. Durable political settlement removing Iranian toll/permit claims and US blockade.
2. Sustained absence of kinetic attacks on shipping for weeks, allowing insurance capacity and premiums to ease.
3. Midterm political pressure on US side + Iranian economic pain from blocked exports creating mutual incentive for off-ramp.
4. Successful demining + protected corridor becoming routine enough for major operators to return.
5. Proxy restraint (especially Houthis) to prevent secondary chokepoint shocks.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

Ranked by importance:
1. Iranian strategic insistence on permanent management/control of Hormuz as leverage (repeated official statements).
2. Insurance market stickiness: premiums rise fast, fall slowly; single incident resets confidence.
3. Proxy autonomy and spillover (Houthis, others) independent of central Iranian restraint.
4. Mutual escalation dynamics and depleted inventories making “mowing the lawn” the default.
5. Commercial operator caution and vessel repositioning lags even after political signals.
6. Domestic Iranian hardliner influence that previously undermined the June MOU.

**9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon**

- Probability: 3%

- Core rationale: No pathway exists for multi-domain normalization in days; backlog, insurance, and confidence cannot clear.  
- Main gating factor: Insurance and operator willingness.  
- What would raise this forecast materially: Immediate comprehensive ceasefire + verified zero attacks + major underwriter announcements of rate cuts.  
- What would lower this forecast materially: Further successful attacks on shipping or bases.  
- Confidence in this estimate: High  

**1 Month**  
- Probability: 8%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~10%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slightly down (fragility of prior MOU, ongoing kinetic cycle, proxy activity)  
- Core rationale: Even an optimistic diplomatic breakthrough would face weeks of demining, vessel repositioning, and insurance lag. Historical commercial recovery trails politics.  
- Main gating factor: Sustained safe transit without incidents.  
- What would raise: Verified durable deal removing toll claims + 2+ weeks zero shipping attacks + premium cuts.  
- What would lower: Renewed large-scale exchanges or Houthi escalation closing secondary routes.  
- Confidence: Medium-High

- Main gating factor: Insurance capacity return + operator confidence.

- What would lower: Hardliner sabotage of any deal or major energy infrastructure strike.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued low-to-medium intensity exchanges and constrained traffic through the US midterms. Post-election political incentives produce another negotiated pause or limited corridor arrangement in late 2026/early 2027. Traffic recovers partially under escort or monitored routes over subsequent months, but insurance remains elevated and full unrestricted commercial normality is delayed into mid-to-late 2027. Iranian control claims are fudged rather than fully abandoned. Proxy activity continues at lower intensity. Functional normality per the strict definition is more likely after 12 months than before, but remains incomplete.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Decisive post-midterm deal that explicitly guarantees free navigation without Iranian permits/tolls, verified demining, rapid underwriter capacity return, and credible proxy restraint. Traffic recovers to 60%+ within 6–8 weeks of the deal and insurance falls toward 2–3× baseline. Probability of this path is low (~15–20% conditional on a deal occurring) because of demonstrated fragility of the June MOU and Iranian strategic view of the strait.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Iran treats Hormuz management as non-negotiable core interest; hardliners block meaningful concessions; proxies keep secondary pressure; insurance markets stay risk-averse after repeated incidents; US maintains blockade and periodic strikes (“mow the lawn”). Traffic remains <30% for an extended period. This path is consistent with Tanker War duration and current Iranian statements. Probability elevated relative to base rate because of the control doctrine.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Strongest case I may be overestimating normalization: Insurance stickiness and commercial caution are under-weighted; a political deal does not automatically restore operator confidence (Red Sea precedent). Iranian domestic politics may lock in the control claim more firmly than economic pain suggests.  
Strongest case I may be underestimating: Mutual exhaustion + midterm pressure + Chinese interest in stable energy flows could produce a faster practical corridor arrangement than pure reference classes imply. Official claims of higher protected flows, if sustained and verified, would raise shorter-horizon probabilities.  
Load-bearing assumptions: That commercial/insurance metrics are true gating factors (they are); that Iranian control claims are sticky (supported by repeated statements); that no catastrophic escalation or sudden regime change intervenes (base case).
