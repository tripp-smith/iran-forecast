**1. Forecast Question**  
Probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months (as of 11 August 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
All of the following must hold simultaneously for a sustained period (not a single-day snapshot), measured against pre-28 February 2026 baselines where applicable:

- Direct military activity: No sustained US/Israeli-Iran kinetic exchanges (strikes on territory, bases, or shipping); no major Iranian missile/drone campaigns against Gulf states or US forces. Isolated incidents do not disqualify if contained and non-escalatory.
- Proxy activity: Material restraint by Houthis, Iraqi militias, and Hezbollah (no sustained attacks on Saudi/UAE energy infrastructure, Red Sea shipping, or US/partner forces that threaten Gulf stability).
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Commercial vessels transit without routine IRGC interdiction, boarding, disabling, or attack risk; US blockade enforcement no longer the primary enabler of limited movement; AIS-visible traffic dominant and corridors stable (not dark/routed under coercion).
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits through Hormuz recover to ≥70–80% of pre-war average (~100–110+ of ~130–140 vessels/day), sustained for weeks, with mixed commercial (not predominantly shadow-fleet or state-directed) participation.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums fall to ≤1–1.5% of hull value (from current elevated multiples of 7–30× peacetime ~0.25%) and remain available without widespread withdrawals or voyage-by-voyage special underwriting; cargo cover normalizes sufficiently for routine chartering.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf producers (Saudi, UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Iraq) restore the large majority of pre-war export volumes via Hormuz or viable alternatives without force majeure dominance; Iranian exports (if any) occur under transparent, non-blockade conditions; Kharg and other terminals operational without chronic waiting queues of dark tankers.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent and related benchmarks stabilize without persistent war-premium spikes driven by Hormuz risk; forward curves and Asian/European buyer behavior reflect restored confidence rather than chronic contingency planning.
- Force posture of major actors: No large-scale additional US/Coalition deployments or Iranian reconstitution that signals imminent major escalation; postures consistent with deterrence rather than active campaigning.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No successful major attacks on energy infrastructure, mining campaigns, or political shocks (succession crises, nuclear breakout claims, etc.) that re-close the strait or restart high-intensity fighting.

A temporary ceasefire, partial traffic rebound under military escort, or oil-price decline alone does **not** qualify. Battlefield pauses or “no war, no peace” stalemates with Iranian control claims and elevated insurance are not normality.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant classes (with breakdowns):

- 1980s Tanker War (Iran-Iraq): Prolonged asymmetric attacks on shipping; traffic never fully normalized during active phase; insurance spiked and stayed elevated; full commercial recovery lagged ceasefire by months–years. Suggests multi-month to multi-year horizons for functional normality after high-intensity disruption.
- 2019–2020 Gulf tanker attacks / IRGC seizures + Soleimani aftermath: Limited duration spikes; traffic and insurance recovered in weeks to a few months once kinetic intensity dropped and deterrence stabilized. Breaks down because scale was far smaller and no prolonged blockade or major war.
- Red Sea/Houthi disruption (2023–2025): Traffic collapse, insurance spike, partial recovery under escorts and diplomacy; residual elevated risk and rerouting persisted for many months. Relevant for insurance and commercial caution; breaks down because Hormuz is a higher-volume energy chokepoint with direct great-power involvement.
- Post-ceasefire Gulf reopenings or mine-clearance episodes (historical and 1990s/2000s analogs): Physical reopening often faster than commercial/insurance confidence; 4–12+ weeks common for traffic rebound, longer for full premium normalization.
- Broader modern great-power or major regional wars with maritime dimensions (limited analogs): Commercial normalization frequently lags military pauses by 3–12+ months due to insurer risk aversion, charterer caution, and residual proxy risk.

Base-rate prior (pre-current-evidence adjustment, for functional normality as defined):  
1 week: <5%; 1 month: 10–15%; 3 months: 25–35%; 6 months: 40–55%; 1 year: 55–70%; 18 months: 65–80%. These reflect that military pauses are more common than rapid, multi-domain commercial normalization after major chokepoint wars.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**  
(Ranked roughly by importance)  
1. Mutual economic/political costs (Iranian inflation/export losses; US/Gulf energy and inflation pressures; Trump desire for “victory” narrative).  
2. Existence of a technical Iran-Oman route framework that could be activated if political conditions are met.  
3. Degraded Iranian military capacity from sustained strikes (reduces ability to sustain high-tempo disruption indefinitely).  
4. Mediation channels (Oman, Qatar, others) and residual interest in avoiding full-scale return to February–June intensity.  
5. Commercial backlog and charterer/insurer incentives to resume once kinetic risk falls.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**  
(Ranked)  
1. Core dispute over Hormuz control/sovereignty claims vs. free navigation; Iran treats the strait as primary remaining leverage.  
2. Conditionality mismatch (Iran demands compensation/end-blockade/sanctions relief; US maintains pressure and adds its own demands).  
3. Residual kinetic and proxy capacity; insurance and charterer caution requires sustained low-risk evidence, not just announcements.  
4. Domestic political constraints on both sides (Iranian hardliners/IRGC; US midterms/political incentives).  
5. Second-order effects: once traffic and premiums are disrupted at this scale, recovery is sticky; “no war, no peace” can persist.

**8. Decomposed Sub-Forecasts** (approximate probabilities of each major component holding by horizon; these are inputs, not simple averages)

- Major reduction in direct strikes: 1w ~15–25%; 1m ~40%; 3m ~55–65%; 6m ~70%; 1y+ higher.  
- Sustained safe commercial transit (≥70–80% volume, low interdiction): 1w <5%; 1m ~10–15%; 3m ~25–35%; 6m ~45–55%; longer higher but gated.  
- Meaningful insurance normalization (≤1–1.5% range, available): lags traffic; 1w/1m very low; 3m ~15–25%; 6m ~35–45%; 1y ~55–65%.  
- Oil/LNG export recovery (majority of Gulf volumes): tracks transit closely but slightly lags.  
- Proxy restraint: moderate probability of tactical pauses but low probability of durable deactivation in near term.  
- Force drawdown / no major new triggers: mixed; drawdown slower than pause.

Gating factors are commercial transit + insurance confidence + absence of major kinetic/proxy spikes. Military pause alone is insufficient.

**9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon**

**1 Week**  
• Probability: 3%  
• Base-rate anchor: <5%  
• Current-evidence adjustment: Slight downward (traffic standstill, active blockade, conditions mismatch).  
• Core rationale: No plausible path to multi-domain recovery in days; even a sudden deal announcement would not immediately normalize traffic, insurance, or confidence.  
• Main gating factor: Physical and commercial restart lag.  
• What would raise this forecast materially: Verified full ceasefire + immediate unrestricted transit surge + insurance rate collapse.  
• What would lower this forecast materially: Fresh major kinetic exchange or vessel attacks.  
• Confidence in this estimate: High  

**1 Month**  
• Probability: 12%  
• Base-rate anchor: 10–15%  
• Current-evidence adjustment: Neutral to slight downward.  
• Core rationale: Technical route deal possible, but political conditionality and commercial lag make full functional normality unlikely; partial traffic rebound more plausible than the full definition.  
• Main gating factor: Bridging Iran-US conditionality gap.  
• What would raise: Signed, implemented deal with rapid traffic and premium response.  
• What would lower: Breakdown of Oman talks or renewed major strikes.  
• Confidence: Medium-High  

**3 Months**  
• Probability: 28%  
• Base-rate anchor: 25–35%  
• Current-evidence adjustment: Slight downward (sticky insurance, residual proxy risk, control dispute).  
• Core rationale: Highest-probability window for a durable pause or limited arrangement given economic pressures, but full multi-domain normality (especially insurance and sustained commercial volumes without residual risk premium) remains challenging.  
• Main gating factor: Sustained low-kinetic environment long enough for insurers/charterers to re-price.  
• What would raise: Verifiable multi-week safe transit at scale + premium compression + proxy restraint.  
• What would lower: Recurrent vessel attacks or infrastructure strikes.  
• Confidence: Medium  

**6 Months**  
• Probability: 45%  
• Base-rate anchor: 40–55%  
• Current-evidence adjustment: Neutral.  
• Core rationale: Economic attrition and political incentives favor some form of stabilization; reference classes show commercial recovery often materializes in this range after pauses, though residual elevation in risk metrics is common.  
• Main gating factor: Whether any arrangement produces durable commercial confidence rather than fragile “no war, no peace.”  
• What would raise: Clear free-navigation outcome or effective multilateral management with insurance market buy-in.  
• What would lower: Entrenched Iranian control claims + intermittent disruption.  
• Confidence: Medium  

**1 Year**  
• Probability: 62%  
• Base-rate anchor: 55–70%  
• Current-evidence adjustment: Neutral to slight downward (structural control dispute and insurer memory).  
• Core rationale: Base rates and cost pressures support eventual functional recovery for most domains, but full insurance/commercial normalization can lag military outcomes; long-horizon uncertainty remains material.  
• Main gating factor: Resolution or durable management of Hormuz governance question.  
• What would raise: Formal agreement + demonstrated multi-month stability.  
• What would lower: Prolonged low-intensity contest or major proxy escalation.  
• Confidence: Medium  

**18 Months**  
• Probability: 72%  
• Base-rate anchor: 65–80%  
• Current-evidence adjustment: Neutral.  
• Core rationale: Over this horizon, attrition, alternative supply adaptations, and political cycles favor functional normality under the definition for most planners, though residual risk elevation relative to 2025 is possible. Overconfidence risk is real if the control dispute hardens.  
• Main gating factor: Same as 1-year, plus cumulative economic and political exhaustion.  
• What would raise: Clear post-conflict security architecture.  
• What would lower: Frozen conflict with periodic Hormuz crises.  
• Confidence: Medium-Low (long-horizon inherent uncertainty)

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Continued “no war, no peace” for weeks to a few months: intermittent diplomacy (Oman channel primary), low-to-moderate kinetic activity, suppressed traffic, elevated insurance, and oil prices carrying a risk premium. Eventual limited arrangement or de facto understanding allows staged traffic recovery; full multi-domain normality (especially insurance confidence and absence of residual control claims) arrives later, most probably in the 6–18 month window if major new triggers are avoided.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Rapid convergence on a deal (Iran prioritizes economic relief; US prioritizes price/political relief), immediate unrestricted transit, rapid premium compression, and proxy restraint. Would require bridging the compensation/control gap quickly. Probability mass shifts upward especially at 1–6 months.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Hardened Iranian insistence on control/fees + US refusal + recurrent vessel or infrastructure attacks + proxy activation. Traffic and insurance remain disrupted or volatile; “functional normality” delayed beyond 18 months or achieved only in attenuated form. Strongest when leadership politics on either side reward intransigence.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Possible over-estimation of normalization speed: Commercial and insurance recovery after major chokepoint disruptions is historically stickier than military pauses; the sovereignty/control dispute is deeper than many temporary Gulf incidents. Possible under-estimation: Mutual economic pain and Trump’s demonstrated willingness to declare victories could produce faster political deals than base rates suggest. Load-bearing assumptions include continued Iranian residual disruption capacity and insurer risk aversion. The definition is deliberately demanding; softer definitions would yield higher probabilities.
