**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each of these horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

“Return to functional normality” requires simultaneous satisfaction of all of the following observable, multi-domain conditions for a sustained period (not a single-day snapshot):

- Direct military activity: No significant US, Israeli, or Iranian strikes on each other’s territory, forces, or commercial shipping in the Gulf/Hormuz area for at least 14 consecutive days; no active major combat operations.
- Proxy activity: No sustained or escalating proxy attacks (Houthis, Iraqi militias, Hezbollah-linked actions affecting Gulf shipping or energy infrastructure) that materially disrupt commerce.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Commercial vessels transit freely without kinetic attacks, effective mine threats, or forced compliance with exclusive Iranian routing/toll regimes; US/partner and Iranian forces not actively interdicting or attacking commercial traffic.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits through Hormuz average ≥80% of pre-war baseline (~100–120+ per day, depending on exact pre-war reference of ~120–140), sustained over at least two weeks, with meaningful participation by major Western, Asian, and independent operators (not limited to shadow fleet or highly risk-tolerant flagged vessels).
- War-risk insurance conditions: Additional war-risk premiums for Hormuz transit fall to ≤1% of hull value (from pre-war ~0.25% and wartime peaks of 3–10%+), with broad market availability and no widespread voyage suspensions by major underwriters or carriers.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Combined Gulf oil/LNG flows via Hormuz recover to ≥70–80% of pre-war volumes on a sustained basis (pre-war Hormuz handled ~20% of global oil and a large share of LNG, especially Qatari); major producers (Saudi, UAE, Qatar, Iraq, Kuwait) loading and exporting without large stranded inventories or prolonged force majeure.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Oil and LNG price volatility attributable to Hormuz risk normalizes (no sustained war premium of the magnitude seen in 2026 spikes); market pricing reflects routine commercial risk rather than acute closure or attack risk.
- Force posture of major actors: US/CENTCOM and Iranian forces shift from high-tempo interdiction/strike posture to routine presence; no large-scale additional deployments triggered by ongoing Hormuz contestation; Gulf state basing and air-defense postures de-escalate from wartime highs.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new major incidents (large-scale mining, mass vessel seizures, strikes on critical energy infrastructure, or nuclear-related escalations) that reset the security environment.

A temporary ceasefire, partial traffic recovery under contested Iranian-administered routes, lower oil prices from demand destruction or other factors, or a battlefield pause do **not** constitute normality. Commercial and insurance confidence must recover alongside military de-escalation.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant reference classes (3–6):

1. **Tanker War / Iran-Iraq War (1980–1988)**: Prolonged attacks on shipping in the Gulf. Relevant for Iranian willingness to contest maritime space and slow commercial recovery even after intensity drops. Breaks down because full-scale interstate war with superpower involvement and modern precision weapons differ; no single chokepoint closure of Hormuz intensity. Suggested timeline: partial commercial stabilization often took 1–3+ years after peak intensity; functional normality lagged military pauses.

2. **2019 Gulf tanker attacks / Iranian seizures and mining threats**: Short, calibrated Iranian actions against shipping. Relevant for rapid insurance/pricing spikes and shipping caution. Breaks down due to limited duration and no full war. Suggested: Insurance and traffic normalized in weeks to a few months once kinetic activity ceased and diplomacy advanced.

3. **Houthi Red Sea campaign (2023–ongoing, episodic)**: Proxy disruption of a major chokepoint with sustained elevated insurance and rerouting. Relevant for insurance stickiness and commercial risk aversion even after intensity declines. Breaks down (Red Sea not Hormuz; different actor capabilities). Suggested: Full commercial normality can take 12–24+ months; partial recovery faster under escort regimes.

4. **Post-ceasefire Gulf maritime recoveries (e.g., after 1980s peaks, limited 21st-century episodes)**: Military pauses rarely produced immediate commercial/insurance normalization. Suggested base: 3–12 months for meaningful traffic recovery; 6–18+ months for insurance and full confidence under residual Iranian leverage.

5. **Other major chokepoint disruptions (Suez 1956/1967/2021, limited Black Sea wartime)**: Show that even after physical reopening, insurance, routing confidence, and volumes lag; political contestation prolongs effects. Breaks down on scale of energy dependence and state actor involvement.

**Base-rate prior (before current-case adjustment)**: Very low short-term probabilities of full multi-domain normality. Rough anchors: 1 week ~1–5%; 1 month ~5–15%; 3 months ~15–30%; 6 months ~30–50%; 1 year ~45–65%; 18 months ~55–75%. These reflect historical lag between kinetic pauses and commercial/insurance recovery in Gulf/chokepoint settings, plus Iranian strategic incentives to retain leverage.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

- Mutual exhaustion and economic pain (Iranian economy severely damaged; global energy costs; US domestic/political costs).
- Ongoing mediated talks with reported progress on practical Hormuz transit mechanisms.
- Gulf states’ strong interest in restored flows and opposition to exclusive Iranian control.
- Precedent of temporary partial reopenings showing technical feasibility of higher traffic under security arrangements.
- Potential for sequenced deal: interim free transit + mine clearance → broader nuclear/sanctions package.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

- Iranian strategic determination to secure lasting recognized control/leverage over Hormuz as primary war achievement.
- Fragile, repeatedly violated ceasefires/MoUs; low trust.
- Commercial and insurance stickiness: risk aversion persists long after kinetic pauses.
- Proxy and residual military capabilities enabling low-cost disruption.
- Force posture and munitions strain creating incentives for continued pressure or miscalculation.
- Damage to LNG infrastructure and production restart lags.
- Second-order effects: buyer caution in Asia/Europe, rerouting habits, political domestic constraints in Iran and US.

Ranked importance: Iranian Hormuz control demand and commercial/insurance lag are the highest-weight barriers; exhaustion and talks are the strongest positive drivers.

**8. Decomposed Sub-Forecasts**

Approximate probabilities of key sub-components holding (by horizon; rough, interdependent):

- Major reduction in direct strikes: 1w ~20%; 1m ~35%; 3m ~50%; 6m ~60%; 1y ~70%; 18m ~75%.
- Sustained safe commercial transit (≥80% volumes): 1w ~5%; 1m ~15%; 3m ~30%; 6m ~45%; 1y ~55%; 18m ~65%.
- Meaningful insurance normalization (≤1% hull): 1w ~2%; 1m ~10%; 3m ~25%; 6m ~40%; 1y ~55%; 18m ~65%.
- Oil/LNG export recovery (≥70–80%): Similar lag to transit; slightly slower for LNG.
- Proxy restraint: Higher than direct strikes short-term but volatile.
- Force drawdown / posture normalization: Lags military pause by months.
- No new major escalation trigger: Declining risk over time but never zero.

Gating factors are safe transit + insurance + export continuity; military pause is necessary but insufficient.

**9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon**

**1 Week**

- Current-evidence adjustment: Downward (traffic near collapse, elevated insurance, active contestation, talks incomplete).  
- Core rationale: Multi-domain normality impossible in days; current low traffic, high risk premiums, and unresolved control dispute preclude it.  
- Main gating factor: Immediate sustained safe transit and insurance drop.  
- What would raise this forecast materially: Sudden finalized binding agreement + immediate free transit + insurance collapse.  
- What would lower this forecast materially: New major strikes or vessel attacks.  
- Confidence in this estimate: High.

- Main gating factor: Durable Hormuz transit regime accepted by commercial/insurance markets.  
- What would raise: Signed interim mechanism + 2+ weeks clean transit + premium collapse.  
- What would lower: Renewed kinetic cycle or Iranian enforcement of exclusive control.  
- Confidence: Medium-High.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Protracted low-to-medium intensity contestation over Hormuz control, with intermittent talks producing temporary partial reopenings that fail to restore full commercial/insurance confidence. Gradual improvement in traffic and some risk reduction over 6–18 months driven by mutual costs, but functional normality delayed by Iranian leverage demands and market caution. Partial recovery (higher but sub-normal volumes under managed risk) more likely than full multi-domain normality in the nearer term.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Rapid finalization of a practical, enforceable interim Hormuz mechanism (free transit, coordinated but non-exclusive lanes, mine clearance) accepted by US, Gulf states, and markets; sustained clean transit for weeks; sharp insurance drop; broader nuclear/sanctions progress reducing overall tensions. Probability of this path rising if talks conclude successfully in coming days/weeks without new kinetic incidents.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Iran successfully embeds a recognized or de-facto control/toll regime; repeated kinetic tests; sticky high insurance; persistent low traffic; proxy actions; force posture remains elevated. This path is supported by Iranian statements prioritizing Hormuz control and the pattern of prior deal breakdowns.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Strongest case for overestimating normalization: Underweighting Iranian domestic political incentives and the strategic value of Hormuz control as a post-war deterrent/asset; underestimating commercial risk aversion and insurance market memory; treating talks as more decisive than they have proven.  

Strongest case for underestimating: Overweighting current low traffic as structural rather than reversible with a credible security deal; underweighting economic pain on all sides and Gulf state pressure for restoration.  

Load-bearing assumptions: Iranian prioritization of control will continue to constrain deals; commercial/insurance recovery will lag military pauses by months. Evidence that would prove the view wrong: sustained high-volume free transit + premium collapse under a durable arrangement, or conversely, formalization of exclusive Iranian administration with market acceptance.

**14. Critical Signals to Monitor**

- Daily/ weekly Hormuz transit volumes and composition (flags, cargo types, corridors used).  
- War-risk premium quotes and underwriter voyage advice.  
- Concrete text and implementation of any Hormuz interim arrangement (lanes, fees, enforcement, mine clearance progress).  
- Kinetic incident frequency and targeting (vessels vs military sites).  
- Official statements from Iran on sovereignty/control vs practical transit facilitation.  
- LNG loading rates at Ras Laffan and other Gulf terminals; stranded cargo values.  
- US/CENTCOM and Iranian force posture statements and visible deployments.  
- Major carrier and charterer decisions on resuming regular Hormuz calls.
