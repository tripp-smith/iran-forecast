**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each horizon: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality requires simultaneous satisfaction of all of the following observable conditions for a sustained period (not a single day or temporary pause):

- Direct military activity: No sustained US/Israel–Iran exchange of strikes on military or dual-use targets in Iran or on US/allied bases and infrastructure in the Gulf; no major Iranian ballistic-missile or large-scale drone campaigns against Gulf states.
- Proxy activity: No sustained Houthi, Iraqi militia, or other Iranian-aligned proxy attacks on commercial shipping, Gulf energy infrastructure, or US/allied forces at levels that materially disrupt operations.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: No successful attacks on commercial vessels; no active minelaying or credible threats that force widespread AIS blackouts or route diversions; traffic separation schemes operate without unilateral Iranian enforcement of alternative corridors or tolls.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits through Hormuz recover to ≥70–80% of pre-war average (~100–110+ vessels/day, including tankers and LNG carriers) on a sustained multi-week basis, not a temporary backlog release.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums return to ≤0.5–1% of vessel value (versus pre-war ~0.1–0.25% and current multi-percent levels); cargo war-risk cover is readily available at non-prohibitive rates; underwriters do not list the entire Persian Gulf as an excluded or extreme high-risk zone.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf crude flows through Hormuz recover to ≥15–18 mb/d range (pre-war ~17–21 mb/d context) with no force-majeure declarations by major exporters (Saudi, UAE, Kuwait, Qatar, Iraq); Qatar LNG loadings and sailings resume at ≥70–80% of pre-war capacity without repeated safety pauses.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent risk premium attributable to Hormuz/Gulf conflict compresses such that prices and implied volatility no longer embed a large, persistent geopolitical disruption premium; no repeated multi-dollar daily spikes driven solely by Hormuz incident reports.
- Force posture of major actors: No major additional US carrier/strike-group surge or permanent expansion of offensive posture beyond pre-escalation levels; Iranian coastal and anti-shipping forces not in continuous high-readiness interdiction mode; Gulf states not under continuous air-defense alert with civilian infrastructure repeatedly targeted.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No successful major attack on critical energy infrastructure (desalination, power, upstream facilities), no mass-casualty US personnel incident that forces domestic political escalation, and no formal Iranian declaration of permanent control/toll regime that is actively enforced.

A temporary ceasefire, partial traffic rebound, or oil-price dip alone does **not** meet the definition. Commercial and insurance normalization lag military pauses and are the binding constraints.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot (as of 25 July 2026)**

War began ~28 February 2026 with US–Israeli strikes. A 17 June MOU established a 60-day negotiation window and attempted Hormuz reopening; it collapsed by early July. As of late July:

- US CENTCOM conducting consecutive nightly strikes (11th–13th+ nights reported) on Iranian maritime, missile/drone storage, coastal radar, air-defense, and command targets.
- Iran conducting missile/drone attacks on US bases and infrastructure in Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, Jordan, and occasional Saudi targets; claims of hits on desalination/power plants and military facilities.
- Shipping: Daily Hormuz transits collapsed from brief post-MOU peaks (~40–70) to single digits–low teens (e.g., 1 tanker on 23 July per Kpler; averages ~13 post-escalation). Pre-war ~130–138 vessels/day. Many vessels using Iranian unilateral route; Omani route suppressed. Houthi naval blockade declared against Saudi shipping in Red Sea/Bab al-Mandeb with confirmed attacks on Saudi-flagged tankers.
- Insurance: Hull war-risk premiums elevated at 3–10% of hull value (pre-war ~0.25%); cargo rates sticky. Cover available but costly; JWC listings expanded.
- Energy: Brent recently $90–100+ range with spikes; Gulf crude flows well below pre-war; Qatar LNG heavily constrained with repeated pauses; Iranian exports partially recovered via alternative means during brief MOU window but remain disrupted.
- Casualties and posture: Dozens of US personnel killed/wounded since resumption; Iranian casualties in thousands cumulative. US force posture elevated; Iran using proxies and horizontal escalation. Negotiations stalled; Iran rejected recent ceasefire proposal over Hormuz control. Gulf states largely prefer de-escalation but some reports of limited reciprocal actions.

Evidence base is strong on military activity and shipping counts from CENTCOM, ISW, Kpler, MarineTraffic, UKMTO, and major outlets; weaker or lagging on exact insurance quotes and precise LNG loading rates day-to-day.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant classes (3–6):

1. **Tanker War (Iran–Iraq, 1984–88)**: Sustained attacks on shipping in Gulf/Hormuz approaches. Traffic never fully stopped but was heavily disrupted; insurance spiked; convoys and reflagging used. Functional commercial normalization only after ceasefire and war end (1988). Timeline for partial recovery: months after major de-escalation; full commercial/insurance normality: 1–2+ years. Breaks down: prolonged multi-year attrition war vs. current higher-intensity but shorter so far; no nuclear/Israeli dimension then.
2. **2019 Gulf crises (tanker attacks, Abqaiq, drone/missile incidents)**: Short, sharp disruptions. Shipping risk premium rose then fell within weeks–months once attacks stopped and deterrence signaled. Insurance normalized relatively quickly. Timeline: weeks to low months for commercial rebound if no sustained campaign. Breaks down: no full-scale US–Iran war, no mass strikes on Iranian territory, no multi-month Hormuz effective closure.
3. **Red Sea/Houthi campaign (2023–ongoing, peak 2023–25)**: Persistent low-level attacks on shipping. Traffic partially diverted; insurance elevated for extended periods; partial adaptation via routing and naval presence. Full normality still incomplete years later. Timeline: years for full insurance and volume recovery under ongoing threat. Breaks down: different geography; Houthis weaker than Iranian state capability; no direct great-power strikes on mainland.
4. **Post-1991 Gulf War / post-2003 Iraq War maritime recovery**: After major combat, shipping and energy flows recovered in months once kinetic activity ceased and mines cleared, but residual risk and force posture lingered. Timeline: 3–12 months for commercial metrics. Breaks down: clear conventional victory and occupation dynamics absent here.
5. **Other chokepoint disruptions (Suez 1956/1967/2021, temporary mine threats)**: Short closures recover in days–weeks once physical obstruction ends; political/insurance lag longer if conflict persists. Breaks down: physical vs. kinetic/threat-based disruption.

**Base-rate prior** (before case-specific updating; probability of meeting the full multi-domain definition):

- 1 week: 1–3%
- 1 month: 5–10%
- 3 months: 15–25%
- 6 months: 30–40%
- 1 year: 45–55%
- 18 months: 55–65%

These reflect historical lag between kinetic pause and insurance/commercial recovery, plus high base rate of prolonged residual risk in Gulf maritime conflicts.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

Ranked: (1) Mutual exhaustion + domestic political costs forcing a durable deal that explicitly settles Hormuz freedom-of-navigation without Iranian unilateral control; (2) Effective degradation of Iranian anti-shipping and missile capabilities reducing attack frequency; (3) Insurance market capacity return once kinetic activity stops for sustained weeks; (4) Gulf state mediation pressure and economic self-interest; (5) Backlog release and commercial incentives once risk premium falls.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

Ranked: (1) Core dispute over Hormuz control/tolls/sovereignty remains unresolved and load-bearing for both sides; (2) Proxy activation (Houthis, militias) allows low-cost continued disruption; (3) Insurance and shipping psychology lag kinetic pauses by months; (4) Horizontal escalation (Gulf infra, Red Sea) raises thresholds for “normal”; (5) Domestic politics in US/Iran can reward continued pressure; (6) Residual mines, degraded infrastructure, and force posture keep risk elevated.

**8. Decomposed Sub-Forecasts**

Approximate probabilities that each major component is satisfied by horizon (these are not independent):

- Major reduction in direct strikes: 1w ~5%; 1m ~15–20%; 3m ~40%; 6m ~55–65%; 1y ~70%; 18m ~75–80%.
- Sustained safe commercial transit (≥70–80% volume, no successful attacks): 1w <5%; 1m ~10%; 3m ~25%; 6m ~40%; 1y ~55%; 18m ~65%.
- Meaningful insurance normalization (≤1% hull war-risk, available capacity): 1w <5%; 1m ~10%; 3m ~20–25%; 6m ~35–45%; 1y ~55%; 18m ~65%.
- Oil/LNG export recovery to near-pre-war continuity: 1w <5%; 1m ~10–15%; 3m ~30%; 6m ~45%; 1y ~60%; 18m ~70%.
- Proxy restraint: 1w ~10%; 1m ~20%; 3m ~35%; 6m ~50%; 1y ~60%; 18m ~70%.
- Force posture de-escalation + no new major trigger: similar lag to direct-strike reduction, slightly slower.

Gating factors are insurance + sustained safe transit + absence of new triggers. Military pause is necessary but far from sufficient.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued tit-for-tat at varying intensity for weeks to low months, intermittent mediation attempts, partial traffic adaptation via Iranian route or naval escorts, elevated but not prohibitive insurance, gradual capability attrition on Iranian side, eventual fragile ceasefire or de facto pause driven by costs, followed by slow commercial and insurance recovery over subsequent 6–12 months. Full multi-domain normality most probable in the 12–18 month window if no new major trigger occurs.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

(~15–20% probability mass) Rapid mutual recognition of stalemate, high-level deal that explicitly restores pre-war Hormuz arrangements without Iranian unilateral control, verified stand-down of proxies, quick mine clearance/insurance capacity return, and Gulf state economic pressure. Traffic and premiums normalize within 1–3 months of kinetic pause.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

(~30–35% probability mass) Hormuz control remains a red line; proxies sustain low-cost disruption; insurance stays elevated for 12+ months; repeated partial ceasefires collapse; energy flows adapt via longer routes, rationing, or strategic reserves but never fully recover confidence. Residual risk persists beyond 18 months.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Overestimation risk: Under-weighting speed of insurance market recovery or commercial desperation once kinetic activity pauses; over-weighting current intensity as permanent. Underestimation risk: Treating Hormuz sovereignty dispute as solvable when it may be structural; under-weighting domestic political incentives for continued pressure on both sides; under-weighting ease of proxy reactivation. Load-bearing assumptions: that a military pause is achievable without resolving Hormuz control, and that insurance/volume metrics can recover without a formal, verified settlement. Strongest contrary evidence would be a sudden, durable, publicly verified stand-down with immediate traffic surge past 80 vessels/day and premium collapse below 1%.
