**1. Forecast Question**  
Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each horizon: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
Functional normality requires simultaneous, sustained satisfaction (minimum 2–4 continuous weeks without material reversal) of these observable conditions:

- Direct military activity: No significant kinetic exchanges (airstrikes, large-scale missile/drone barrages) between the US/Israel and Iranian territory or major Gulf state targets. Residual low-level activity must be at or below pre-February 2026 baseline threat levels.
- Proxy activity: Houthi attacks on Saudi/Gulf infrastructure and shipping at or below pre-2026 escalation intensity; no sustained IRGC-directed projectile, mine, or boarding attacks on commercial vessels in the Strait of Hormuz or adjacent waters.
- Shipping security: Hormuz and nearby waters free of active mines, projectile threats, and boarding risks for commercial traffic. Naval escorts and routine “dark” (AIS-off) sailing are no longer standard or necessary.
- Commercial transit levels: Vessel transits (AIS-visible plus adjusted dark) at ≥80–90% of pre-war baseline (~100–120 total daily). Oil and LNG flows through Hormuz at ≥90% of pre-war volumes (~18–20 mbd liquids).
- War-risk insurance: Additional premiums ≤1% of hull value (ideally approaching the pre-war 0.15–0.25%), with broad market availability of cover. Special US government backstops or escorts are not the primary enabling mechanism.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Iranian seaborne exports unblocked and flowing on commercial terms; non-Iranian Gulf exports no longer reliant on STS shuttles, dark corridors, or bypass pipelines as the primary mode. Overall Middle East exports stable at pre-war levels without continuous military protection.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent crude within a normal historical range or <20% above the immediate pre-war average (~$70–80 range), with the persistent wartime risk premium largely gone and volatility normalized.
- Force posture of major actors: Material drawdown of US carrier strike groups and continuous escort operations; Iranian IRGC Navy not on continuous high-alert targeting of commercial traffic; no active dual blockade of Iranian ports and Hormuz.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new leadership assassinations, major infrastructure attacks, nuclear-related strike justifications, or expansion of proxy campaigns that re-ignite the cycle.

A temporary ceasefire, volume recovery under escort, or lower oil prices alone do not meet the definition. Commercial and insurance normalization are gating.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot**  
As of late October 2026 (war day ~242 since 28 February US-Israeli strikes that killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei), the conflict continues. A June Memorandum of Understanding collapsed in July; the US reimposed a naval blockade of Iranian ports on 14 July (now >100 days), which CENTCOM states has prevented ~$25 billion in Iranian crude exports. IRGC forces continue attacking commercial shipping; October has seen the highest attack numbers of the war (dozens of incidents, some sinkings or severe damage, claims of mines). UKMTO and other trackers log elevated incidents. 

Oil flows through Hormuz (including dark/STS) are roughly 50–80% of pre-war on various measures (Kpler and others report weekly figures in the 9–14 mbd range against ~17–21 mbd baselines), with overall Middle East exports sometimes approaching or exceeding pre-war levels via pipelines and shuttles. Brent has traded near $110–117. War-risk additional premiums stand at 6–10% of hull value (vs. 0.15–0.25% pre-war), often millions of dollars per VLCC transit and a gating factor. Houthi attacks on Saudi airports, oil infrastructure, and the East-West pipeline have intensified, with Saudi counterstrikes in Yemen; Bab el-Mandeb is also affected. Regional talks (Saudi, Turkish, Pakistani officials in Tehran) continue without a durable deal. Trump has pledged no major strikes before the 3 November US midterms; UN Ambassador Waltz has said the war could last another year. Iranian statements assert control over non-compliant traffic; US forces provide escorts. Facts on exact attack success rates and some mine claims remain disputed between IRGC and CENTCOM.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
- **Iran-Iraq Tanker War (1984–1988)**: Prolonged maritime harassment in the Gulf with attacks on neutral shipping. Relevance: direct analog for sustained IRGC-style interdiction. Breakdown: ended with broader ceasefire and regime calculations; no modern insurance markets or dual blockade. Suggests commercial recovery lags military pause by several months to a year.
- **Limited 2019–2020 Hormuz/Saudi incidents**: Isolated tanker and Aramco attacks produced temporary premiums and rerouting that largely normalized within weeks to a few months once attacks stopped. Relevance: shows rapid insurance and traffic response to de-escalation. Breakdown: scale was far smaller than current sustained campaign.
- **2023–ongoing Red Sea Houthi campaign**: Persistent low-level attacks forced multi-year Cape of Good Hope rerouting and elevated premiums. Relevance: proxy endurance and insurance stickiness. Breakdown: less central chokepoint volume and no concurrent major power blockade.
- **1991 Gulf War aftermath**: Rapid military resolution of the Iraq threat, followed by months of oil infrastructure and market recovery. Relevance: force posture drawdown timelines. Breakdown: one-sided outcome and different chokepoint dynamics.
- **Post-JCPOA and sanctions cycles**: Oil flows adjusted within weeks to policy shifts, but residual security threats persisted. Relevance: commercial adaptability. Breakdown: lacked active wartime interdiction.

Base-rate prior (outside-in, before case-specific weighting): 1 week ~5%; 1 month ~12%; 3 months ~28%; 6 months ~45%; 1 year ~60%; 18 months ~70%. These already embed lags between military pauses and commercial/insurance normalization.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**  
- US political incentive to reduce energy prices before/after midterms and limit costs.  
- Regional mediation (Pakistan, Oman, Saudi, Turkey) seeking to contain spillover.  
- Commercial incentives: high freight and oil prices make some risk-taking profitable once attacks ease.  
- Iranian economic pressure from blockade (lost revenue, proxy funding strain).  
- Existing partial volume recovery demonstrates technical adaptability of shuttle/escort systems.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**  
- Sustained IRGC attack capability and political utility of Hormuz pressure (ranked highest).  
- Insurance market lag and risk aversion (second; premiums do not fall quickly).  
- Dual blockade and unresolved core issues (nuclear, sanctions, force posture).  
- Active Houthi campaign and Yemen theater (proxy endurance).  
- History of collapsed deals and mutual distrust; post-midterm strike options remain open.  
- Force posture remains elevated on both sides.

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Near-term (through midterms and immediate aftermath) continues with elevated attacks, escort-dependent flows, high premiums, and dual blockade. A military pause becomes more likely in the 1–3 month window under political or cost pressure, but commercial normalization lags: insurance remains elevated, free transit is incomplete, and Houthi activity persists. Partial volume recovery continues via shuttles and pipelines. By 6–12 months a more durable restraint or limited agreement is the modal outcome, with full functional normality (per the strict definition) more probable only in the 12–18 month range. Frozen conflict with residual risk premium is a close competitor.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Post-midterm deal that lifts the blockade, ends major strikes, and includes verified Hormuz security arrangements within weeks. Insurance markets reprice quickly on confirmed multi-week safety. Houthi campaign is contained by Saudi/regional pressure. Volumes and free transit recover by 3–6 months. Probability of this path is modest (~15–25% conditional on near-term pause).

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
No durable deal; intermittent strikes resume after midterms; IRGC maintains harassment capability; Houthis continue pressure; insurance stays structurally elevated; dual blockade persists as the new baseline. Commercial traffic remains escort- or dark-dependent for 12+ months. Probability material (~30–40% through the 6–12 month window).

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Strongest case that normalization is faster: commercial incentives and partial volume recovery already demonstrate adaptability; political calendars create windows; blockade costs on Iran are high. Strongest case that it is slower: insurance and shipowner risk aversion have multi-month lags even after attacks stop; proxies are hard to restrain; prior agreements collapsed quickly; core political issues (nuclear, sanctions, force posture) remain unresolved. Load-bearing assumptions: that IRGC attack capability can be durably suppressed without regime-level change, and that insurance markets will reprice on verified safety rather than residual political risk. I may be underestimating near-term political deal probability and overestimating insurance stickiness; conversely, I may be underweighting the durability of low-level interdiction once established.
