**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each horizon: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months (from 1 August 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality requires simultaneous satisfaction of all of the following observable conditions for a sustained period (minimum 14 consecutive days, with no material reversal in the subsequent 7 days):

- Direct military activity: No sustained direct strikes by Iran, the United States, or Israel on each other’s forces, territory, or critical infrastructure in the Gulf region (occasional isolated incidents below a defined threshold of intensity do not disqualify if they do not trigger tit-for-tat cycles).
- Proxy activity: No significant proxy attacks (Houthis, Iraqi militias, Hezbollah-linked actions affecting Gulf/Red Sea energy or shipping routes) at levels that materially disrupt commercial operations or force major diversions.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Safe, routine commercial transit using established Traffic Separation Schemes without routine IRGC “permission,” warning shots, boarding, or drone/missile attacks on merchant vessels. Mine threat reduced to residual levels manageable by normal clearance operations.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits through Hormuz sustained at ≥80% of pre-war baseline (~100–110+ of the ~130 daily pre-war average), with AIS visibility and normal routing (not predominantly “dark” or corridor-restricted).
- War-risk insurance conditions: War-risk premiums and additional premiums for Gulf/Hormuz transit returned to near pre-war levels or a stable modest elevation (<2–3× pre-war baseline for standard tanker coverage), with broad market availability and no systematic refusal by major underwriters.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Combined Gulf oil exports (Saudi, UAE, Kuwait, Qatar, Iraq, Iran where relevant) sustained near pre-war volumes (~18–20+ mb/d oil equivalent range through Hormuz routes), with Qatar LNG production/exports at ≥80–90% of pre-war capacity and no force majeure declarations driven by security.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent crude price volatility (30-day realized) and risk premia consistent with pre-war ranges; no persistent large geopolitical premium driven by Hormuz closure risk; physical cargoes and freight rates normalized.
- Force posture of major actors: No active US naval blockade of Iranian ports; no Iranian assertion of exclusive administrative control requiring fees or mandatory coordination as a condition of transit; major-power force levels in the Gulf not in active combat posture beyond normal peacetime presence.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new nuclear-related red lines crossed, no large-scale proxy mobilization that reopens secondary fronts (Bab el-Mandeb, Red Sea), and no political events that re-ignite direct US–Iran kinetic cycles.

A temporary ceasefire, battlefield pause, lower oil prices alone, or partial recovery in one domain does not meet the standard. All domains must be functionally restored together.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot (as of ~1 August 2026)**

War began 28 February 2026 with US–Israeli strikes. A June 17 Islamabad MoU created a 60-day negotiating window and partial reopening of Hormuz, producing a temporary recovery in transits (peaks in the 50–70+ range in late June, still well below ~130 pre-war daily). The MoU collapsed around 7–8 July after Iranian attacks on commercial vessels and US retaliatory strikes. Hostilities resumed and continue in cycles of US strikes on Iranian coastal/missile/drone/naval targets and Iranian missile/drone attacks on US bases, Gulf states, and shipping. Iran continues to assert exclusive or dominant control over Hormuz transit (permission, designated routes, threats of force). US maintains or reimposes elements of a naval blockade on Iranian ports. Shipping volumes have collapsed again relative to the June recovery; many vessels operate dark or avoid; mines remain a residual issue. Oil prices (Brent) are elevated around the high $80s–low $90s with heightened volatility. Proxy activity (Houthis on Red Sea/Saudi shipping) has reactivated. No durable ceasefire or agreed transit regime exists. **4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant reference classes (selected for chokepoint disruption + regional state-on-state or state-proxy war involving energy transit):

1. Tanker War / Iran–Iraq War (1980–88), especially 1984–88 phase: Repeated attacks on shipping in Gulf/Hormuz approaches. Partial commercial adaptation occurred within months via escorts and routing changes, but full functional normality (safe routine transit without elevated risk and insurance distortion) required the 1988 ceasefire and subsequent years. Suggests multi-year tail for full normalization after intense phase.
2. 2019–2021 Gulf tanker attacks / Max Maxwell / Iranian seizures and limpet mines: Episodic rather than continuous war. Shipping largely continued with elevated premiums and some diversions; risk premium persisted for 12–24+ months. Breaks down because intensity and duration far lower than 2026.
3. Houthi Red Sea campaign (late 2023–ongoing into 2025–26 context): Major commercial diversion (Cape of Good Hope), sustained high insurance, and incomplete recovery even after military pressure and partial deals. Full return of volume and risk pricing took/ takes many months to years; partial recovery possible in 3–6 months under de-escalation.
4. 1990–91 Gulf War (Iraq invasion of Kuwait through Desert Storm): Rapid military resolution (~7 months total active phase) produced relatively quick commercial recovery once ceasefire held, but residual risk and force posture adjustments lasted longer. Suggests faster path if decisive military/diplomatic end occurs.
5. Post-2003 Iraq War maritime/energy recovery and 2011–12 Strait of Hormuz closure threats: Threats alone produced temporary spikes and modest insurance elevation lasting months; actual sustained closure (rare historically) produces longer tails.
6. Broader post-major Middle East wars (1973, 1991, 2006 Lebanon, etc.): Energy market risk premia typically decay over 3–12 months after kinetic peak if no new trigger, but commercial/insurance/force-posture normalization often lags 6–18+ months.

Base-rate prior (before 2026-specific evidence, for functional multi-domain normality as defined): 1 week <5%; 1 month ~8–12%; 3 months ~20–25%; 6 months ~35–40%; 1 year ~50–55%; 18 months ~60–65%. These reflect historical patterns that military pauses or partial deals produce incomplete commercial recovery, with long tails driven by insurance, residual mines/threats, proxy activity, and unresolved political claims over the waterway.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

1. Mutual economic exhaustion and desire of Gulf producers for revenue restoration (highest importance).
2. US political incentive to reduce costs, casualties, and gasoline/oil price pressure.
3. Iranian incentive to convert temporary leverage over Hormuz into durable political recognition or sanctions relief without indefinite kinetic costs.
4. Mediation channels (Oman, Qatar, Pakistan) that previously produced the MoU.
5. Demonstrated partial commercial rebound during the June pause (proves elasticity once kinetic risk falls).

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

1. Iranian hardline insistence on administrative control of Hormuz as core residual leverage (highest importance; load-bearing).
2. Cycle of retaliation that has repeatedly broken pauses.
3. Residual mines, elevated war-risk pricing inertia, and insurer caution (commercial lag).
4. Proxy forces providing low-cost disruption options.
5. Domestic political constraints on both sides (US credibility, Iranian regime survival narratives, Israeli concerns).
6. Absence of a verified, multi-party, enforceable transit and monitoring regime.

**8. Decomposed Sub-Forecasts**

Approximate independent-ish probabilities of key components being substantially met by horizon (then gated):

- Major sustained reduction in direct strikes: 1w 5–10%; 1m 20%; 3m 40%; 6m 55%; 1y 65%; 18m 70%.
- Sustained safe commercial transit (≥80% volume, normal routing): 1w <5%; 1m 10–15%; 3m 30%; 6m 45%; 1y 55%; 18m 65%.
- Meaningful insurance normalization: lags transit safety by 1–3+ months; lower than transit probabilities.
- Oil/LNG export recovery near pre-war: tracks transit + Qatar restart; similar to transit but slightly higher once kinetic risk falls.
- Proxy restraint: 1w low; rises more slowly (persistent option).
- Force drawdown / end of blockade posture: requires political deal; slower.
- No new major escalation trigger: declines with time as windows for miscalculation remain open.

Gating factors are (1) durable political agreement on Hormuz administration/transit and (2) sustained absence of kinetic cycles. Commercial and insurance recovery cannot fully occur without these.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued episodic kinetic cycles and failed or partial pauses for the next 1–3 months. Mediation produces another temporary arrangement (possibly with limited Iranian coordination role or fees under international monitoring). Shipping recovers partially (50–70% volumes) with still-elevated insurance. Full multi-domain normality remains elusive until a more durable political bargain or exhaustion produces sustained quiet, most likely in the 6–18 month window. Residual risk premium and proxy options persist.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Decisive mutual recognition that continued fighting is net-negative, leading to a verified free-navigation agreement (possibly with Omani/IMO role), rapid demining, US blockade lift, and sanctions relief package within weeks. Shipping and insurance rebound on the June model but sustain. Probability of this path driving the short horizons higher is low (~10–15% conditional on major diplomatic breakthrough).

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Iran successfully converts temporary physical control into de-facto recognition or a fee/permission regime that major shippers and insurers partially accommodate. Low-level attacks and proxy actions continue indefinitely. Volumes recover only partially; risk pricing and force posture remain elevated for years. This is the main downside that keeps long-horizon probabilities from rising further.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Possible over-estimation of normalization: The June rebound showed surprising commercial willingness to return quickly; economic pain on all sides (Iran, Gulf producers, US domestic prices) could force faster compromise than reference classes suggest. Under-estimation risk: Iranian regime may treat Hormuz control as existential residual leverage post-nuclear and leadership losses, making any free-transit deal politically toxic; insurance markets and shippers may require longer proof of safety than military pauses provide. Load-bearing assumption is that a political resolution of the transit-administration dispute is feasible within 18 months; if it is not, probabilities at longer horizons are too high.
