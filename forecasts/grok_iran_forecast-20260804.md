**1. Forecast Question**

Probability that the Persian Gulf returns to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War, by horizons of 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months (from ~4 August 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality requires simultaneous satisfaction of these observable, multi-domain criteria for a sustained period (not a temporary pause):

- Direct military activity: No sustained US/Israeli or Iranian strikes on each other’s military assets, bases, or energy/port infrastructure in the Gulf theater; no active major air/missile campaigns.
- Proxy activity: No significant proxy attacks (Houthis, Iraqi militias, Hezbollah-linked actions) on Gulf shipping, energy infrastructure, or commercial targets that materially disrupt operations.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Commercial vessels transit freely via established TSS or mutually accepted corridors without routine IRGC interdiction, boarding, drone/missile attacks, mine threats, or requirement for Iranian prior approval/coordination as a de facto veto. Threat levels (JMIC/UKMTO) at or near pre-war “substantial” or lower, not “severe.”
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits ≥70–80% of pre-war baseline (~100–130 vessels/day, ~half energy-related), sustained over weeks, including mainstream (non-Iranian-linked, non-sanctioned) tankers and LNG carriers.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Premiums and availability return near pre-war norms (not elevated multiples that price out most commercial operators); major underwriters resume standard cover without extraordinary war exclusions or escorts required for most voyages.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf (Saudi, UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Iraq, Iran) crude/product/LNG exports at ≥80–90% of pre-war volumes via Hormuz or viable alternatives, without chronic backlog, stranded vessels, or forced rerouting that materially cuts seaborne flows.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent/WTI realized volatility and risk premia near pre-war levels; no persistent “Hormuz disruption premium” dominating price formation; inventories and flows support normal commercial behavior without emergency stock draws as the primary buffer.
- Force posture of major actors: US/CENTCOM and Iranian forces shift from high-tempo kinetic posture and enforced corridors/blockades toward routine presence; no large-scale active naval blockade of Iranian ports or enforced “safe corridors” as the primary transit mode.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new large-scale attacks on desalination/power plants, nuclear sites, Kharg Island, major ports, or carrier strike groups that restart the tit-for-tat cycle.

A temporary ceasefire, partial traffic recovery under Iranian veto, lower oil prices from demand destruction/alternative routes, or a battlefield pause does **not** meet this standard. Commercial normalization lags military pauses due to insurance, charterer risk aversion, and crew/owner decisions.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant classes (3–6):

1. **1980s Tanker War (Iran-Iraq)**: Prolonged attacks on shipping in Gulf/Hormuz approaches; traffic reduced but never fully halted; insurance spiked; ended with war’s end (1988). Partial functional recovery took months after ceasefire; full commercial confidence lagged. Suggests multi-month to year+ for normality after major kinetic phase. Breaks down: interstate conventional war vs today’s asymmetric + great-power involvement; no full modern insurance/global just-in-time markets.
2. **2019–2021 tanker attacks / max-pressure period (Iran-linked)**: Sporadic attacks, seizures, insurance spikes; traffic largely continued with elevated risk. Normalization of premiums/behavior took 6–18 months after peak incidents subsided. Relevant for insurance and commercial caution; breaks down: far lower intensity than 2026 full war + de facto closure.
3. **Red Sea/Houthi disruption (2023–ongoing, intensified 2026)**: Major diversion around Africa; insurance/war-risk elevated; partial recovery only with sustained escorts + reduced attacks. Full normality not yet achieved after 2+ years. Strong parallel for chokepoint + proxy + insurance lag.
4. **Gulf War 1990–91 / post-invasion recovery**: Shipping and oil flows recovered relatively quickly (weeks-months) after kinetic phase ended and sanctions/escorts stabilized, but with US force posture elevated for years. Relevant for rapid post-ceasefire traffic rebound if security credible; breaks down: clear conventional victory + no residual Iranian veto ambition.
5. **Post-2015 JCPOA / sanctions relief periods**: Partial oil export recovery and shipping normalization over 6–12 months when political risk fell, but reversible. Relevant for export continuity under political uncertainty.
6. **Other chokepoint disruptions (Suez 1956/1967/2021, Black Sea post-2022)**: Traffic rebounds in weeks-months once physical threat clears and insurance stabilizes, but confidence and volumes lag if political risk persists.

**Base-rate prior (before case-specific evidence)**: Probability of full functional normality (per definition) low in short term due to insurance/commercial lag and residual risk. Approximate cumulative: 1 week <5%; 1 month 5–15%; 3 months 15–30%; 6 months 30–50%; 1 year 45–65%; 18 months 55–75%. These reflect historical pattern that kinetic pauses precede commercial recovery by months, and residual claims of control or proxy risk slow full return.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

Ranked: (1) Credible, enforceable Iran-Oman/US-backed arrangement restoring free transit without Iranian veto (gating for insurance/traffic); (2) Sustained reduction in direct kinetic exchanges and ship attacks; (3) Gulf state mediation + economic pain on all sides; (4) Market/commercial pressure (backlogs, high freights, Asian buyer needs); (5) US political incentive to declare “win” via reopening.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

Ranked: (1) Iranian strategic insistence on lasting leverage/control over Hormuz as compensation for losses and deterrent; (2) Mutual distrust + history of MOU collapses → rapid re-escalation risk; (3) Insurance and commercial risk aversion lagging any military pause by months; (4) Proxy networks and multi-theater linkage (Lebanon, Red Sea, Iraq); (5) Force posture inertia and domestic politics in US/Iran making full drawdown hard; (6) Second-order effects (stranded vessels, crew issues, alternative route capacity limits).

**10. Most Likely Path**

Fragile or partial arrangement on a managed corridor (Iran-Oman with external monitoring) produces episodic traffic recovery to 30–60% of normal over 1–3 months, with insurance remaining elevated and occasional incidents. Full criteria met only gradually if kinetic exchanges stay low and commercial confidence rebuilds; otherwise oscillating low-level disruption continues. Oil markets price a persistent risk premium. Force postures stay elevated longer than traffic.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Strong, enforceable free-transit deal (no Iranian veto, multilateral monitoring) + sustained quiet for 4–6 weeks triggers rapid vessel release, premium compression, and volume recovery. Gulf economic pressure + US political need for a deliverable accelerate this. Probability of this path materializing soon is modest (~15–25% in next 1–3 months).

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Iran prioritizes leverage over revenue; talks fail or produce contested arrangements; low-level attacks and proxy actions continue; insurance stays high; transits remain structurally suppressed or episodic. US responds intermittently. This path is plausible given MOU history and stated Iranian positions (~40–50% weight in medium term).

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Possible overestimation of normalization: Underweights Iranian domestic/hardline incentives to retain Hormuz leverage after wartime losses and the stickiness of insurance markets (historical cases show multi-month lags even after physical threats ease). Commercial actors may require longer proof periods than political announcements. Underestimation risk: Economic pain on Gulf exporters, Asian buyers, and Iran itself, plus Trump incentive for a visible “win,” could produce faster practical accommodation than base rates suggest. Load-bearing assumptions: Iranian preference for control is sticky; insurance is a true gate; no sudden major escalation (or sudden comprehensive deal) in next weeks. Evidence that would falsify: Sustained >80 vessel/day mainstream transits + major insurer statements of near-normal rates for 4+ weeks without attacks.
