**1. Forecast Question**  
Probability that the Persian Gulf returns to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War, by horizons of 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months (as of 26 August 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
Observable, multi-domain criteria that must hold concurrently for a sustained period (not a single day or temporary pause):

- Direct military activity: No large-scale reciprocal strikes on military/infrastructure targets inside Iran, Gulf states, or major US/partner bases; residual low-level incidents (isolated drones/mines) below thresholds that deter commercial operators.
- Proxy activity: No sustained high-tempo attacks by Iranian-aligned groups (Houthis, Iraqi militias, Hezbollah) on shipping, energy infrastructure, or US/partner forces that materially raise regional risk.
- Shipping security (Strait of Hormuz and approaches): Freedom of navigation restored under international norms or accepted multilateral arrangements; no systematic vessel targeting, mine threats, or coercive turn-backs; AIS-visible traffic dominant.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily commodity vessel transits ≥70% of pre-war baseline (~85–130/day range, targeting sustained ≥60–90); “dark” transits <20%; no large stranded fleet.
- War-risk insurance: Hull war-risk premiums ≤2–3× pre-war baseline (pre-crisis ~0.1–0.25% of hull value); cover widely available without case-by-case denial or extreme surcharges that price out most commercial operators.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf crude/products/LNG flows via Hormuz + pipelines restoring to ≥80–85% of pre-war regional export capacity (~15–20 mbd crude/products equivalent context); Iranian exports not fully blockaded if commercial.
- Energy market stress/volatility: Brent risk premium attributable to Hormuz/Gulf security normalized (prices reflecting fundamentals rather than acute closure risk); no repeated multi-dollar daily spikes driven by maritime incidents.
- Force posture of major actors: Meaningful de-escalation or drawdown of surge deployments (US naval/air assets, Iranian coastal/missile posture, Gulf state alert levels); no open-ended high-intensity escort operations as primary transit enabler.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No breakthrough nuclear reconstitution, major ground operations, third-party great-power kinetic involvement, or irreversible territorial/control claims that reset the conflict.

A temporary ceasefire, partial US-escorted “dark” flows, or oil-price dips without the above do not qualify. Commercial normalization lags military pauses.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant classes (3–6):

1. **Iran-Iraq Tanker War (1984–88 peak intensity)**: Hundreds of attacks; US escorts (Earnest Will); mines. Strait never fully closed; commercial shipping adapted with elevated risk/insurance; flows largely maintained. Stabilization tied to 1988 ceasefire; functional commercial recovery over months post-ceasefire, residual risk years. Breaks down: No full modern closure or nuclear overlay; lower insurance impact relative to today’s capital intensity. Suggests: Partial security recovery 3–12 months post-major de-escalation; full commercial 6–18+ months.

2. **Houthi Red Sea campaign (2023–ongoing into 2026 context)**: Sustained proxy attacks; insurance spikes; major carrier rerouting; partial US/partner escorts. Traffic suppressed for many months; incomplete recovery despite military pressure. Breaks down: Secondary chokepoint, not primary Gulf oil artery; different actor control. Suggests: Multi-month to multi-year elevated risk even with reduced tempo.

3. **Suez closures/disruptions (1956, 1967–75, intermittent later)**: Physical/political closures; long recovery of routing confidence and insurance. 1967–75 multi-year. Breaks down: Canal vs. open strait; different geopolitics. Suggests: Confidence rebuild measured in many months to years after physical reopening.

4. **Gulf War 1990–91 / post-invasion maritime**: Rapid military resolution; shipping/insurance normalized within months of ceasefire due to clear coalition victory and no ongoing Iranian-style coastal threat. Breaks down: Decisive conventional defeat vs. current attritional/asymmetric. Suggests faster recovery only under clear decisive outcome (unlikely baseline).

5. **Prior Iran Hormuz threats/mini-crises (2011–12, 2019 tanker attacks, seizures)**: Short spikes; insurance elevated weeks–months; rapid commercial rebound after de-escalation. Breaks down: No sustained war or blockade. Suggests weeks–months for limited episodes.

**Base-rate prior (pre-current-evidence adjustment, approximate cumulative probability of functional normality under demanding definition):**  
1 week: ~1–3%; 1 month: ~5–10%; 3 months: ~15–25%; 6 months: ~30–40%; 1 year: ~45–55%; 18 months: ~55–65%. Anchored on prolonged asymmetric Gulf cases where commercial lag is structural.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Durable political/military de-escalation or accepted transit regime (Iran-Oman + US acquiescence).  
2. Sustained safe AIS-visible transit without coercive enforcement.  
3. Insurance market response (capacity return, rate compression after weeks of calm).  
4. Pipeline + alternative flows + production offsets reducing urgency of full Hormuz.  
5. Fatigue/economic pressure on Iran producing compromise.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Iranian insistence on control/fees/role + linkage to broader war terms/compensation.  
2. Structural commercial lag (insurance, crew willingness, charterer risk aversion) even after kinetic pause.  
3. Residual asymmetric capability (mines, drones, coastal systems) enabling low-cost disruption.  
4. Proxy activation or secondary fronts (Red Sea, Iraq, Lebanon).  
5. Domestic/political incentives on both sides against full concession; US blockade leverage.

**8. Decomposed Sub-Forecasts** (illustrative probabilities of component success by horizon; gating factors noted)  

- Major reduction in direct strikes: 1w ~10%; 1m ~25%; 3m ~45%; 6m ~60%; 1y ~70%; 18m ~75%.  
- Sustained safe commercial Hormuz transit (≥70% baseline, low dark): 1w ~5%; 1m ~15%; 3m ~30%; 6m ~45%; 1y ~55%; 18m ~65%. (Gating.)  
- Meaningful insurance normalization (≤2–3×): lags transit safety by weeks–months; lower than transit probs early, converging later.  
- Oil/LNG export recovery (≥80–85% regional): higher than pure Hormuz due to pipelines (~Saudi East-West, UAE Habshan-Fujairah); 1w ~20%; rising to ~70%+ by 1y.  
- Proxy restraint: 1w ~30%; rising slowly to ~60% by 18m.  
- Force drawdown: lags furthest; low single digits early, ~40–50% by 18m.  
- No new major escalation trigger: ~60–70% early, declining modestly with time as opportunities accumulate.

Gating: Shipping security + insurance are binding for “functional normality.” Military pause alone insufficient.

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Protracted low-to-medium intensity contestation of Hormuz control. Temporary corridors or US-facilitated dark flows continue at suppressed commercial levels. Insurance remains elevated for many months. Partial export recovery via pipelines + assisted transit. Diplomatic tracks (Oman, others) produce interim arrangements short of full freedom-of-navigation normality. Functional normality arrives unevenly after 6–18 months via fatigue and adaptation rather than clean resolution. Oil markets price residual risk.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Clean implementation of Iran-Oman corridor within weeks, zero major incidents for 30–45 days, US signals blockade flexibility, insurers rapidly compress rates, major carriers resume AIS-visible schedules. Probability of this path low (~10–15% cumulative influence on early horizons). Would lift 1–3 month forecasts materially.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Iran links full access to comprehensive war settlement/compensation; residual asymmetric attacks or proxy activation continue; insurance stays punitive; commercial operators stay out; secondary fronts expand. Extends elevated disruption well beyond 18 months. Plausible given current Iranian statements and prior MoU fragility.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Overestimating normalization: May underweight Iranian domestic incentives to retain Hormuz leverage and the depth of commercial risk aversion (crew, charterers, banks). Insurance markets can remain sticky longer than kinetic pauses. Underestimating: Pipelines + US-assisted capacity + global supply response already mitigate absolute crisis, potentially accelerating political willingness to accept imperfect regimes. Base rates from Tanker War may be too optimistic given today’s higher capital intensity and ESG/crew constraints. Load-bearing assumption: No sudden decisive military outcome or third-party intervention that resets timelines.
