**1. Forecast Question**  
Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each horizon (1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
Functional normality requires simultaneous, sustained satisfaction (minimum 14 consecutive days, preferably 30) of these observable criteria as of the horizon date:  
- Direct military activity: No significant kinetic exchanges (US or Iranian strikes on territory/assets, major IRGC attacks) beyond isolated, non-escalatory incidents.  
- Proxy activity: Houthis and other Iranian-aligned groups cease major attacks on shipping, Saudi/Gulf infrastructure, or related targets.  
- Shipping security (Hormuz and nearby waters): No active mining campaigns, projectile/drone attacks, or boardings; vessels transit with AIS on as the norm, without routine military escorts or “dark” operations as primary mode.  
- Commercial transit levels: AIS-visible commercial transits ≥70% of pre-war average (~60+ of ~85 daily).  
- War-risk insurance: Additional premiums ≤1% of hull value (near pre-war 0.15–0.25%).  
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf exports (including via Hormuz) at ≥90% of pre-war volumes without heavy reliance on bypasses, shuttle tankers, or dark shipping as the primary mechanism; damaged LNG trains and terminals largely restored.  
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent risk premium normalized (no sustained $20+ war premium; volatility consistent with non-crisis baselines).  
- Force posture of major actors: US carrier presence reduced to ≤1 strike group; Iranian IRGC naval posture defensive rather than actively aggressive.  
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No verified nuclear breakout indicators, no major Gulf-state infrastructure attacks, and no collapse of mediation channels into open escalation.  

A temporary ceasefire, lower oil prices alone, or a battlefield pause does not satisfy the definition. Commercial and insurance normalization are gating factors.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot (as of 8 November 2026)**  
The conflict began 28 February 2026 with US-Israeli strikes. An April ceasefire and June Islamabad Memorandum collapsed by early July; hostilities resumed and continue at low-to-moderate intensity. Day ~254 of the crisis.  
AIS-visible commercial traffic through Hormuz is ~9% of pre-war levels (~7.3/day vs ~85). Hundreds of vessels remain anchored or waiting. Ongoing IRGC claims of mine strikes and confirmed projectile attacks on tankers continue (UKMTO and open sources); limited US strikes on Qeshm Island air defenses occurred in early November.  
Gulf crude exports (excluding Iran) have recovered near pre-war levels via pipelines (Saudi East-West, UAE to Fujairah; ~40% bypass share vs 17% pre-war), ship-to-ship transfers in the Gulf of Oman, night/dark transits under US protection, and other adaptations (Kpler and related tracking). Physical Hormuz flows remain far below normal. War-risk premiums are 6–10% of hull value (vs ~0.25% pre-war). Brent is near $98 after midterm-related dips from higher levels.  
US force posture includes 2–3 carriers, blockade enforcement, and preparations for possible post-midterm intensification. Qatari and other mediation continues but has not produced a deal; Iranian conditions include sanctions relief, blockade lift, and other demands. Nuclear sites show no confirmed reconstruction (IAEA), but inspectors lack access and the stockpile status is unverified. Houthis remain active against Saudi targets; Hezbollah is degraded. Midterms (3 November) have occurred.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
- **1980s Tanker War (1984–1988)**: Closest maritime analogue. Hundreds of attacks occurred; Hormuz never fully closed; oil continued to flow with escorts and higher costs. Full recovery followed the 1988 ceasefire within months. Suggests partial commercial adaptation during active conflict is possible, but functional normality (insurance, unrestricted transit, reduced posture) requires political settlement. Partial stabilization timeline: months to years during fighting; post-settlement recovery in 3–6 months.  
- **Red Sea Houthi campaign (2023–ongoing)**: Persistent chokepoint disruption with elevated insurance, diversions, and incomplete normalization after years. Relevant for proxy persistence and insurance stickiness. Suggests multi-year residual risk even after partial de-escalation.  
- **2019–2020 Gulf tanker attacks**: Shorter, more limited incidents resolved with deterrence and diplomacy relatively quickly. Suggests rapid recovery possible when attacks are not sustained by a full war.  
- **Other chokepoint disruptions (Suez temporary closures, etc.)**: Physical reopening can be rapid, but insurance and behavioral recovery lag by weeks to months.  

Base-rate prior (pre-current-evidence adjustment, weighted toward Tanker War and Red Sea cases):  
- 1 week: ~5%  
- 1 month: ~10%  
- 3 months: ~20%  
- 6 months: ~35%  
- 1 year: ~50%  
- 18 months: ~60%  

Full functional normality is uncommon without a durable political settlement; commercial lags typically exceed military pauses.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**  
1. Oil-export adaptation (pipelines, dark shipping, escorts) already restored substantial volumes—reduces immediate economic pressure for escalation.  
2. Active mediation channel (Qatar and others) and post-midterm political calendar.  
3. Iranian nuclear program degraded with no confirmed reconstruction (IAEA).  
4. Hezbollah degradation limiting one proxy front.  
5. US interest in avoiding prolonged high energy prices and open-ended commitment.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**  
1. Persistent IRGC capability and willingness to attack shipping and lay mines—core leverage.  
2. Extremely elevated war-risk insurance and low AIS transit—commercial lag is structural.  
3. Unresolved nuclear verification and Iranian demands (sanctions, blockade, reparations).  
4. Active Houthi campaign and residual proxy risk.  
5. High US force posture and post-midterm escalation options still available.  
6. Infrastructure damage and behavioral caution by shippers/insurers.

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Continued low-intensity conflict with intermittent attacks on shipping, managed oil flows via adapted routes, elevated insurance, and stalled diplomacy. Gradual commercial improvement only after a durable political settlement (most plausible in the 6–18 month window). Full functional normality is unlikely before 1 year.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Post-midterm deal that meets core Iranian economic demands while freezing nuclear activity under limited verification; rapid attack cessation; insurance market responds within weeks; transit recovers above 70%. Probability of this path leading to definition satisfaction by 3–6 months: low single digits to low teens.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
No deal; continued or renewed strikes; persistent mining and attacks; Houthis maintain pressure; insurance remains elevated for years; buyers permanently diversify. Functional normality still incomplete at 18 months. This path is plausible given current leverage dynamics.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Strongest case that I overestimate normalization: Commercial and insurance recovery historically lags far behind military pauses (Tanker War, Red Sea); Iranian leverage via Hormuz is existential and cheap to maintain; nuclear stockpile unverified creates permanent escalation risk; infrastructure damage takes years.  
Strongest case that I underestimate it: Oil-export adaptation already substantial; US domestic political incentives against prolonged high prices; Iranian military degradation may force restraint; markets can normalize faster than reference classes once attacks stop.  
Load-bearing assumptions: Insurance is the slowest variable; political settlement is required for full definition; proxy restraint follows Iranian decisions more than independent Houthi calculus.
