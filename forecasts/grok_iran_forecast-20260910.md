**1. Forecast Question**  
Probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by horizons of 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months (as of 10 September 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
“Return to functional normality” requires simultaneous, sustained satisfaction of all of the following observable conditions for at least 14 consecutive days (to distinguish from transient pauses):

- Direct military activity: No exchanges of fire, missile/drone strikes, or kinetic operations by Iran, the US/coalition, Israel, or major Gulf states against each other’s military assets, territory, or energy infrastructure in the Persian Gulf, Gulf of Oman, or Strait of Hormuz littoral.
- Proxy activity: No significant Houthi, Iraqi Shia militia, or other Iran-aligned attacks on Gulf shipping, energy facilities, or US/partner bases that disrupt operations.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Commercial vessels transit without kinetic attacks, mining incidents, or coercive interdictions; AIS-visible traffic recovers to ≥70% of pre-war baseline (~90+ large commercial vessels/day); no active exclusion zones or permit regimes enforced by force.
- Commercial transit levels: Sustained daily commodity vessel transits ≥90 and oil/LNG-equivalent throughput ≥15 million barrels of oil equivalent per day (vs pre-war ~20 mb/d oil + significant LNG).
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums fall to ≤3–5× pre-war levels (from current ~40×) and become routinely available without special exclusions for most operators.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: GCC + Iran crude/condensate exports through or bypassing Hormuz recover to ≥80% of pre-war aggregate levels with predictable scheduling; Qatari LNG loadings and loadings from major Gulf terminals resume without force majeure or prolonged deferrals.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent front-month settles in a range consistent with global supply-demand balance without a persistent Gulf-risk premium >$15–20/bbl attributable to Hormuz insecurity; implied volatility normalizes.
- Force posture of major actors: US/coalition naval and air presence remains elevated but transitions from active blockade/escort/combat operations to routine presence; Iranian IRGC Navy returns to pre-war patrol patterns without minelaying or anti-shipping campaigns.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new large-scale strikes on nuclear/energy sites, no successful major attacks on capital ships or key ports, and no formal re-imposition of reciprocal blockades.

A temporary ceasefire, reduced oil prices alone, or a battlefield pause without commercial recovery does **not** meet the standard.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant historical cases (Gulf/chokepoint/maritime disruption + limited regional wars):

1. **1980s Tanker War (Iran-Iraq)**: Prolonged attacks on shipping (hundreds of incidents). Shipping adapted via escorts, flags of convenience, and higher premiums; full commercial normalization lagged formal ceasefire by many months to years. Relevance: high for asymmetric anti-shipping campaigns. Breaks down: no superpower direct combat with Iran; lower modern precision/insurance integration. Timeline signal: partial functional recovery 6–18+ months after peak intensity.
2. **2019 Gulf tanker attacks / Iranian seizure episodes**: Limited kinetic events produced insurance spikes and temporary traffic caution that normalized in weeks to a few months once attacks stopped. Relevance: shows commercial sensitivity. Breaks down: far smaller scale, no sustained war or blockade. Timeline: weeks–3 months for insurance/traffic recovery after cessation.
3. **2023–2024 Red Sea / Houthi disruption**: Sustained proxy attacks caused major rerouting, high premiums, and volume collapse. Partial recovery only after military pressure + political signals; full normalization incomplete years later for some operators. Relevance: modern insurance markets, proxy dynamics, chokepoint. Breaks down: not a direct state-on-state war closing a primary oil artery. Timeline: meaningful recovery 6–18 months after peak, incomplete at longer horizons without political resolution.
4. **1990–91 Gulf War / Kuwait liberation**: Intense combat followed by relatively rapid restoration of export infrastructure and shipping once ground war ended and mines were cleared. Relevance: Gulf energy recovery post-major combat. Breaks down: clear conventional victory and occupation of the aggressor territory; different political end-state. Timeline: infrastructure and commercial recovery measured in months after decisive military phase.
5. **Iran-Iraq War overall (1980–88) and post-war Gulf shipping**: Prolonged attrition; commercial adaptation occurred under fire, but true normality required war termination. Relevance: endurance of Iranian strategy. Breaks down: multipolar Cold War context.

**Base-rate prior (pre-case evidence)** for full functional normality under the strict definition:  
1 week ≈ 1–3%; 1 month ≈ 5–10%; 3 months ≈ 15–25%; 6 months ≈ 30–45%; 1 year ≈ 45–60%; 18 months ≈ 55–70%.  
These reflect that commercial/insurance normalization typically lags military pauses by months and that durable political settlement is rarer and slower than temporary de-escalation.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Mutual economic pain (Iranian export collapse + global oil price/volatility pressure on consumers and Gulf producers).  
2. US military superiority and ability to degrade Iranian anti-shipping and export capacity.  
3. Gulf state and Asian buyer interest in restoring reliable flows.  
4. Potential for renewed mediation (Qatar, Oman, China, others).  
5. Historical pattern that pure anti-shipping campaigns eventually face diminishing returns under sustained pressure.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Iranian strategic view of Hormuz control as core leverage and regime survival tool; refusal to return to pre-war status quo.  
2. Absence of a credible political end-game or verification mechanism after the failed June MOU.  
3. Tit-for-tat logic now extended to tanker targeting, raising risk of further escalation.  
4. Proxy networks (Houthis et al.) that can sustain disruption independently.  
5. High insurance and operator risk aversion: commercial recovery requires sustained safety, not just military claims of control.  
6. Domestic political constraints on both sides (US midterms context noted in some reporting; Iranian regime cohesion under pressure).

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Continued cycles of kinetic spikes and partial pauses. Traffic remains suppressed well below pre-war levels for several more months. Insurance stays elevated. Gradual, incomplete recovery of some GCC export volumes via escorted or alternative routes, with Iranian exports remaining constrained. Full multi-domain normality most likely only after a more durable political arrangement or decisive change in Iranian capacity/incentives, probably measured in many months rather than weeks.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
A new mediated framework (building on Qatar/Oman efforts) produces a verified multi-week stand-down, rapid mine clearance, traffic rebound above 70–80 vessels/day, and insurance compression. Iranian economic stress forces prioritization of revenue over leverage. Probability of this path materializing within 3–6 months is low but non-zero (~15–20% conditional on early signals).

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Tit-for-tat expands; Iran formalizes broader exclusion zones; proxies sustain attacks; insurance markets price Hormuz as semi-permanent high-risk; dual control/blockade regimes harden. Normalization delayed beyond 18 months. This remains a substantial tail risk given the failed June MOU and current escalation logic.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Possible overestimation of normalization at 6–18 months: Iranian doctrine treats Hormuz as existential leverage; commercial risk aversion and insurance markets may stay elevated longer than military metrics suggest; reference classes under-weight modern precision munitions and information effects. Possible underestimation: US ability to degrade Iranian maritime and export capacity could produce faster de facto commercial recovery than pure political models imply; global energy market pressure and Gulf state agency could force earlier accommodations. Load-bearing assumptions include continued Iranian regime cohesion and absence of a decisive military or internal political shift.
