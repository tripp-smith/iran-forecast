**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each of these horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months. (As of 10 October 2026.)

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality requires simultaneous, sustained (minimum ~2–4 weeks continuous) satisfaction of all the following observable conditions. A temporary ceasefire, lower oil prices alone, or a battlefield pause does not qualify.

- **Direct military activity**: No confirmed kinetic strikes by the US, Israel, or Iran against each other’s territory, forces, or major assets, and no Iranian ballistic/drone attacks on Gulf host-state infrastructure or forces.
- **Proxy activity**: Houthi and other Iran-aligned proxy attacks on Saudi, UAE, or other Gulf targets (airports, energy, shipping) and Red Sea/Bab al-Mandeb disruptions reduced to sporadic, low-intensity pre-2026 levels or under a durable local ceasefire. No systematic interference with commercial traffic.
- **Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters**: No routine Iranian attacks, mining, or permit requirements for standard commercial vessels. Transit does not depend on constant US/coalition naval escorts or special arrangements. Threat level not “severe.”
- **Commercial transit levels**: Total commercial vessel transits (AIS-visible + credible dark estimates) at ≥70–80% of pre-crisis averages (roughly 60–90+ vessels/day versus ~85–140 pre-war). Waiting fleets largely dispersed.
- **War-risk insurance conditions**: Additional war-risk premiums for hull returned to ≤0.5% of vessel value (or clearly near pre-crisis ~0.1–0.25% range), with broad underwriting capacity available without dominant government reinsurance backstops. Joint War Committee high-risk designation lifted or meaningfully narrowed.
- **Oil and LNG export continuity**: Sustained Gulf crude + products + LNG exports at ≥90% of pre-war levels (pre-war Hormuz crude/products ~17 mb/d range; LNG largely from Qatar). No force majeure on major LNG contracts. Pipeline bypasses not the primary mode.
- **Energy market stress and volatility**: Brent (or equivalent) without a large persistent Gulf-risk premium; volatility not driven by Hormuz attack fears or export outages. Refinery and port operations in the Gulf near normal utilization.
- **Force posture of major actors**: US regional troop levels and carrier presence drawn down from wartime peaks (~50–60k+ and 2–3 carriers); no active naval blockade of Iranian ports. Iranian missile/drone launch tempo minimal.
- **Absence of major new escalation triggers**: No significant new nuclear advances, large-scale proxy campaigns, or political shocks that restart high-intensity conflict.

This definition is deliberately demanding. It separates military pauses from commercial, insurance, and force-posture normalization.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot** (as of ~10 October 2026)

Multiple ceasefire/MOU attempts (April two-week pause, June 17 Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding) collapsed; Trump declared the June MOU “over” on 8 July after Iranian shipping attacks.

No major US/coalition strikes on Iranian territory ashore for weeks (31+ consecutive operational periods in some reporting). Trump has stated the US will not attack Iran before the 3 November midterms; post-midterm options are being prepared, with force reinforcements (additional ~9–10k personnel, third carrier elements) en route. Indirect talks continue via Qatar and others, but sequencing disputes persist (US blockade/sanctions lift vs. Hormuz reopening and nuclear constraints). 

Hormuz status: severely restricted. AIS-visible commercial traffic ~10/day (≈10–12% of pre-crisis ~85–140). Hundreds of ships waiting; US-facilitated southern-corridor transits exist but remain limited. Sources: JINSA updates, GlobalSecurity, hormuz.now, Kpler/Vortexa tracking, Reuters, Al Jazeera, Lloyd’s-related reporting. Disputed elements include exact transit volumes (AIS vs. dark/facilitated) and attribution of some attacks.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant historical cases:

- **1980s Tanker War (Iran-Iraq)**: Prolonged shipping attacks, mines, escorts. Partial traffic continued under protection; full commercial confidence lagged years. Suggests 6–24+ months for stabilization after intensity drops. Breaks down: less intense US involvement, different technology.
- **2019 Gulf tanker/drone incidents**: Short spikes resolved via diplomacy/deterrence within weeks–months. Suggests faster recovery possible with restraint. Breaks down: lower intensity, no full war.
- **Red Sea Houthi campaign (2023–2025)**: Sustained diversion, elevated insurance, traffic never fully normalized while active. Years for partial recovery. Highly relevant for proxy and insurance dynamics.
- **1990–91 Gulf War aftermath**: Rapid shipping recovery (weeks–months) after decisive military outcome and clear ceasefire. Suggests quicker path if decisive pause + political settlement. Breaks down: different scale and outcome.
- **Yemen civil war / Houthi-Saudi (2015–present)**: Intermittent for a decade; commercial normality never fully restored in affected zones. Long-tail risk.
- **Prior Iran nuclear/sanctions crises (2012, 2019)**: Elevated shipping and insurance risk without sustained kinetic war; recovery tracked diplomacy. Relevant for non-kinetic drag.

**Base-rate prior** (outside-in, before case-specific evidence):  
1 week: ~5%; 1 month: ~12%; 3 months: ~28%; 6 months: ~45%; 1 year: ~60%; 18 months: ~70%.  
Rationale: Maritime/commercial normalization in Gulf/chokepoint wars typically lags military pauses by months due to insurance, behavior, and residual proxy risk. Decisive short wars recover faster; protracted or proxy-heavy ones slower. Long-horizon priors rise but never approach certainty given historical recurrence.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

1. Mutual interest in avoiding full escalation (US midterm politics, Iranian regime resilience costs, Gulf states’ energy revenue).  
2. Partial technical ability to move oil via escorts and pipelines (already demonstrated).  
3. Ongoing (if stalled) diplomatic channels and prior MOU frameworks.  
4. High costs of prolonged disruption (insurance losses, Asian buyer shifts, Iranian export constraints).  
5. Historical pattern that intensity can drop after months of attrition.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

1. Active shipping attacks and residual Iranian capability/intent to contest Hormuz.  
2. Insurance market stickiness and behavioral lag (owners/insurers wait for sustained proof).  
3. Proxy escalation (Houthis) and dual chokepoint risk (Hormuz + Red Sea).  
4. US force posture and post-midterm strike options remaining live.  
5. Sequencing and nuclear/trust gaps blocking durable deal.  
6. Dark traffic and ambiguity prevent clean “open” signal.  

Ranked by importance: (1) sustained military restraint, (2) insurance + traffic recovery, (3) proxy restraint.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Intermittent low-intensity conflict continues through and beyond the US midterms. Limited oil flows persist via escorts and bypasses while AIS-visible traffic and insurance stay constrained. A fragile, incomplete arrangement emerges in the 3–9 month window under economic and political pressure, allowing partial traffic recovery but not full insurance or force normalization. Proxies remain a residual risk. Full functional normality (per definition) is more likely in the 12–18 month range than sooner, and even then not guaranteed.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Post-midterm political incentive for Trump to declare victory via a limited deal; Iranian economic exhaustion forces acceptance of sequencing that reopens Hormuz quickly; insurers respond rapidly to a verified multi-week quiet period; Houthis are constrained by Saudi/US pressure. This could push 3-month probability toward 40% and 6-month toward 55–60%. Requires simultaneous restraint from multiple actors that has not been sustained so far.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Post-midterm strikes restart high-intensity phase; Iran maintains shipping harassment as leverage; Houthi campaign expands; insurance market stays withdrawn for 6–12+ months; force posture remains elevated. This keeps 6-month probability near 15–20% and 1-year near 30–35%. Supported by historical proxy persistence and current force movements.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Strongest case I am overestimating normalization: Insurance and commercial behavior are stickier than military pauses; current partial oil recovery may mask deeper LNG and confidence problems; proxy dynamics (Houthis) have independent momentum. Strongest case I am underestimating: Midterm calendar plus mutual costs could produce a quicker, more durable pause than reference classes from pure maritime wars suggest; tracking data already show adaptive oil flows. Load-bearing assumptions: that insurance lags military reality by months, and that no decisive political breakthrough occurs before 6 months. Evidence that would prove the forecast wrong includes a verified multi-week total attack halt plus rapid premium collapse and traffic rebound above 60% within 4–6 weeks.
