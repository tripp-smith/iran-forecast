**1. Forecast Question**  
Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each horizon: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
Functional normality requires simultaneous, sustained (minimum 2–4 weeks of observed stability) satisfaction of the following observable thresholds:  
- Direct military activity: No significant kinetic exchanges (missile, drone, or airstrike campaigns by US/Israel or Iran targeting each other or Gulf states) for the prior 14+ days; residual low-level probing below 2019–2020 incident levels.  
- Proxy activity: Houthi and other Iranian-aligned proxy attacks on shipping or regional targets at or below pre-February 2026 baseline (near-zero sustained Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb disruption).  
- Shipping security (Hormuz and approaches): UKMTO/JMIC threat level “moderate” or lower; no confirmed attacks, mining, or seizure incidents for 14+ days; free transit without mandatory Iranian permits or US convoy dependency as the dominant mode.  
- Commercial transit levels: AIS-visible commercial crossings averaging ≥70% of pre-crisis baseline (~60+ vessels/day versus ~85).  
- War-risk insurance: Additional premiums ≤1% of hull value (near pre-war 0.15–0.25% range) with broad market availability and no widespread cancellations or 7-day only cover.  
- Oil/LNG export continuity: Gulf crude + products + LNG flows through Hormuz and alternative routes at ≥90% of pre-war volumes on a sustained basis; Iranian exports not systematically blockaded.  
- Energy market stress: Brent volatility and risk premium consistent with non-crisis geopolitical baselines (no sustained >$10–15/bbl war premium; TTF/JKM no longer elevated 100%+).  
- Force posture: US carrier/surface presence reduced to pre-crisis peacetime levels (≤1 CSG routinely); Iranian IRGC naval posture defensive rather than offensive interdiction.  
- No major new escalation triggers: Absence of large-scale strikes, nuclear red-line crossings, or new proxy campaigns that would re-trigger the above.  

A temporary ceasefire, oil-price decline, or US-facilitated low-volume transit does **not** meet the definition. Commercial and insurance normalization are gating.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot (as of ~19 November 2026, ~Day 265)**  
The war began 28 February 2026 with US-Israeli strikes. An April ceasefire and June MOU collapsed; intermittent exchanges resumed. Current state is a fragile “no war, no peace” with ongoing limited kinetic activity: Iran has struck targets in Bahrain and Kuwait; US has struck Qeshm Island and other sites in response to shipping attacks. Diplomacy (Qatar/Oman-mediated) continues but is stalled over Iranian demands (lift blockade/sanctions, asset release, nuclear sequencing) versus US insistence on verifiable nuclear constraints.  

Hormuz is severely restricted: AIS commercial traffic ~5–6 vessels/day (~6% of ~85 pre-crisis baseline); hundreds of ships waiting; threat level SEVERE; ongoing attacks, claimed mining, and US-facilitated “dark” or coordinated transits. US naval blockade of Iranian ports remains active. War-risk premiums reported in the 6–10% of hull value range (tens of millions per VLCC transit in peak quotes). Gulf oil exports (ex-Iran) have partially recovered via Hormuz and bypasses but remain well below pre-war and recently volatile; Iranian crude exports near zero under blockade. Brent ~$92 (pre-crisis ~$72). Two US carrier strike groups remain in the region supporting the blockade and limited strikes. Proxies (Houthis) retain Red Sea capacity. Cumulative oil/gas supply losses are described by the IEA as the largest ever disruption to global trade.  

Facts confirmed by multiple maritime trackers (AIS, Windward, Kpler/Vortexa reporting), CENTCOM statements, insurer/broker commentary, and major wires. Some Iranian claims on specific incidents and exact transit facilitation volumes remain disputed or inflated.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
- **Tanker War (1984–88)**: Sustained shipping attacks, mining, and US escort (Earnest Will). Relevant because of IRGC tactics, Hormuz geography, and insurance spikes. Breakdown: embedded in broader Iran-Iraq land war that ended in 1988; shipping never fully halted and recovered post-ceasefire within months. Suggests partial commercial stabilization 3–6 months after major kinetic pause; full normality only after political settlement.  
- **2019–2020 Hormuz tanker incidents**: Limited attacks, brief insurance spikes, rapid traffic recovery. Relevant for short-duration maritime coercion. Breakdown: far lower intensity and no blockade. Suggests recovery in weeks to 2 months once incidents stop.  
- **Houthi Red Sea campaign (2023–ongoing)**: Persistent chokepoint disruption, high insurance, diversion, incomplete commercial normalization >12–18 months. Relevant for proxy persistence and insurer memory. Breakdown: lower volume impact than Hormuz. Suggests multi-month to year+ lag even after partial threat reduction.  
- **1991 Gulf War**: Brief major disruption, rapid military and commercial recovery post-ceasefire (days–weeks military, 1–3 months commercial). Relevant for US-led outcome. Breakdown: decisive conventional defeat and rapid political resolution.  
- **2011–12 Iranian sanctions/threats**: Elevated risk without sustained closure. Relevant for economic pressure baseline. Breakdown: no active war. Suggests limited lasting commercial damage if kinetic activity ceases.  

**Base-rate prior** (pre-current-evidence adjustment, weighted toward Tanker War + Houthi persistence given unresolved political core):  
1 week: ~5%; 1 month: ~15%; 3 months: ~30%; 6 months: ~45%; 1 year: ~65%; 18 months: ~75%.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**  
- Mutual exhaustion and high cost of sustained high-intensity war (neither side seeking full re-escalation).  
- US interest in reducing carrier commitment and oil-price pressure.  
- Gulf states’ strong preference for commercial recovery.  
- Mediator channels (Qatar/Oman) remain open.  
Ranked: political will for de-escalation > commercial incentives > force-posture costs.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**  
- Unresolved nuclear red lines and Iranian conditions for Hormuz reopening.  
- Active US blockade and Iranian residual strike capacity creating action-reaction cycles.  
- Insurer and shipper memory (premiums and risk aversion lag kinetic pauses).  
- Proxy residual capacity and infrastructure damage.  
- Elevated force posture.  
Ranked: political/nuclear deadlock > commercial/insurance friction > residual military capability.

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Continued intermittent kinetic activity and blockade for weeks to months, followed by a fragile de-escalation that allows partial traffic recovery (30–60% levels) under residual high insurance and US facilitation. Full multi-domain normality remains beyond the 6-month horizon in the modal case and is only modestly likely by 12–18 months.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
A rapid political breakthrough (nuclear constraints traded for blockade/sanctions relief) within 4–8 weeks produces a sustained pause. Traffic and insurance normalize on a 2–4 month lag. Probability of this path is low (~15–20% by 6 months) given current deadlock.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Frozen conflict with periodic spikes, persistent blockade, and insurer aversion keeps traffic and premiums far from baseline for 12+ months. Probability ~25–30% through 18 months.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Strongest case against this forecast: mutual exhaustion plus commercial pressure could produce a faster de-escalation than Tanker War analogies imply, and partial US-facilitated traffic already shows resilience. Strongest case for lower probabilities: nuclear red lines and Iranian leverage via Hormuz make durable settlement harder than historical cases; insurance and shipper behavior create longer lags than military metrics suggest. Load-bearing assumptions: that commercial/insurance thresholds are truly gating and that current diplomatic channels do not produce a sudden breakthrough.
