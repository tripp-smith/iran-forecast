**1. Forecast Question**  
Probability that the Persian Gulf returns to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months (from 23 August 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
Measurable, multi-domain standard (all elements must hold concurrently for a sustained period of at least 14 consecutive days, verifiable by public data):

- Direct military activity: No Iranian attacks on commercial vessels or Gulf Arab energy infrastructure; no US/Israeli strikes inside Iran or on Iranian coastal/maritime assets linked to Hormuz enforcement; no reciprocal major exchanges.
- Proxy activity: No significant Houthi, Iraqi militia, or other Axis residual attacks on Gulf shipping, energy facilities, or US/partner bases tied to the Hormuz dispute.
- Shipping security (Strait of Hormuz and approaches): Safe transit on both northern and southern routes without boarding, seizure, drone/missile strikes, or mandatory Iranian permits/fees enforced by force. UKMTO/JMIC risk level no higher than moderate; no active mine threat requiring clearance operations.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits (all types, AIS + inferred) averaging ≥80% of pre-war baseline (~100–110+ per day sustained; pre-war ~130). Liquid cargoes (oil/LNG/products) recovering to ≥15–18 mb/d through Hormuz or equivalent effective Gulf export.
- War-risk insurance: Hull war-risk premiums ≤1% of hull value (ideally approaching pre-war ~0.25%); broad availability from major markets without capacity withdrawal or heavy government backstops; JWC listed-area status relaxed or rates reflecting low residual risk.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf producers (Saudi, UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Iraq, Iran under any residual sanctions regime) able to meet contractual volumes without force majeure declarations driven by Hormuz risk; Qatar LNG and major crude terminals operating near pre-war utilization.
- Energy market stress/volatility: Brent in a range consistent with non-crisis fundamentals (not structurally elevated solely by Hormuz risk premium); reduced day-to-day geopolitical risk premium; no recurrent spikes >10–15% driven by Hormuz incidents.
- Force posture: Meaningful de-escalation—US naval presence reduced from wartime blockade/escort intensity; Iranian coastal/missile/drone posture not in continuous high alert for strait interdiction; no permanent Iranian “Persian Gulf Strait Authority” fee/permit regime accepted as the new status quo by major shippers/insurers/Gulf states.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new leadership assassination, nuclear breakout crisis, major proxy war expansion, or third-party (e.g., China/Russia) military intervention that reopens kinetic exchange.

A temporary ceasefire, partial Omani-corridor traffic under escort, lower oil prices from demand destruction or SPR releases, or a battlefield pause do **not** qualify. Commercial normalization lags military pauses.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant classes (with breakdowns):

1. **Tanker War (1980–1988 Iran-Iraq)**: Prolonged attacks on Gulf shipping; traffic never fully stopped but risk elevated for years. Partial commercial adaptation occurred; full risk normalization lagged ceasefire (1988) by many months to years. Suggests multi-year residual risk for insurance/shipping behavior. Breaks down: full-scale state war vs. today’s asymmetric + great-power involvement; less precise modern tracking/insurance markets.

2. **2019–2020 Gulf tanker attacks / Iranian seizures (and related)**: Discrete Iranian interdictions produced short spikes in premiums and temporary traffic caution; rapid partial recovery once attacks paused, but elevated baseline risk persisted. Timelines for partial normalization: weeks to a few months after last incident. Breaks down: limited scale vs. current multi-month near-closure + blockade.

3. **Red Sea / Houthi disruption (2023–ongoing into 2026 context)**: Significant traffic diversion around Cape; insurance spikes; partial recovery only with sustained security (escorts, strikes) and reduced attack tempo. Full commercial return incomplete even after major reductions in attacks. Suggests 6–18+ months for substantial normalization after kinetic peak. Breaks down: single chokepoint alternative exists (Cape); Hormuz has fewer viable large-volume alternatives.

4. **Suez closures / major chokepoint disruptions (1956, 1967–75)**: Full closures produced multi-month to multi-year commercial adaptations and elevated costs; reopening did not instantly restore pre-crisis volumes or insurance. Partial recovery in months; full in 1–3 years. Breaks down: different geopolitics and vessel technology.

5. **Post-ceasefire maritime recoveries in modern conflicts (e.g., aspects of Black Sea grain corridor, limited Gulf precedents)**: Political deals can produce rapid traffic rebounds (weeks), but insurance and force-posture normalization lag (months). Breaks down: grain corridor was narrower and externally guaranteed.

Base-rate prior (pre-current-evidence adjustment, approximate cumulative probability of meeting the full multi-domain definition):  
- 1 week: <5%  
- 1 month: 5–10%  
- 3 months: 15–25%  
- 6 months: 30–40%  
- 1 year: 45–55%  
- 18 months: 55–65%  

These reflect historical lag between kinetic pause and commercial/insurance/force-posture normalization, plus the difficulty of restoring trust in a critical chokepoint after prolonged disruption and competing sovereignty claims.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Durable political settlement on Hormuz administration (no unilateral Iranian fee/permit regime accepted by major users; US blockade lifted).  
2. Sustained absence of attacks (kinetic pause long enough for insurers and charterers to reprice).  
3. US-Iran economic/nuclear understandings that reduce Iranian incentive to use the strait as leverage.  
4. Effective, trusted multi-national or Omani-led transit arrangements with credible security guarantees.  
5. Market adaptation (alternative routes, STS, inventory draws) reducing immediate pressure while politics catch up.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Iranian insistence on formalized control/sovereignty over Hormuz as core post-war leverage and deterrence.  
2. US refusal to accept any Iranian administrative role or fees; continued blockade and economic pressure.  
3. Residual military capability on both sides enabling rapid re-escalation; incomplete destruction of Iranian anti-ship/mine/drone capacity.  
4. Insurance market structural caution and capacity limits after prolonged high claims risk.  
5. Proxy activation risk and Gulf state hedging behavior that keeps force postures elevated.  
6. Domestic Iranian politics (hardline IRGC elements vs. pragmatists) and US political incentives.

**9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon**

**1 Week**  
- Probability: 3%  
- Base-rate anchor: <5%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight downward (ongoing low traffic, high premiums, no deal, recent incidents).  
- Core rationale: No credible path to multi-domain recovery in days; even a sudden ceasefire would leave insurance, traffic, and posture lagging.  
- Main gating factor: Insurance and charterer confidence.  
- What would raise this forecast materially: Immediate verified comprehensive ceasefire + free transit announcement with major insurer rate cuts.  
- What would lower this forecast materially: New major vessel attack or strike exchange.  
- Confidence in this estimate: High  

**1 Month**  
- Probability: 8%  
- Base-rate anchor: 5–10%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Neutral to slight downward.  
- Core rationale: Possible kinetic pause via mediation, but commercial/insurance lag and competing sovereignty claims make full normality improbable.  
- Main gating factor: Verifiable free high-volume transit without Iranian permits enforced by force.  
- What would raise: Signed durable Hormuz arrangement + sustained zero attacks + rapid premium collapse.  
- What would lower: Breakdown of talks + renewed interdictions.  
- Confidence: Medium-High  

**3 Months**  
- Probability: 20%  
- Base-rate anchor: 15–25%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight downward (MoU failure history, entrenched positions).  
- Core rationale: Window for a political deal exists, but historical chokepoint recoveries and current Iranian leverage incentives point to incomplete recovery.  
- Main gating factor: Political settlement on administration of the strait.  
- What would raise: Comprehensive US-Iran package including sanctions relief and verified free navigation.  
- What would lower: Entrenchment of dual corridors with ongoing friction or major proxy activation.  
- Confidence: Medium  

**6 Months**  
- Probability: 35%  
- Base-rate anchor: 30–40%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Neutral.  
- Core rationale: Time allows for deal-making under economic pressure (Iran) and market costs (all parties), but residual risk and posture issues likely persist.  
- Main gating factor: Insurance market repricing after sustained calm.  
- What would raise: Sustained 60+ day attack-free period + traffic recovery above 50–60% of baseline + premium compression.  
- What would lower: Cycle of partial deals collapsing into renewed low-level conflict.  
- Confidence: Medium  

**1 Year**  
- Probability: 50%  
- Base-rate anchor: 45–55%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Neutral.  
- Core rationale: Base rates from prolonged disruptions support roughly even odds of functional (not perfect) recovery; second-order effects (adaptation, fatigue, external mediation) accumulate. Long-horizon uncertainty is high.  
- Main gating factor: Whether Iranian control claims become a durable new equilibrium or are rolled back.  
- What would raise: Stable post-settlement security architecture with Gulf state buy-in.  
- What would lower: Frozen conflict with permanent dual-track (US-escorted vs. Iranian-permitted) traffic and elevated risk premium.  
- Confidence: Medium-Low  

**18 Months**  
- Probability: 60%  
- Base-rate anchor: 55–65%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Neutral to slight downward (risk of new equilibrium of managed insecurity).  
- Core rationale: Most reference classes show substantial recovery within 1–2 years of major kinetic peak, but full multi-domain normality (especially insurance and force posture) can lag further if sovereignty disputes remain unresolved. Avoid overconfidence.  
- Main gating factor: Absence of renewed major escalation and gradual market re-entry.  
- What would raise: Verified long-term free-navigation regime + major capacity return by insurers.  
- What would lower: Institutionalization of Iranian administrative role or recurrent crises.  
- Confidence: Low-Medium  

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Intermittent low-level kinetic activity and contested dual-route traffic (US-supported southern/Omani corridor under escort vs. Iranian northern/permit regime) continues for months. Partial traffic recovery occurs under security umbrellas and STS adaptations, but insurance remains elevated and full commercial volumes lag. A limited political arrangement (possibly via Oman) produces a fragile pause by 3–6 months; functional normality (per definition) is reached only later, if at all, as economic costs and external pressure mount. Residual elevated risk premium persists into 2027.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Rapid comprehensive deal (sanctions relief + verified free transit + mutual force restraint) in coming weeks, followed by 30–60 days of zero incidents. Insurers rapidly reprice, traffic surges to clear backlogs, and Gulf producers restore full exports. Probability of this path is low given current positions but would move near-term forecasts sharply higher.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
No durable deal; Iran consolidates de-facto administrative control with selective enforcement; US maintains blockade and escorts; insurance capacity stays constrained; proxies remain active. Gulf becomes a permanently higher-risk theater with structural oil/LNG risk premium and diverted trade. This is a material tail.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Strongest case I am overestimating normalization: Iranian domestic politics and the post-Khamenei regime’s need for external leverage make any genuine rollback of Hormuz claims unlikely; the MoU collapse already demonstrated fragility. Historical tanker-war and Red Sea experience shows commercial memory is long. Strongest case I am underestimating: Extreme economic pressure on Iran + US domestic desire to lower energy prices + Chinese interest in stable energy flows could force a pragmatic deal faster than base rates suggest. Load-bearing assumptions include continued Iranian residual anti-ship capability and insurer risk aversion. Evidence that would prove the forecast wrong: sudden verified free high-volume transit + premium collapse below 1% sustained for weeks, or conversely a major new kinetic spiral or formal Iranian toll regime accepted by markets.
