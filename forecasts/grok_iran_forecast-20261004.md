I will ground the forecast in current reporting on the 2026 Iran conflict, Hormuz traffic, war-risk insurance, and energy flows before setting base rates and horizon probabilities.The Persian Gulf is not functionally normal on 4 October 2026, day 217–219 of the Hormuz crisis. A fragile June memorandum, partial crude recovery, and intermittent stand-downs have not restored open commercial navigation.

## 1. Forecast Question

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each horizon: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months. Horizons are measured from 4 October 2026.

## 2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”

All of the following must hold at the same time, and have held for at least 21 consecutive days (so a weekend pause or a single escorted convoy does not count):

- Direct military activity: no US–Iran or Iran–Gulf-state kinetic exchanges against military or commercial targets in the Gulf, Strait of Hormuz, or Gulf of Oman, and no active blockade of Iranian or other Gulf ports.
- Proxy activity: Houthi and other Iran-linked attacks on Gulf energy, ports, or shipping at or below pre-February 2026 sporadic levels, not wartime campaign intensity.
- Shipping security: the traffic separation scheme in routine commercial use; no permit, toll, or selective-authorization regime; residual mine risk assessed by UKMTO/JMIC as low rather than severe; naval escort optional rather than the operating system.
- Commercial transit: AIS-visible commercial crossings averaging at least 60 per day, against a pre-crisis baseline of about 85 (roughly 70 percent). Current run-rate is about 6 per day.
- War-risk insurance: standard hull war-risk additional premium at or below about 0.5 percent of hull value on renewable terms, not crisis single-voyage pricing in the several-percent or reported ~10–40× band, and available from ordinary market capacity without a government backstop as the binding constraint.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf crude, products, and LNG exports by sea near pre-war volumes without dependence on a wartime escort or bypass-only system. Products matter as much as crude.
- Energy-market stress: the war-risk premium in Brent largely unwound and realized volatility back toward pre-crisis ranges. A price level alone does not qualify. Brent near $100–102 still embeds a large risk premium.
- Force posture: US and Iranian force postures reduced from wartime surge and blockade footing; no standing threat of immediate re-closure as the price of talks.
- Escalation triggers: no new major trigger in the prior 21 days (tanker strike campaign, mine reseeding, missile/drone salvo on Gulf hosts, or collapse of the diplomatic channel into renewed general hostilities).

A ceasefire, a lower oil price, or a battlefield pause is not normality. Partial crude flow under escort is not normality.

## 3. Current Situation Snapshot

The crisis dates from about 28 February 2026. As of 4 October, live AIS trackers put commercial crossings at about 5–6 per day over the past week, roughly 7 percent of the pre-crisis 85, with hundreds of commercial ships stationary on both sides of the strait. Iran says the strait stays shut until seven conditions in the June Islamabad memorandum are met, including sanctions relief and a stop to hostilities. The United States maintains a blockade of Iranian ports and has rejected Tehran’s seven-day reopening proposal. Talks continue through Qatar; a full October reopening is widely judged unlikely.

Kinetic activity has not stopped. Over the weekend of 3–4 October, US forces struck Iranian tankers and Iranian forces struck tankers and US vessels; traffic fell to the lowest since May. Mines have been reported cleared from the main traffic scheme, but maritime authorities still warn of drifting mines and attack risk, and shipowner bodies say clearance alone will not restore traffic. War-risk cover is available only on tight, often single-voyage terms at multiples of peacetime rates.

Oil is the partial exception. September tracking put Hormuz crude movements and bypass-pipeline flows together at a large fraction of pre-war regional exports — estimates range from roughly 7–10 million b/d through the strait plus several million via bypass, with some analysts near 80 percent of regional crude exports. Refined-product exports remain deeply depressed. Brent is about $100–102, up sharply on the year. In Yemen, the Houthis have retaken the Red Sea coast and Bab al-Mandab and are striking Saudi targets, including a claimed hit on an Aramco facility near Riyadh this weekend. That is a second chokepoint and a live proxy front.

Confirmed versus disputed: the low AIS transit count, the blockade, the rejected seven-day plan, weekend tanker strikes, and elevated insurance are well supported. Exact barrel counts differ by tracker and are partly inferred. Iranian claims of lawful control and US claims of a cleared, usable strait are both political; the commercial outcome is the better measure.

## 4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior

- Tanker War, 1984–88. Relevant because it is the closest Gulf shipping-war case: attacks, escorts, high war-risk premia, and continued but distorted trade. It breaks down because it was attrition inside an existing war, not a US–Iran state war with a port blockade and a nuclear file. Shipping stabilized within weeks to a few months after the 1988 ceasefire once attacks stopped, but political normality took longer. Suggests commercial function can return in 1–3 months after a real stop to attacks, not before.
- 2019 Gulf attacks and Abqaiq. Relevant as a short shock with insurance and freight spikes and no general war. Breaks down because there was no closure, no mines campaign, and no blockade. Partial normalization in weeks. Suggests the fast tail if this war ends cleanly, which it has not.
- Red Sea and Bab al-Mandab, late 2023–2025. Relevant for insurer and liner behavior: diversions persisted long after individual lulls, and carriers did not treat a pause as a reopening. Breaks down because volumes were smaller than Hormuz and alternative routing existed. Suggests 6–18 months of sub-normal traffic and premia even after intensity falls.
- Black Sea grain corridor, 2022–23. Relevant for mines, contested control, and insurance lag behind military pauses. Breaks down because it was a negotiated corridor for one commodity. Suggests repeated false reopenings and multi-month lags.
- 1990–91 Gulf War. Relevant for Gulf host-state risk and energy-price overshoot. Breaks down because Hormuz itself stayed open. Suggests the strait can function in a regional war if the local threat to shipping is removed — a condition not met here.
- Hormuz crisis to date, March–October 2026. The internal reference: a June memorandum produced a brief traffic spike (about 50 transits on 24 June in one series) and then relapse to low single digits by August–October. Suggests political announcements move traffic for days, not quarters.

Base-rate prior before case-specific update, for the demanding definition above:

- 1 week: 3 percent
- 1 month: 8 percent
- 3 months: 15 percent
- 6 months: 28 percent
- 1 year: 42 percent
- 18 months: 55 percent

The prior already assumes most chokepoint wars do not normalize inside a month, and that insurance and carrier behavior lag the last attack.

## 6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization

1. Both sides still have a diplomatic channel and a written MOU, so a bargain is imaginable without a change of regime.
2. Partial crude recovery shows escorts, bypass pipelines, and risk-tolerant owners can move barrels. That reduces the economic pressure to escalate purely to restore supply, and it is a platform for a later volume rebound.
3. Reported mine clearance in the traffic scheme removes one physical barrier if the political regime changes.
4. US electoral timing and Gulf fiscal pressure create incentives to bank a visible reopening if Iran will trade it for sanctions or blockade relief.
5. Insurers have not permanently exited. Cover exists at a price, so the market can reprice within weeks of a real quiet period, as it did after 1988 and after shorter 2019 shocks.

## 7. Key Drivers Against Normalization

1. The political price of reopening is unmet: Iran wants sanctions and blockade relief before full opening; the US wants opening and nuclear constraints without paying that price. That deadlock is the main gate.
2. Violence is still being used as leverage. Weekend tanker strikes show neither side has retired the shipping weapon.
3. Insurance and liner behavior lag. Red Sea experience says carriers and underwriters will not treat the next announcement as durable until there are quiet weeks and renewable, cheap cover.
4. A second front is active. Houthi control of Bab al-Mandab and strikes on Saudi energy keep Gulf risk high even if Hormuz talk progresses.
5. Force posture is wartime: blockade, coastal strike capacity, and a permit claim. Drawdown is a political act, not a technical one.
6. Internal reference class: the June reopening failed within days to weeks. Base rates must be conditioned on that relapse.

Ranked: political deadlock and continued use of shipping attacks outrank mines, price, and even crude-volume recovery.

The joint event is closer to the minimum of the gating items than to the average, with a small positive dependence: a real political deal moves several gates together.

## 9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon

### 1 week
- Probability: 1%
- Base-rate anchor: 3%
- Current-evidence adjustment: down, because weekend strikes, an explicit Iranian closure condition, and a rejected plan make a 21-day normal state impossible inside seven days.
- Core rationale: normality requires three quiet weeks already in the bank. The system is in a live exchange.
- Main gating factor: ongoing kinetic use of the strait.
- What would raise this forecast materially: nothing plausible inside seven days can meet the 21-day test.
- What would lower this forecast materially: a wider strike on Gulf hosts or ports.
- Confidence in this estimate: High

### 1 month
- Probability: 4%
- Base-rate anchor: 8%
- Current-evidence adjustment: down. Talks are alive, but October reopening is the low-probability branch, and insurance cannot reprice to peacetime inside a month after fresh tanker attacks.
- Core rationale: a deal could be announced. Functional normality also needs traffic, cover, and proxy quiet. The June episode showed announcement is not system reset.
- Main gating factor: political terms on blockade and sanctions.
- What would raise this forecast materially: a signed implementation deal, verified free transit above 40 per day, and no attacks for two weeks.
- What would lower this forecast materially: another tanker-strike cycle or a Houthi campaign against Saudi export terminals.
- Confidence in this estimate: High

### 3 months
- Probability: 12%
- Base-rate anchor: 15%
- Current-evidence adjustment: slight down. Mine clearance and partial crude flow help the physical side; deadlock, Yemen, and insurer memory hurt the commercial side.
- Core rationale: this is the first horizon where a late-2026 bargain could, if it stuck, produce a 21-day normal window by early January. Most paths still look like managed restriction.
- Main gating factor: conversion of talks into a durable opening, not another spike-and-relapse.
- What would raise this forecast materially: blockade eased, permit regime dropped, war-risk under 1 percent, transits sustainably above 40 per day.
- What would lower this forecast materially: collapse of the Qatar channel or reseeding of mines.
- Confidence in this estimate: Medium

### 6 months
- Probability: 22%
- Base-rate anchor: 28%
- Current-evidence adjustment: down. Half a year is enough for insurance to heal after a real stop, as in 1988, but the stop is not the modal path, and the Houthi front can keep Gulf premia elevated even if Hormuz is quieter.
- Core rationale: by April 2027 a deal, fatigue, or a tacit transit arrangement could have compounded. The internal base rate of failed reopenings keeps this well below even odds.
- Main gating factor: whether attacks on shipping actually stop for a full quarter.
- What would raise this forecast materially: 60-plus daily transits and renewable war cover near historical Gulf rates.
- What would lower this forecast materially: a return to general US–Iran strikes or a successful sustained attack on Saudi loading ports.
- Confidence in this estimate: Medium

### 1 year
- Probability: 38%
- Base-rate anchor: 42%
- Current-evidence adjustment: slight down. A year is long enough for a political settlement or a stable armed management that markets learn to live with. It is not long enough to assume the nuclear file, sanctions, and Yemen are resolved.
- Core rationale: the probability mass is “restrictive peace or managed corridor becomes predictable,” not “pre-2026 status quo.” Some of those outcomes still fail the definition if escorts and punitive premia remain.
- Main gating factor: replacement of wartime control arrangements by routine commercial rules.
- What would raise this forecast materially: sanctions-for-opening bargain implemented and proxy front de-escalated.
- What would lower this forecast materially: regime instability in Iran, or a decision in Washington or Tehran that closure is a permanent bargaining chip.
- Confidence in this estimate: Low-medium

### 18 months
- Probability: 52%
- Base-rate anchor: 55%
- Current-evidence adjustment: slight down. Eighteen months clears the modal length of an unresolved chokepoint crisis only if neither side still gains from keeping the strait as a weapon. That is plausible, not assured.
- Core rationale: above even odds only at this horizon, and only barely, because time allows deal, fatigue, or adaptation. The definition stays demanding: escorted crude at pre-war barrels with expensive cover would still fail.
- Main gating factor: political settlement sufficient for insurers and liners, not merely for state oil companies under escort.
- What would raise this forecast materially: a year of transit above 70 percent of baseline and war-risk inside normal Gulf bounds.
- What would lower this forecast materially: institutionalized dual control or a frozen conflict that leaves premia and permits in place.
- Confidence in this estimate: Low

Long-range figures stay near 50 percent rather than above 80 percent because the operational test includes insurance, proxies, and force posture, not just the absence of missiles on a given day.

## 10. Most Likely Path

Through early 2027 the modal path is a controlled, low-throughput strait: intermittent talks, periodic strikes used as leverage, US blockade intact, selective or escorted movements, crude partially flowing by escort and bypass, products and ordinary commercial shipping lagging, war-risk remaining a transaction-by-transaction decision. Yemen stays hot enough to matter for Saudi risk. A brittle implementation deal is more likely than either a clean pre-war restoration or a total multi-year closure. Functional normality, as defined, arrives only if that deal survives long enough for underwriters and liners to treat it as routine — a step beyond the signature.

## 11. Faster Normalization Case

The stronger upside case is a narrow bargain in which Washington eases the port blockade and offers limited, reversible sanctions relief, Iran drops the permit regime and stops tanker attacks, and Gulf states plus the US keep a quiet escort only as backstop. Insurers reprice within weeks of a verified quiet period, as they did after the 1988 ceasefire. Crude and then products rebound. Houthis are persuaded or pressured to pause Saudi strikes. That path can put the system inside the definition by roughly the six-month horizon. It requires both capitals to prefer a partial deal to leverage, which weekend behavior argues against but does not rule out.

## 12. Prolonged Instability Case

The stronger downside case is a frozen weaponization: Iran keeps a claimed veto and the option to reseed mines or hit unauthorized tankers; the US keeps the blockade; each spike in talks is followed by a relapse, as in June. Houthis hold Bab al-Mandab and periodically hit Saudi energy. Insurers never leave crisis pricing. By 18 months the Gulf has a stable abnormal equilibrium — some barrels moving, normality failed. A wider strike cycle that hits Gulf ports or desalination and energy nodes would push even the 18-month probability well down.

## 13. Red-Team Critique

The forecast may understate normalization if AIS undercounts dark, state-directed, or escorted traffic and if markets will accept escort-dependent crude flow as “functional” faster than the definition allows. It may also underweight US electoral incentive to force a visible reopening before late 2026.

It may overstate normalization if the June relapse is the true regime, if Iranian domestic politics reward keeping the strait shut, and if the Houthi front is now independent enough to hold Gulf risk premia up even after a US–Iran paper deal. The 18-month figure near 50 percent is the softest number: it assumes fatigue and bargaining eventually dominate, which is a prior, not a finding. The load-bearing assumptions are that neither side wants a full return to general war, and that insurance follows a real 30–60 day quiet period rather than a political narrative.

What would prove this view wrong: sustained transits above 60 per day and war-risk under 0.5 percent inside 90 days (upside miss), or a new general strike campaign plus mine reseeding that keeps transits under 15 per day through mid-2027 (downside miss).

## 14. Critical Signals to Monitor

- Daily AIS and PortWatch commercial transits, not official “facilitated transit” counts alone.
- Whether new voyages are permit-based, escorted, or ordinary TSS traffic.
- Hull war-risk quotes as a percent of value, and whether cover returns to time policies.
- Confirmed attacks on merchant ships and mine reports from UKMTO/JMIC, separated from belligerent claims.
- Status of the US port blockade and of Iranian oil departures past enforcement.
- Product exports versus crude exports.
- Houthi attacks on Saudi loading ports and Red Sea traffic.
- Any implementation text that ties opening to sanctions or nuclear steps, and whether it survives 21 days.
