**1. Forecast Question**  
Probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War, by horizons of 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months (from 9 September 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
Observable, multi-domain conditions that must hold simultaneously for a sustained period (not a single day or week of quiet):

- Direct military activity: No sustained US/coalition–Iran or Israel–Iran exchanges of fire involving Persian Gulf waters, Iranian territory adjacent to the Gulf, or major Gulf energy infrastructure; no major Iranian ballistic/missile/drone salvos at Gulf states or US forces in the theater.
- Proxy activity: Material reduction or cessation of Houthi (and other IRGC-linked) attacks on Saudi/UAE energy infrastructure and shipping that are clearly linked to the Iran conflict; no significant new proxy activation that re-escalates Gulf risk.
- Shipping security (Strait of Hormuz + nearby waters): Commercial vessels transit freely without routine Iranian interdiction, mine threats, or US-escorted “facilitated” corridors as the dominant mode; incident rate (attacks, boardings, near-misses) near pre-war baseline.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits through Hormuz recover to ≥70–80% of pre-February 2026 averages (pre-war ~100–135+ vessels/day depending on metric; commodity/oil/LNG flows correspondingly restored).
- War-risk insurance: Hull and cargo war-risk premiums for Hormuz/Persian Gulf return to within ~1–2× pre-war levels (pre-war typically 0.1–0.25% of hull value range); widespread availability without Iranian “mandatory insurance” coercion schemes or mass cancellations; Joint War Committee listed-area status materially eased.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf (ex-Iran under blockade) oil/LNG loadings and loadings via Hormuz approach pre-war volumes; Iranian shadow-fleet/export constraints do not dominate global balances; no sustained large-scale disruption to Saudi/UAE/Qatar export terminals.
- Energy market stress: Brent (and regional markers) volatility and risk premia consistent with ordinary geopolitical noise rather than active Hormuz closure risk; no persistent $20–40+ war premium relative to fundamentals.
- Force posture: US/coalition naval and air posture in the Gulf de-escalates from wartime blockade/enforcement levels toward routine presence; Iranian naval/IRGC posture no longer enforces exclusion or restricted zones as policy.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No fresh high-casualty strikes, tanker sinkings, infrastructure hits, or political shocks that reset the cycle.

A temporary ceasefire, partial traffic recovery under escort, lower oil prices from demand destruction, or a battlefield pause do **not** meet the standard. Commercial confidence, insurance pricing, and transit volumes are gating factors.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant classes (partial/functional stabilization of Gulf/chokepoint or comparable regional war + maritime disruption):

1. **1980–88 Iran–Iraq War Tanker War phase**: Prolonged attacks on shipping; traffic continued at reduced levels with escorts/insurance spikes; full commercial confidence lagged years after major fighting ended. Suggests multi-year residual risk for insurance/transit even after kinetic peak.
2. **2019–2021 Gulf tanker attacks / Abqaiq–Khurais / Soleimani aftermath**: Discrete Iranian/proxy actions produced sharp but temporary insurance spikes and traffic dips; normalization of rates and confidence took months once kinetic activity stopped and diplomacy stabilized. Breaks down because 2026 scale is far larger (full war, blockade, sustained Hormuz contest).
3. **2023–2025 Red Sea / Houthi campaign**: Shipping largely avoided or diverted; insurance stayed elevated; traffic recovered only gradually and incompletely even after reduced attacks, with lasting route changes. Closest recent chokepoint analogue for commercial behavior lagging kinetic reduction.
4. **1990–91 Gulf War + aftermath**: Rapid military resolution; shipping and oil flows recovered within months once ground war ended and mines cleared, but residual insurance and posture effects lingered. Breaks down because 2026 involves no clear decisive land campaign ending Iranian capacity to contest the Strait.
5. **Post-2015 Iran nuclear deal / JCPOA period vs. 2018–2020 maximum-pressure episodes**: Maritime risk fluctuated with sanctions/diplomacy; commercial normalization tracked credible de-escalation more than battlefield pauses.
6. **Suez 1956 / 1967 / occasional mine/closure episodes**: Physical reopening ≠ commercial normality; insurance and charterer confidence lagged clearance and political settlement.

**Base-rate prior (pre-case-specific update)** for functional normality under the tight definition:  
1 week ~1–3%; 1 month ~5–10%; 3 months ~15–25%; 6 months ~30–45%; 1 year ~45–60%; 18 months ~55–70%.  
These reflect that chokepoint wars produce sticky commercial and insurance effects; kinetic pauses alone rarely restore pre-crisis transit + insurance + confidence within months.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Credible, sustained US–Iran (or broader) political settlement that removes Iranian incentive/ability to contest Hormuz.  
2. Sustained drop in direct kinetic exchanges + measurable decline in proxy attacks on energy/shipping.  
3. Demonstrated free commercial transit at scale without routine escorts or Iranian coordination demands.  
4. Insurance market re-rating after weeks/months of low incidents (capacity returns, premiums fall).  
5. Gulf state confidence and restored export continuity reducing political pressure for escalation.  

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Iranian residual capability and political will to contest the Strait (exclusion zones, tanker targeting, mines, proxies) even after degradation.  
2. Cycle of retaliation (tanker strikes ↔ missile attempts ↔ proxy attacks) that repeatedly resets commercial confidence.  
3. Sticky insurance and charterer behavior: elevated rates and risk aversion persist after kinetic lulls.  
4. Proxy autonomy (Houthis especially) and secondary fronts (Lebanon, Red Sea) that keep Gulf risk elevated.  
5. Domestic Iranian politics + economic desperation creating hardline incentives; US domestic calendar (midterms) constraining deals.  
6. Force posture inertia: wartime naval presence and blockade logic hard to unwind quickly.

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Intermittent kinetic lulls and partial US-facilitated traffic continue; another diplomatic window opens under economic pressure but produces only temporary arrangements; insurance and charterer caution keep volumes and premiums abnormal for many months; proxy activity (especially Houthi) periodically resets risk perception; gradual, incomplete recovery toward 50–70% of pre-war commercial normality by 12–18 months, still short of the full multi-domain definition for a large fraction of scenarios.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Decisive US degradation of Iranian maritime and missile capacity combined with a credible, verified political settlement (Strait governance, sanctions relief for restraint) that holds for 60–90 days; rapid insurance capacity return; Gulf states and Asian buyers resume full loadings; proxy stand-down. Could push 3–6 month probabilities materially higher.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Iran successfully maintains contested status via residual capabilities, exclusion zones, and proxies; repeated retaliation cycles; insurance market remains elevated or withdraws capacity; traffic stays structurally depressed; secondary fronts (Red Sea, Lebanon) keep Gulf risk elevated. Probabilities at 1 year and beyond stay below 40–45%.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
- Over-estimating normalization: May underweight how sticky insurance and charterer behavior proved in Red Sea and prior Gulf episodes; Iranian political incentives to retain Strait leverage may be stronger than economic pain suggests; US domestic politics could constrain durable deals.  
- Under-estimating normalization: May underweight cumulative degradation of Iranian capabilities, the strength of US naval control claims, and market adaptation (alternative routes, strategic stocks, demand destruction). Base rates from less intense conflicts may be too pessimistic for a high-intensity case that eventually exhausts one side.  
Load-bearing assumptions: Iranian residual capacity to disrupt remains material; commercial actors require sustained low-incident periods, not announcements; proxy activity is not fully controllable by Tehran.
