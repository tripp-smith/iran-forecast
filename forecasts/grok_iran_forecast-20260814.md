**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each of these horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality requires simultaneous satisfaction of all of the following observable conditions for a sustained period (minimum 14 consecutive days, not a single snapshot):

- Direct military activity: No US/Israeli/Iranian kinetic strikes inside Iran or against Gulf energy/port/military infrastructure related to the war; no major naval or air engagements in the Gulf or Strait of Hormuz.
- Proxy activity: No sustained Houthi or other Iranian-aligned attacks on commercial shipping in the Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb or Gulf approaches that materially deter traffic.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Daily commercial transits ≥80% of pre-war baseline (~100–140 vessels/day, majority AIS-visible, using established TSS or agreed dual corridors without routine escort requirement); no successful attacks on commercial vessels for 14+ days; mines cleared or risk assessed as residual/low by JMIC/UKMTO/IMO equivalents.
- Commercial transit levels: Aggregate oil + LNG + other cargo volumes through Hormuz recovered to ≥75–80% of pre-war averages on a rolling 7-day basis, without systemic ship-to-ship transfers or dark fleet dominance as the primary mode.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums for Hormuz transit ≤1.0–1.5% of hull value (vs pre-war ~0.1–0.25% and current multi-percent levels); coverage widely available without major capacity withdrawal or JWC listing that effectively prices out commercial operators.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf exporters (Saudi, UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Iraq, Iran under any residual constraints) able to load and export at ≥80% of pre-war capacity without force majeure declarations driven by security; Ras Laffan and major crude terminals operating near normal utilization.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent crude risk premium attributable to Hormuz/Gulf security below ~$10–15/bbl sustained (not temporary spikes); no repeated IEA emergency stock draws driven by Gulf disruption; LNG spot volatility in Asia/Europe normalized relative to non-Gulf factors.
- Force posture of major actors: US naval/air presence reduced from peak wartime levels toward a sustainable deterrence posture; Iranian coastal anti-ship/missile/drone posture not actively enforcing unilateral control or authorization regimes that require foreign naval escort for routine commercial transit.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new large-scale Iranian attacks on Gulf states hosting US forces, no expansion of the conflict to new theaters that re-closes or severely constrains Hormuz, and no collapse of any interim diplomatic arrangements that restarts sustained kinetic cycles.

A temporary ceasefire, battlefield pause, or oil-price dip alone does not meet the definition. Commercial and insurance normalization lag kinetic pauses; the definition is multi-domain and operational.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot (as of 14 August 2026)**

War began 28 February 2026 with US-Israeli strikes that killed Supreme Leader Khamenei and degraded Iranian capabilities. Iran retaliated with widespread missile/drone attacks on Gulf states, US bases, and shipping, producing a de facto Hormuz closure. An April ceasefire and June 17 Islamabad MOU produced partial reopening (traffic recovered toward ~20–40% of baseline in late June). The MOU collapsed in early July after Iranian attacks on commercial vessels; US reimposed blockade, struck Iranian coastal/radar/anti-ship sites (dozens to 80+ targets in waves), and Iran responded against regional US positions.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant historical cases:

1. **Iran-Iraq Tanker War (1984–1988)**: Sustained attacks on shipping (~400–500 vessels hit); strait never fully closed; traffic reduced ~25% at peaks; insurance spiked; US reflagging/escorts (Earnest Will) and limited strikes (Praying Mantis) contained but did not end attacks until the overall war ceasefire. Shipping recovered gradually with the 1988 ceasefire; full commercial confidence took months. Relevant for prolonged asymmetric maritime attrition and insurance lag. Breaks down because Iran depended on Hormuz for its own exports and the conflict was bilateral land-war driven rather than a direct US-Iran war over regime survival and strait control.

2. **2019 Gulf of Oman tanker attacks + tanker seizures**: Short kinetic spikes, rapid insurance spikes (10×), limited traffic disruption; de-escalation within weeks to months via diplomacy and restraint. Suggests weeks for partial shipping recovery if political will exists; less relevant for multi-month sustained closure and regime-decapitation context.

3. **Houthi Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb campaign (late 2023–2025/26)**: Traffic through Suez/Bab el-Mandeb fell 50–90% for extended periods; insurance elevated; even after attack pauses or ceasefires, commercial return was slow and uneven (months of trial voyages, residual avoidance). Full recovery lagged kinetic pause by many months due to insurance, crew willingness, and residual risk perception. Highly relevant for commercial lag after proxy/maritime disruption.

4. **Suez Canal closures (1956, 1967–75, 2021 Ever Given)**: Physical total closures; recovery of traffic after clearance/reopening was relatively fast (days–weeks for Ever Given; years for political closures). Less relevant for ongoing kinetic/insurance risk.

5. **Black Sea grain corridor / Ukraine war maritime restrictions**: Partial corridors restored under diplomacy; residual risk and insurance kept volumes below potential for extended periods. Relevant for “managed” partial reopenings.

6. **Broader post-major-war Gulf energy recovery patterns (e.g., after 1991, 2003)**: Infrastructure and confidence recovery measured in months to years when security architecture is unsettled.

**Base-rate prior (before case-specific evidence)**: Probability of meeting the full multi-domain definition:
- 1 week: ~2–5%
- 1 month: ~8–15%
- 3 months: ~20–30%
- 6 months: ~35–45%
- 1 year: ~50–60%
- 18 months: ~60–70%

These reflect historical patterns in which kinetic pauses occur faster than commercial/insurance/force-posture normalization, and full multi-domain recovery after major Gulf maritime crises is measured in many months to >1 year.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

1. Mutual exhaustion and economic pressure on Gulf exporters + global oil/LNG consumers (highest weight).
2. Diplomatic channels (Oman, Qatar, Pakistan) already active and producing partial texts.
3. US military degradation of Iranian anti-ship/missile/drone capacity reduces Iran’s ability to sustain high-intensity enforcement.
4. Commercial incentive for both sides to restore revenue/flows once face-saving formula exists.
5. Historical precedent that sustained high oil prices and stockpile draws eventually force political accommodation.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

1. Core dispute over Hormuz control/sovereignty remains unresolved; Iran treats it as residual leverage after leadership losses.
2. Fragility of prior MOUs/ceasefires (April, June) shows rapid reversion on small triggers.
3. Insurance and commercial risk perception lag kinetic pauses by months (Red Sea precedent).
4. Proxy residual capacity (Houthis) and potential for new escalation triggers (Lebanon/Gaza, internal Iranian politics, US domestic calendar).
5. Force posture and mine residual risk keep war-risk premiums elevated even in quiet periods.
6. Domestic political incentives on both sides to avoid appearing to “lose” the strait.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued “no war, no peace” with intermittent kinetic spikes and diplomatic pulses. Traffic recovers unevenly to 30–60% under managed/dark/escorted arrangements by 3–6 months if talks produce a temporary formula. Full multi-domain normality (insurance + unrestricted commercial confidence + posture drawdown) more likely only after 9–18 months, contingent on a face-saving political text that freezes the control dispute. Oil markets remain range-bound with periodic risk premia.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Strong economic pain (stockpile exhaustion, Gulf fiscal stress, Asian buyer pressure) plus a narrow technical agreement on dual corridors + temporary mutual stand-down, producing 60%+ traffic and insurance compression within 2–3 months. Requires both sides to prioritize revenue over maximalist control claims. Probability of this path ~20–25%.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Iran treats Hormuz leverage as non-negotiable residual power; intermittent attacks and authorization demands persist; insurance stays elevated; commercial operators permanently diversify (Cape, pipelines, dark fleet). “Managed” low traffic becomes the equilibrium for >18 months. Supported by prior MOU fragility and Red Sea lag precedent. Probability ~25–30%.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Potential overestimation of normalization: I may underweight the depth of the sovereignty dispute and Iranian domestic incentives to maintain leverage after leadership losses; commercial/insurance lag could be longer than Red Sea analogies suggest because the stakes (regime survival, US presence) are higher. Potential underestimation: Economic and military attrition may force accommodation faster than historical asymmetric cases; US degradation of Iranian coastal capabilities may already make sustained enforcement harder than assumed. Load-bearing assumptions: (1) neither side seeks full-scale re-escalation, (2) global oil demand remains robust enough to generate pressure, (3) proxies remain controllable. Evidence that would falsify: sustained zero-attack + >80% traffic + premium collapse within 30–45 days (faster case); or explicit Iranian permanent “authorization regime” with successful enforcement for 60+ days (prolonged case).
