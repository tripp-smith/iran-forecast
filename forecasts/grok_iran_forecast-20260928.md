**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months (from 28 September 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality requires simultaneous, sustained satisfaction of all of the following observable criteria for at least 14 consecutive days (to exclude temporary lulls):

- Direct military activity: No sustained US/Iran or allied kinetic exchanges (missile, drone, air, or naval strikes) targeting military or dual-use assets in or immediately adjacent to the Persian Gulf/Strait of Hormuz area at a rate exceeding pre-war baseline harassment levels.
- Proxy activity: No significant proxy attacks (Houthi, Iraqi militias, or others) on Gulf energy infrastructure, shipping, or bases that demonstrably disrupt commercial operations.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: No successful attacks on commercial vessels; mines cleared or rendered non-threat to international transit corridors; freedom of navigation without Iranian permit requirements or US/coalition escort as a routine necessity.
- Commercial transit levels: Average daily large commercial vessel transits (tankers, gas carriers, bulkers, containers) ≥80% of pre-war baseline (~100+ of ~125 large vessels/day), with AIS-visible traffic dominant and dark shipping not the primary mode.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums ≤3× pre-war baseline (~0.1% of hull value per voyage) and widely available without case-by-case exclusion or government backstop dominance; cargo war-risk also materially normalized.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Aggregate Gulf crude + products + LNG flows through Hormuz or equivalent (including verified bypass) ≥85% of pre-war levels (~17+ mb/d oil-equivalent), with Qatar LNG trains operating near capacity and no major ongoing force majeure on terminal loadings.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent not persistently elevated solely by Hormuz risk premium (e.g., sustained periods below levels reflecting structural supply/demand only); forward curves and volatility indices not pricing acute chokepoint disruption as the dominant near-term driver.
- Force posture of major actors: No active large-scale US/CENTCOM “mow the lawn” campaign or Iranian anti-access/area-denial surge in the strait; force levels and alert status consistent with post-crisis residual deterrence rather than active campaign.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new major Iranian nuclear threshold crossing, mass-casualty strike on Gulf state territory, or equivalent that reopens full-scale hostilities.

A temporary ceasefire, lower oil prices from demand destruction, battlefield pause, or partial escorted flows do **not** constitute normality. All domains must cohere.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant classes (with limitations):

1. **Tanker War (1980–88 Iran-Iraq)**: Prolonged attacks on shipping in the same theater. Commercial traffic adapted via escorts, flags of convenience, and higher premiums rather than full normalization until ceasefire + political settlement. Full commercial recovery lagged formal end of hostilities by months to years. Breaks down: interstate conventional war with less precise modern munitions and no nuclear overhang; US reflagging was more decisive.
2. **2019–2021 Gulf tanker attacks / Soleimani aftermath / Iranian seizures**: Short, deniable disruptions. Insurance spiked and traffic adjusted within weeks to months once kinetic intensity fell; full insurance normalization took longer. Breaks down: far lower intensity and duration than 2026 interstate war.
3. **Red Sea / Houthi campaign (2023–ongoing)**: Shipping largely rerouted; premiums elevated; no rapid return to Suez normality despite partial ceasefires. Commercial avoidance persists while threat remains credible. Breaks down: alternative routes exist (Cape); Hormuz has fewer scalable alternatives for LNG/volume.
4. **Suez 1956 / 1967–75 closures and post-war reopenings**: Physical or political closure followed by staged recovery tied to political settlement and demining/clearance. Full traffic recovery measured in months after political deal. Breaks down: different legal/physical character.
5. **Black Sea / Ukraine grain corridor and wartime shipping**: Partial corridors under military guarantee; insurance and commercial volumes never fully normalized while active war continued. Breaks down: different commodity and geography.
6. **Post-ceasefire chokepoint recoveries (limited sample)**: Where political settlement is incomplete and residual capabilities remain, commercial/insurance normalization routinely lags military pause by 3–12+ months.

Base-rate prior (before case-specific evidence; approximate cumulative probability of meeting the full multi-domain definition):  
- 1 week: <5%  
- 1 month: 5–10%  
- 3 months: 15–25%  
- 6 months: 30–40%  
- 1 year: 45–55%  
- 18 months: 55–65%  

These reflect that full commercial + insurance + multi-domain recovery after intense maritime disruption historically requires durable political settlement plus demonstrated safety, not just reduced kinetic activity.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

- Exhaustion and mutual interest in revenue/stability (Iran needs oil sales; Gulf states and global consumers need flows).
- US military superiority and demonstrated ability to degrade Iranian coastal systems.
- Existing workarounds (pipelines, STS, dark fleet) that reduce pure leverage of full closure.
- Mediation infrastructure (Oman, Qatar, others) and residual MOU language.
- Commercial adaptation pressure and insurer desire for volume.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

- Iranian strategic view of Hormuz control as core residual leverage and regime-security tool.
- Incomplete political settlement (nuclear, sanctions, force posture, “administration” of the strait).
- Residual capabilities on both sides enabling rapid re-escalation.
- Insurance market conservatism after large claims and repeated false starts.
- Proxy and multi-front linkages (Lebanon, Yemen, etc.).
- Domestic political incentives on both sides (US midterms context; Iranian hardliner influence).
- High bar of the multi-domain definition itself.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued low-to-moderate intensity contestation of the strait with intermittent diplomacy, partial escorted/dark flows, elevated but fluctuating insurance, and gradual commercial adaptation via workarounds. Functional normality (full multi-domain) is more likely after a political settlement than before; pure military attrition is slower and incomplete for insurance/commercial confidence. Probability mass concentrates in the 6–18 month window for crossing the threshold.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

A sudden, verified comprehensive stand-down (perhaps post-midterms or under external pressure) producing rapid demining, high-volume unescorted transit, and insurer rate collapse within weeks. Requires both sides to prioritize near-term economic/political gains over leverage. Would push 3-month probability toward 40%+ and 6-month toward 55%+.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Iran treats residual Hormuz leverage as non-negotiable; US maintains periodic strikes and blockade pressure; proxies remain active; insurance stays elevated; traffic recovers only partially via workarounds. Multi-domain normality delayed beyond 18 months. Would keep even 18-month probability below 40%.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Overestimation risk: The definition is intentionally stringent; markets and operators may treat “good enough” partial flows and lower kinetic intensity as operational normality earlier than the criteria allow. Adaptation (STS, pipelines, dark fleet, selective risk-taking) may erode the practical impact of residual threat faster than expected.  
Underestimation risk: Iranian regime cohesion and willingness to absorb costs may be higher; US political constraints (costs, midterms, domestic priorities) may limit sustained pressure; insurance memory and seafarer aversion may prove stickier than base rates from shorter crises suggest.  
Load-bearing assumptions: (1) No sudden regime-threatening escalation or collapse in Iran; (2) US remains willing to sustain residual presence and strikes; (3) Insurance responds primarily to demonstrated safety rather than political declarations.
