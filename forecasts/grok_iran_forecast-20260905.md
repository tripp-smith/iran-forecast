**1. Forecast Question**  
Probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War (onset ~28 February 2026) by the horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months from 5 September 2026.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
All of the following must hold concurrently and sustainably (not as a temporary pause), observable via primary indicators:

- Direct military activity: No kinetic exchanges (missiles, drones, airstrikes, naval engagements) between Iran/IRGC and the US, Israel, or Gulf states involving Persian Gulf/Strait of Hormuz targets or bases for ≥30 consecutive days; no new major Iranian minelaying or anti-ship systems activation.
- Proxy activity: No significant proxy attacks (Houthis or others) on Gulf energy infrastructure, shipping, or related targets linked to the conflict.
- Shipping security (Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters): Freedom of navigation restored under international law/IMO TSS or equivalent widely accepted lanes; no IRGC/Iranian enforcement of unilateral permits, tolls, or corridor mandates; residual mine risk cleared or managed to commercial standards; risk assessments (e.g., JMIC) at moderate or lower, not severe/critical.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits (AIS + reliable dark estimates) sustainably ≥70–80% of pre-war baseline (~120–130/day, or ~3,000+/month); mix includes diverse commercial flags, not dominated by state/shadow fleet.
- War-risk insurance: Hull war-risk premiums for Hormuz transit back to ≤0.25–0.5% of hull value (near or modestly above pre-war ~0.1–0.25%), with broad capacity and no systematic cancellations or extreme surcharges.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf crude/products flows through Hormuz sustainably near pre-war volumes (~15–20+ mbpd range depending on exact baseline); LNG (Qatar etc.) largely restored; Iranian exports normalized under whatever sanctions regime exists without blockade-level disruption.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent (and related) risk premium attributable to Hormuz largely eliminated; volatility normalized relative to non-Gulf drivers; no sustained structural shortage pricing from the chokepoint.
- Force posture of major actors: Significant US/CENTCOM drawdown from peak wartime posture in the Gulf; Iranian coastal/anti-access forces not in heightened continuous readiness for closure; Gulf states not in continuous high alert for Iranian strikes.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No unresolved flashpoints (e.g., contested control claims, Lebanon linkage, nuclear red lines) that routinely produce kinetic responses.

A temporary ceasefire, partial traffic recovery, lower oil prices alone, or battlefield pause does **not** qualify. Commercial confidence and multi-domain stability are required.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant classes (with caveats):

- Tanker War / Iran-Iraq War maritime phase (1980s): Prolonged low-intensity disruption of Gulf shipping; escorts and adaptations eventually restored flows but full commercial normality and confidence took years amid ongoing conflict. Suggests multi-year tails for full normalization when core political disputes unresolved.
- 2019–2020 Gulf tanker attacks / max-pressure period + Soleimani aftermath: Short spikes in risk and premiums; traffic largely continued with elevated insurance and caution. Faster recovery (weeks–months) once kinetic intensity dropped, but not a full-scale war analog.
- Red Sea / Houthi disruptions (2023–2025+): Significant diversion and risk premium; partial recovery with military pressure and diplomacy, but persistent elevated risk and incomplete return for many operators. Partial commercial adaptation possible in months; full confidence longer.
- Suez closures / major chokepoint disruptions (historical): Traffic recovers relatively quickly once physical access restored if political settlement holds; confidence and insurance lag.
- Broader post-major Middle East wars/ceasefires (e.g., various Gulf crises): Military pauses common within months; durable commercial and insurance normalization rarer without political settlement and force posture changes. Base rates for full multi-domain normality within 3–6 months of peak intensity are low (<20–30% in high-intensity cases); within 12–18 months moderate if de-escalation path emerges.

**Base-rate prior (pre-current evidence adjustment, approximate cumulative probability of meeting the full operational definition):**  
1 week: ~1–3%  
1 month: ~5–10%  
3 months: ~15–25%  
6 months: ~25–40%  
1 year: ~40–55%  
18 months: ~50–65%  

These reflect historical stickiness of maritime risk, insurance lag, and political disputes over chokepoints.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**  
Durable political settlement or stable informal modus vivendi on Hormuz administration; sustained absence of kinetic incidents (≥30–60 days); successful, verified mine clearance and accepted transit regime; insurance market re-pricing on evidence; Gulf state mediation success; US force posture stabilization; commercial adaptation (escorts, STS) building confidence.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**  
Iranian insistence on recognized control/tolls/permits as core leverage; hardline IRGC influence; linkage to Lebanon/Hezbollah or nuclear issues; US domestic/political incentives or alliance commitments limiting concessions; residual mines/asymmetric capabilities; commercial and insurance risk aversion (long memory); proxy residual activity; second-order effects (buyer caution in Asia, Gulf state hedging).

Ranked importance: Political settlement on Hormuz control (highest gate); sustained kinetic quiet; insurance/commercial confidence; force posture.

**8. Decomposed Sub-Forecasts** (approximate probabilities of component achievement by horizon; not independent)

- Major sustained reduction in direct strikes: 1w <10%; 1m ~20%; 3m ~40%; 6m ~55%; 1y ~65%; 18m ~70%.  
- Sustained safe commercial transit (≥70–80% volume, accepted lanes): 1w <5%; 1m ~10–15%; 3m ~25%; 6m ~40%; 1y ~55%; 18m ~65%.  
- Meaningful insurance normalization: lags transit by weeks–months; lower probabilities, especially near term.  
- Oil/LNG export recovery to near-baseline: somewhat higher than full transit normality due to adaptation, but still constrained.  
- Proxy restraint + no major new triggers + force drawdown: intermediate; political settlement is the common gate.

Gating factors are political settlement + sustained kinetic quiet; commercial/insurance follow with lag.

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Protracted low-intensity contest over Hormuz control with intermittent diplomacy and fragile quiet periods. Traffic and flows recover partially via adaptation, escorts, and corridors, but full definitional normality is delayed by political disagreement, insurance lag, and residual kinetic risk. Gradual improvement over 6–18 months more likely than rapid reset or permanent closure.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Credible, verified political bargain (possibly mediated) that resolves or defers Iranian control claims acceptably to commercial actors and the US; rapid insurance compression; months of incident-free traffic building confidence. Would require Iranian prioritization of economic relief over leverage and US willingness to accept some face-saving Iranian role.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Hardline consolidation in Tehran treating Hormuz as permanent strategic asset; repeated breakdown of understandings; residual mining/asymmetric threats; commercial and insurance markets treating elevated risk as structural; proxy or Lebanon linkage keeping temperature high. Could extend definitional abnormality well beyond 18 months.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Possible overestimation of normalization: Underweights Iranian domestic hardliner incentives and the strategic value of Hormuz leverage; commercial/insurance recovery may lag political quiet longer than assumed (Red Sea precedent); definition is strict, so “functional” commercial adaptation short of full normality is more likely.  
Possible underestimation: Economic pain on all sides (Gulf states, Iran, global markets, US political costs) may force faster pragmatic accommodation than pure power analysis suggests; adaptive shipping (dark, STS, escorts) can restore volumes faster than pure transit statistics imply.  
Load-bearing assumptions: That political settlement on the Strait is necessary for the full definition; that insurance and diverse commercial traffic are slow to return; that no sudden exogenous shock (regime change, major external war) intervenes.
