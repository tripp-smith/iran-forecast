**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each of these horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months. (As of 18 October 2026.)

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

“Return to functional normality” requires sustained, multi-domain conditions meeting all of the following for a rolling 30-day window (with no material reversals):

- Direct military activity: Near-zero confirmed kinetic attacks (missiles, drones, mines, projectiles, or boarding) by Iranian forces or proxies on commercial vessels, Gulf energy infrastructure, or host-state territory. No reciprocal US/Israeli strikes on Iranian territory or ports in response to maritime incidents.
- Proxy activity: Houthis and other Iran-aligned groups not conducting sustained attacks on shipping in the Bab el-Mandeb, Red Sea, or Gulf approaches, and not targeting Gulf state energy or transport infrastructure.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: UKMTO/JMIC threat level no longer “severe”; no requirement for US/partner escorts or dark (AIS-off) corridors as the dominant transit mode; vessels can transit standard routes without Iranian “authorization” or toll claims.
- Commercial transit levels: Average daily commercial transits (AIS-visible plus confirmed dark/STS) at or above 80–90% of the pre-war baseline (~80–100+ vessels/day equivalent), without heavy concentration in one corridor.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Additional war-risk premiums for hull at or below ~1–2% of insured value per voyage (or ≤2–3× peacetime baseline), with capacity available on standard terms and no widespread cancellations or exclusions.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Aggregate Gulf petroleum liquids and LNG flows (including non-Iranian and any resumed Iranian volumes) at ≥90% of pre-war baselines (~17 Mbpd petroleum liquids through Hormuz equivalent, adjusted for bypasses), with low day-to-day volatility driven by security incidents. Bypass pipelines and STS transfers not required as primary outlets.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent crude and LNG prices no longer pricing a structural Hormuz risk premium (sustained below ~$80–85/bbl for Brent in the absence of other shocks); freight and insurance costs normalized.
- Force posture of major actors: US carrier and amphibious presence reduced from multi-carrier wartime levels; Iranian IRGC Navy not conducting active interdiction; no large-scale reinforcements signaling imminent resumption of major combat operations.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No collapse of remaining diplomatic channels, no major infrastructure attack, no large civilian casualties from strikes, and no political decision in Tehran or Washington to re-escalate for leverage (e.g., pre- or post-election).

A temporary ceasefire, lower spot oil prices alone, or a pause in land strikes does not meet the standard. Commercial and insurance normalization are gating requirements.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot**

As of mid-October 2026 (roughly Day 231 of the conflict that began with US-Israeli strikes on 28 February 2026), the Persian Gulf remains far from the definition above. The June 17 Islamabad MOU (60-day framework) collapsed by early July; the US blockade of Iranian ports/shipping was reimposed around 14 July and has held. Iranian crude exports are reported at or near zero in October (Kpler, Vortexa, UANI tracking). 

Iranian attacks on commercial shipping in and near Hormuz reached wartime highs in early-to-mid October (UKMTO/JMIC: 10–12+ vessels in recent weeks; multiple sinkings or disabling events, including a Singapore-flagged container ship). Both sides claim control of the strait; US CENTCOM reports continued escorted/dark transits and significant weekly oil movement, while Iranian sources assert closure of “unauthorized” routes and mine strikes. Oil flows through Hormuz have been volatile (recent 7-day averages cited by Kpler in the ~9–13 Mbpd range, ~60–75% of the ~17 Mbpd pre-war baseline, with sharp daily drops during attack spikes). Non-Iranian Gulf exports have partially recovered via pipelines, Fujairah/Oman loadings, and STS transfers. 

War-risk premiums are elevated (reports of 5–10% of hull value in peak periods; multiples of 15–30× peacetime in some trackers). Brent has traded above $100–110. US force posture includes multiple carriers in the region, with additional reinforcements (Theodore Roosevelt CSG and amphibious elements) en route or arriving; Trump has deferred major strikes on Iranian territory until after the 3 November midterms while maintaining the blockade. Houthi activity continues to affect Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb routes and Saudi infrastructure. Diplomatic channels via Pakistan, Qatar, and others remain open but without a breakthrough. Direct large-scale strikes on Iranian territory have been limited in recent weeks relative to earlier phases.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant classes (selected for maritime/chokepoint disruption under active or residual conflict):

- 1980s Tanker War (Iran-Iraq, ~1984–88): Sustained attacks on shipping, mines, US escorts (Earnest Will). Traffic continued at reduced security but never fully “normal” until the broader UN ceasefire. Suggests 6–18+ months of elevated risk even under naval presence; full commercial confidence lagged the military pause. Relevant for attrition dynamics; breaks down because the current conflict has a clearer US blockade objective and higher Iranian asymmetric capacity plus proxies.
- 2019 Gulf tanker attacks and Abqaiq: Short, sharp incidents produced temporary insurance spikes and price volatility that largely normalized within weeks to a few months once direct attacks ceased. Suggests rapid recovery if kinetic activity drops to near-zero. Breaks down because 2019 was episodic rather than a sustained war with blockade.
- 2023–24 Red Sea/Houthi disruptions: Multi-month elevated insurance, rerouting, and partial volume recovery under naval presence (Prosperity Guardian and others), but not full return to pre-crisis norms or low premiums while attacks continued. Suggests 3–12 months of residual commercial friction after attacks decline. Relevant for insurance lag and buyer behavior; breaks down due to Hormuz’s narrower geography and higher oil share.
- 1990–91 Gulf War aftermath: Oil disruptions were relatively short once hostilities ended; markets and shipping recovered over months. Suggests faster post-conflict normalization when the military outcome is decisive. Breaks down because the current conflict is not a short conventional war ending in clear territorial control.
- Post-2003 Iraq and episodic Hormuz tensions (2011–12 threats, 2015–19 seizures): Mostly threats or limited incidents with limited sustained commercial impact. Suggests low base rates for multi-month full closures, but also that residual risk can persist under sanctions pressure.

Base-rate prior (before current-case adjustment), for full multi-domain normality as defined:  
1 week: 1–3%; 1 month: 5–10%; 3 months: 15–25%; 6 months: 30–40%; 1 year: 45–55%; 18 months: 55–65%.  
These reflect that commercial/insurance normalization lags military pauses in chokepoint cases, and that attrition or unresolved political disputes extend timelines.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

Ranked: (1) Economic pain on Iran from blockade (zero crude exports) raising incentive for a deal that restores revenue; (2) US electoral calendar creating a window for de-escalation rhetoric before/after midterms; (3) Partial operational adaptation (escorts, STS, pipelines) demonstrating that volumes can be maintained even under risk; (4) Mutual interest in avoiding permanent high oil prices and global recession risk; (5) Continued mediator channels (Pakistan, Qatar, etc.).

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

Ranked: (1) Iranian demonstrated willingness and capacity to impose high costs on shipping (current attack tempo) to retain leverage; (2) Unresolved core disputes (Hormuz control terms, nuclear constraints, sanctions/blockade relief, frozen assets); (3) High and reinforcing US force posture that signals readiness for post-election options; (4) Insurance and shipper risk aversion that lags any military pause by weeks to months; (5) Proxy (Houthi) activity as an independent escalation vector; (6) Mutual claims of control and attribution disputes that sustain uncertainty.

Approximate probabilities that each sub-condition holds by horizon (these are intermediate; final synthesis weights gating factors):

- Major reduction in direct strikes (near-zero for 30 days): 1w 5%; 1m 15%; 3m 35%; 6m 50%; 1y 65%; 18m 75%.
- Sustained safe commercial transit (volumes + no escorts dominant): 1w 2%; 1m 8%; 3m 20%; 6m 35%; 1y 50%; 18m 60%.

- Oil/LNG export recovery to near baseline with low volatility: 1w 10%; 1m 20%; 3m 40%; 6m 55%; 1y 65%; 18m 70%.
- Proxy restraint (Houthis maritime): 1w 10%; 1m 20%; 3m 35%; 6m 45%; 1y 55%; 18m 60%.

- No new major escalation trigger: 1w 60%; 1m 50%; 3m 45%; 6m 40%; 1y 35%; 18m 30% (longer horizons accumulate risk).

Insurance and sustained safe transit (without escorts) are the slowest and most gating for the full definition.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued contested maritime domain through the end of 2026, with volatile but non-zero oil flows under escort/STS/bypass adaptations, elevated insurance, and Iranian exports remaining blocked. Post-midterm period sees either limited strikes or intensified pressure, followed by a possible late-2026 or 2027 negotiated framework that reduces direct attacks but leaves residual risk, insurance premiums, and force postures above peacetime levels for many months. Full normality (as defined) is more likely in the 12–24 month range than sooner.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Iranian economic distress from the blockade produces a deal within 1–3 months that trades shipping restraint and nuclear constraints for phased blockade relief and sanctions adjustments. Attack tempo drops immediately; insurers reprice within weeks; volumes recover with reduced escort reliance by month 3–4. Probability of this path is low-to-moderate given current positions.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Post-election US strikes or Iranian decision to sustain high-tempo shipping attacks produce a multi-month attrition phase. Insurance remains prohibitive for many operators; flows stay volatile and dependent on military protection; proxies remain active. Full normality delayed beyond 18 months. This path has material probability given demonstrated capabilities and unresolved issues.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Strongest critique of overestimating normalization: The operational definition is demanding (insurance + unescorted volumes + force drawdown), and reference classes show commercial lags of many months even after military pauses. Iranian asymmetric incentives and US domestic politics can sustain disruption longer than the central forecast allows. Strongest critique of underestimating: Blockade pain could force Iranian concessions faster than historical analogies suggest, and markets/insurers can reprice aggressively once a credible pause is observed. Load-bearing assumptions include continued Iranian capacity to attack shipping and the absence of a decisive US military outcome.
