**1. Forecast Question**  
Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each horizon: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
Functional normality requires simultaneous, sustained satisfaction of all of the following observable conditions (not a temporary ceasefire, partial traffic rebound, or oil-price decline alone):

- Direct military activity: No sustained US/Iran or Israel/Iran kinetic exchanges targeting military infrastructure, bases, or forces in the Gulf region; any residual activity limited to isolated, non-escalatory incidents.
- Proxy activity: Material restraint by Iranian-aligned groups (Houthis, Iraqi militias, others) affecting Gulf shipping, energy infrastructure, or US/partner forces; no pattern of attacks that measurably elevates risk premiums or transit avoidance.
- Shipping security (Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters): Free, unrestricted commercial transit without Iranian unilateral schemes, mandatory coordination/tolls/fees, boarding threats, drone/missile/mine incidents, or US blockade enforcement that materially impedes non-sanctioned traffic. Standard Traffic Separation Scheme or equivalent open international regime in effective use.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits sustained near or above ~60–70% of pre-war baseline (~130 total daily transits; tanker-heavy component consistent with historical oil/LNG volumes) for at least 14 consecutive days, with AIS-visible traffic dominant and dark/AIS-off transit residual rather than primary.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull and cargo war-risk premiums returned to within ~2–3× pre-war levels (pre-war ~0.1–0.25% of hull value) and widely available without special consortia or voyage-by-voyage rationing; Joint War Committee listings normalized or risk pricing reflects residual rather than active conflict.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf producers (Saudi, UAE, Kuwait, Qatar, Iraq, etc.) able to sustain export volumes approaching pre-war levels via Hormuz without large-scale forced shut-ins or exclusive reliance on limited pipeline bypass capacity; Qatar LNG loadings and loadings from other terminals flowing at near-normal rates.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent risk premium attributable to Hormuz/Gulf conflict reduced such that prices and implied volatility no longer reflect acute physical disruption or imminent closure risk (distinct from broader supply/demand factors).
- Force posture of major actors: Material de-escalation from peak wartime posture (reduced strike tempo, no active large-scale blockade enforcement, no massed anti-shipping assets on high alert); residual presence consistent with deterrence rather than active campaign.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new large-scale kinetic events, major infrastructure attacks, or diplomatic breakdowns that reverse the above conditions within the observation window.

A ceasefire or MOU without commercial, insurance, and transit normalization does not meet the standard. Partial recovery (e.g., 20–40 vessels/day under Iranian-controlled lanes) does not.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot (as of ~13 August 2026)**

A US-Iran Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) around 17 June aimed at a ceasefire and reopening; shipping briefly recovered toward half or more of pre-war levels in late June before collapsing again with tit-for-tat strikes (Iranian attacks on commercial vessels; US CENTCOM strikes on Iranian coastal/radar/drone/small-boat targets; Iranian responses against US facilities in Bahrain/Kuwait).  

As of early-mid August: daily Hormuz transits typically in single digits to low teens (IMF PortWatch, Kpler, MarineTraffic, Windward data show recent days of 1–14 total vessels, often heavily inbound or Iranian-route concentrated, versus ~130 pre-war). Many vessels use Iranian unilateral schemes or run dark. US maintains a naval blockade/enforcement posture (dozens of vessels redirected/disabled/boarded). Iran insists the strait remains closed pending major US concessions (sanctions relief, reparations/compensation, force withdrawal or end of threats, asset unfreezing, end to actions against proxies). Iran-Oman talks on routes/lanes are reported advanced or “final stages” but explicitly decoupled by Tehran from actual reopening. Brent crude trading roughly $88–93 (elevated vs pre-war). **4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant classes (selected for maritime chokepoint disruption + regional war elements):

1. **Tanker War (Iran-Iraq, 1980s, especially 1984–88 peak)**: Prolonged attacks on shipping in the Gulf/Hormuz approaches. Shipping adapted with escorts, higher premiums, and route changes; full commercial/insurance normalization lagged major combat by years and required war termination + confidence restoration. Suggests multi-year tails for full functional recovery after high-intensity periods; partial adaptation possible in months under protection.
2. **2019–2020 Gulf tanker attacks / Saudi Aramco strikes / Iranian seizures**: Discrete Iranian/proxy actions caused sharp but temporary spikes in premiums and traffic caution; recovery to near-normal in weeks to a few months once kinetic activity subsided and deterrence was reasserted. Breaks down because scale was far smaller and no multi-month closure or regime-level war.
3. **Houthi Red Sea / Bab el-Mandeb campaign (2023–ongoing into 2026 context)**: Sustained asymmetric disruption produced large traffic diversion, elevated insurance, and incomplete recovery even after periods of reduced attacks. Shows that low-level residual threat + insurance market memory can keep conditions abnormal for 12–24+ months.
4. **Suez Canal closures (1956, 1967–75)**: Full physical closures produced multi-year commercial and insurance adjustments; reopening required political settlement and clearance. Partial analogy for chokepoint centrality, though different legal/political context.
5. **Post-ceasefire maritime recoveries in other conflicts** (e.g., certain phases of Lebanon/Israel or limited Gulf incidents): Traffic and premiums often lag formal pauses by weeks to months; “functional” commercial normality frequently requires 3–12 months of quiet plus institutional confidence.

Base-rate prior (before heavy case-specific updating; approximate probabilities of meeting a demanding multi-domain normality standard):  
- 1 week: ~5%  
- 1 month: ~15%  
- 3 months: ~30%  
- 6 months: ~45%  
- 1 year: ~55–60%  
- 18 months: ~65%  

These reflect historical lag between kinetic pauses and commercial/insurance normalization, plus the high bar of the operational definition. Current case has larger physical disruption and higher Iranian stake in control than most post-2019 incidents, pushing priors downward relative to short discrete crises.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Mutual economic pain (Gulf producers, Iran oil revenues, global energy costs, Asian importers) creating pressure for a workable deal.  
2. Existence of Oman (and other) mediation channels and residual June MOU framework language.  
3. US incentive to declare a form of victory / reduce commitment cost and oil-price political pressure.  
4. Iranian internal pragmatic faction (economic relief priority) potentially gaining relative to maximalist IRGC line if costs mount.  
5. Technical possibility of demining, route agreements, and insurance capacity return once kinetic activity drops for a sustained period.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Iranian strategic view of Hormuz control as core leverage/deterrent post-war; maximalist linkage of reopening to broad concessions.  
2. Deep mutual distrust after MOU collapse and continued low-intensity exchanges; verification and enforcement problems.  
3. Insurance market and shipowner memory/risk aversion—traffic and premiums lag formal agreements.  
4. Elevated force posture and residual capabilities (mines, drones, coastal systems, proxies) that can rapidly re-disrupt.  
5. Domestic political constraints on both sides against appearing to concede core points.  
6. Second-order effects: production shut-ins, buyer diversification, pipeline reliance that slow full commercial recovery even after traffic resumes.

**9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon**

**1 Week**  
- Probability: 3%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~5%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Down (near-zero traffic, maximalist Iranian conditions, active enforcement/stalemate)  
- Core rationale: No plausible path to multi-domain normality in days; even a sudden deal announcement could not clear mines, restore insurance confidence, or rebuild volumes that fast.  
- Main gating factor: Physical/commercial restart lag.  
- What would raise: Immediate verified full open-regime agreement + simultaneous cessation of all kinetic activity and blockade.  
- What would lower: Further vessel attacks or major strikes.  
- Confidence: High  

**1 Month**  
- Probability: 12%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~15%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slightly down  
- Core rationale: Possible if a narrow technical + political package is agreed quickly and holds, but Iranian linkage of reopening to broad concessions and residual distrust make this low-probability. Insurance and volume recovery would still lag.  
- Main gating factor: Whether Iran-Oman route deal is paired with US concessions sufficient for Tehran to declare reopening.  
- What would raise: Public bilateral or mediated agreement removing Iranian unilateral controls and US blockade, followed by 2+ weeks of incident-free high-volume traffic.  
- What would lower: Breakdown of Oman channel or new kinetic spike.  
- Confidence: Medium-High  

**3 Months**  
- Probability: 28%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~30%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slightly down  
- Core rationale: Time allows negotiation, demining, and testing of arrangements; historical partial recoveries occur in this window after pauses. Still constrained by Iranian strategic interest in leverage and insurance lag.  
- Main gating factor: Durable political bargain that separates (or resolves) Hormuz control from broader sanctions/nuclear issues.  
- What would raise: Sustained quiet + traffic climbing past 60–70 vessels/day under open rules + insurance rate compression.  
- What would lower: Proxy activation or renewed direct strikes.  
- Confidence: Medium  

**6 Months**  
- Probability: 42%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~45%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slightly down  
- Core rationale: Longer window increases chance of cost-driven compromise or exhaustion of maximalist positions; still not high because of verification problems and second-order commercial scars.  
- Main gating factor: Whether a “no war, no full peace” equilibrium stabilizes into de facto commercial normality or remains fragile.  
- What would raise: Multiple months of low incidents + Gulf producers restoring most export volumes + insurers returning capacity at near-normal rates.  
- What would lower: Iranian parliamentary or IRGC moves to formalize permanent control/fees, or major new US/Israeli action.  
- Confidence: Medium  

**1 Year**  
- Probability: 55%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~55–60%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Neutral to slightly down  
- Core rationale: Base rates and economic incentives begin to dominate; prolonged disruption is costly for all parties. However, the operational definition remains demanding (full multi-domain, not just higher traffic under managed conditions). Residual threat and market memory keep probability from rising faster.  
- Main gating factor: Whether Iranian leadership prioritizes economic recovery over permanent leverage claims.  
- What would raise: Formal settlement or durable informal regime restoring pre-war legal status + sustained high volumes.  
- What would lower: Entrenchment of dual-control or fee regimes that keep insurance elevated and Western carriers cautious.  
- Confidence: Medium  

**18 Months**  
- Probability: 65%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~65%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Neutral  
- Core rationale: Longest horizon allows for leadership adaptation, exhaustion, external mediation success, or technological/operational work-arounds. Still well below certainty because chokepoint control can become institutionalized and because new triggers remain possible. Exceeding ~70–75% would require stronger evidence that Iranian core interests have shifted.  
- Main gating factor: Institutionalization vs. erosion of Iranian control claims.  
- What would raise: Clear restoration of unrestricted international regime + normalized insurance + full export recovery for multiple consecutive quarters.  
- What would lower: Permanent dual administration, ongoing low-level attacks, or major new regional war.  
- Confidence: Medium-Low (inherent long-horizon uncertainty)

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Continued “no war, no peace” stalemate for weeks to a few months: low single-digit to low-teen daily transits under contested or Iranian-influenced arrangements, elevated but not extreme oil prices, intermittent kinetic or enforcement incidents, and slow diplomatic progress via Oman/Qatar channels. Gradual, incomplete commercial recovery if a narrow technical deal emerges, with full multi-domain normality more likely only after 6–18 months if economic costs force compromise. Partial adaptation (pipelines, alternative suppliers, higher permanent risk premia) occurs in parallel.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Rapid, verified political package in which the US provides limited sanctions/asset relief and force posture adjustments sufficient for Iran to declare the strait open under a joint Iran-Oman technical regime without fees or unilateral veto. Followed by rapid demining, insurance capacity surge, and traffic rebound above 60–70 vessels/day within weeks. Requires Iranian pragmatic faction dominance and US willingness to accept a face-saving controlled reopening. Probability of this path materializing soon is low but non-zero.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Iran hardens permanent control claims (legislation, fees, mandatory coordination); US maintains or intensifies blockade/enforcement; intermittent vessel attacks or base strikes continue; proxies activate selectively; insurance remains elevated; traffic stays well below 50% of normal for 12+ months; Gulf producers rely more on pipelines and accept lower volumes; oil risk premium persists. This path is supported by current maximalist rhetoric and the June MOU collapse precedent.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Strongest critique of the forecast: It may under-weight the economic pain on all sides and the precedent of partial June recovery, leading to modestly low probabilities at 3–6 months. Conversely, it may still be too optimistic at 1-year+ if Iranian leadership treats Hormuz control as non-negotiable post-war deterrence. The operational definition is deliberately demanding; a looser definition focused only on “more ships moving” would produce higher numbers. Load-bearing assumptions include continued Iranian regime coherence and absence of a sudden major escalation (or sudden collapse). Evidence on exact internal Iranian bargaining ranges and residual mine density is thinner than on traffic and prices.
