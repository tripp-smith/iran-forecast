**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War (initiated 28 February 2026) by each horizon: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality requires simultaneous satisfaction of all of the following observable conditions for a sustained period (not a single day or week of quiet):

- Direct military activity: No kinetic strikes by Iran, the United States, Israel, or major Gulf states on each other’s territory, bases, or military assets inside or immediately adjacent to the Persian Gulf / Strait of Hormuz for at least 30 consecutive days. No new major minelaying or anti-ship missile deployments that materially raise transit risk.
- Proxy activity: No significant proxy attacks (Houthis, Iraqi militias, residual Hezbollah elements, or others) on Gulf shipping, energy infrastructure, or US/allied bases that disrupt commercial operations.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Commercial vessels transit the TSS or established corridors without kinetic attack, boarding, or credible mine threat. No Iranian “toll” or authorization regime that commercial operators treat as mandatory for safe passage.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits (AIS-visible + dark) recover to ≥70–80% of pre-war baseline (~100–130+ vessels/day depending on source). Liquid tanker and LNG carrier movements resume at scale without systematic US naval facilitation or Iranian northern-corridor preference.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk additional premiums for Hormuz/Gulf fall to ≤2–3× pre-war levels (pre-war ~0.1–0.25% of hull value; current ~1.5% or 15×) and remain there without frequent cancellations or route-specific exclusions.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf crude exports (Saudi, UAE, Kuwait, Iraq, Qatar, Iran) recover to within ~10–15% of pre-war volumes via sea; Qatari LNG (pre-war ~20% of global seaborne) resumes substantial loadings and transits without prolonged force majeure. Iranian exports move under transparent commercial terms rather than pure shadow-fleet/dark activity.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent futures and front-month volatility return to levels consistent with normal geopolitical risk premium (not sustained $90–100+ or multi-dollar daily swings driven by Hormuz headlines). No persistent “Hormuz risk premium” dominating price formation.
- Force posture of major actors: US, Iranian, and Gulf forces remain present but in a non-escalatory posture; no ongoing large-scale US strike packages or Iranian multi-country retaliatory salvos.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No successful high-casualty attack on a major tanker, no new closure declaration that sticks, no collapse of residual diplomatic channels into open unlimited warfare, and no credible Iranian nuclear breakout announcement that restarts large-scale strikes.

A temporary ceasefire, partial corridor traffic under escort, lower oil prices from demand destruction, or a battlefield pause does **not** meet the definition. Commercial and insurance normalization are gating factors; military quiet alone is insufficient.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant historical cases (Gulf/chokepoint/maritime disruption/regional war):

1. **Iran–Iraq War tanker phase (1984–1988)**: Sustained attacks on shipping; traffic continued at reduced levels under escort; full commercial/insurance normalization lagged the 1988 ceasefire by 1–2+ years in some metrics. Relevant for asymmetric maritime harassment and insurance persistence. Breaks down: no full closure attempt comparable to 2026 and weaker global energy interdependence.
2. **1990–91 Gulf War / Iraq invasion of Kuwait**: Rapid military resolution; oil facilities recovered faster than expected once combat ended, but insurance and residual risk lingered months. Suggests 3–9 months for functional commercial recovery after decisive military end. Breaks down: clear conventional victory vs. current unresolved hybrid conflict.
3. **Houthi Red Sea / Bab el-Mandeb campaign (2023–ongoing into 2026)**: Partial disruption, high war-risk premiums, traffic diversion; even with US/coalition action and partial pauses, full normalization has taken >18 months and remains incomplete. Strong analog for insurance and commercial caution outlasting kinetic peaks.
4. **2019 Gulf tanker attacks / Saudi Abqaiq–Khurais strikes**: Short sharp shocks; traffic and prices normalized in weeks to a few months once attribution and deterrence settled. Suggests rapid partial recovery possible if escalation is contained and diplomacy holds. Breaks down: scale far smaller; no multi-month closure or leadership decapitation.
5. **Suez Canal closures (1956, 1967–75)**: Prolonged economic adaptation (pipelines, Cape routes); reopening did not instantly restore pre-crisis volumes or risk pricing. Relevant for structural chokepoint effects.
6. **Post-2003 Iraq and post-2011 Libya energy recovery**: Physical export recovery often 6–24 months after major combat reduction, longer for insurance/investment confidence.

Base-rate prior (before current-case adjustment, probability of meeting the full operational definition):

- 1 week: ~1–3%
- 1 month: ~5–10%
- 3 months: ~15–25%
- 6 months: ~30–40%
- 1 year: ~45–55%
- 18 months: ~55–65%

These reflect that pure military pauses occur faster than commercial/insurance recovery, and sustained multi-domain normality is rarer than temporary de-escalation.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

Ranked: (1) Mutual economic exhaustion and oil-price pressure on both sides (US midterms context, Iranian revenue needs); (2) Residual diplomatic architecture (Pakistan/Qatar/Oman) that has produced temporary pauses before; (3) US ability to clear mines/corridors and provide limited escorts; (4) Gulf states’ interest in restoring export revenues and investment climate; (5) Chinese demand for stable energy and quiet Iranian shadow exports.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

Ranked: (1) Iranian hardline consolidation and demonstrated willingness to use Hormuz as leverage after leadership change; (2) Collapse of successive MOUs and low trust; (3) Insurance and commercial operator caution that lags kinetic quiet by months; (4) Qatari LNG physical damage requiring multi-year repairs; (5) Risk of new triggers (successful tanker hit with casualties, nuclear-related escalation, proxy reactivation); (6) US domestic political incentives around midterms and “hard” responses.

Synthesis logic: Sub-components are not averaged. Direct-strike reduction is necessary but early; shipping security, insurance, and export continuity are sequential gating factors that move more slowly and with higher dependence on sustained quiet. Final probabilities sit near or modestly below base-rate priors because the current evidence shows repeated failure of temporary understandings and structural damage (especially LNG).

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued cycles of limited kinetic exchanges, partial US-facilitated corridors, Iranian northern-route/shadow activity, elevated but fluctuating war-risk premiums, and oil prices in the $80–100 range. Diplomatic efforts produce short pauses but no durable multi-domain settlement until economic pressure and political calendars align, most plausibly in the 6–18-month window. Functional normality arrives unevenly: military quiet first, then traffic, then insurance and full export continuity.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Strong mutual interest in ending oil-driven inflation/political pain produces a new monitored framework within weeks; mine clearance succeeds; traffic recovers to 50%+ inside 1–2 months; insurers reprice aggressively; LNG partial restart. Probability of this path materializing: low-to-moderate (~15–20% by 3–6 months).

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Hardline Iranian posture + US domestic incentives lock in episodic harassment, successful or near-miss tanker attacks, persistent high premiums, and incomplete export recovery into 2027–28. Proxy or nuclear-related triggers keep risk elevated. Probability: material (~30–40% that full definition is still unmet at 18 months).

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Overestimation risk: The forecast may underweight Iranian capacity and will to sustain asymmetric maritime pressure after leadership change, and may overestimate the speed of insurance/commercial re-engagement even after kinetic quiet (Houthi Red Sea precedent). Underestimation risk: Mutual exhaustion + midterm pressures + Chinese quiet diplomacy could produce a faster deal than reference classes suggest; partial dark/facilitated flows already show some resilience. Load-bearing assumptions: (1) no decisive military victory by either side, (2) commercial operators remain risk-averse longer than governments, (3) Qatari LNG physical damage is a multi-year constraint. Evidence that would falsify: sustained ≥60–70% traffic + premiums ≤3× for 30+ days without new attacks.

- Qatari LNG loading/export volumes and force-majeure status.
- Frequency and scale of kinetic exchanges vs diplomatic announcements.
- Brent volatility and front-month structure specifically tied to Hormuz headlines.
- Any high-casualty shipping incident or successful major infrastructure strike.
