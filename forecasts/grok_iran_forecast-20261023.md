**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each horizon (1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months), measured from 23 October 2026.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality requires a sustained (multi-week) multi-domain state, not a ceasefire, price dip, or temporary traffic spike. All of the following must hold simultaneously:

- Direct military activity: Average confirmed attacks on commercial shipping in the Strait of Hormuz, Gulf of Oman, and nearby waters <1 per week, with no sinkings, major engine-room hits, or confirmed mine damage in the preceding 3–4 weeks.
- Proxy activity: Near-zero attacks by Iran-aligned groups (Houthis and others) on Gulf shipping, ports, or energy infrastructure in the preceding month.
- Shipping security: AIS-visible commercial transits through Hormuz at ≥60–70% of the pre-crisis baseline of ~85/day (IMF PortWatch/hormuz.now figures), without routine US naval escorts or widespread dark sailing as the primary safety method. Iran does not maintain a functional permit/control regime that ships must observe.
- Commercial transit levels: Consistent with the above; not reliant on opaque ship-to-ship transfers or extreme risk tolerance by a minority of operators.
- War-risk insurance: Additional premiums for Hormuz/Gulf tankers ≤1% of hull value (or ≤4× the pre-war ~0.25% baseline), widely available on standard terms without special government facilities or 7-day cancellation clauses dominating pricing.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Flows through Hormuz (including dark/STS where relevant) at ≥80% of the ~17–21 mb/d pre-war liquids baseline for a sustained period; LNG carriers operating normally. Bypass pipelines remain available but are not the dominant workaround forced by risk.
- Energy market stress: Brent (or equivalent) risk premium largely dissipated; prices driven primarily by fundamentals rather than acute war-risk spikes above ~$10–15/bbl attributable to Hormuz.
- Force posture: US naval blockade of Iranian ports lifted or substantially eased; major combatant naval presence reduced from wartime levels; no active Iranian claim of exclusive control over the strait that is enforced by force.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No large-scale strikes on land infrastructure, nuclear-related moves, undersea cable attacks, or equivalent new fronts in the preceding month.

Temporary pauses, partial traffic under escort, or oil price moves alone do not meet the threshold.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot (as of 23 October 2026)**

The conflict began ~28 February 2026 (~Day 238 of the Hormuz crisis). A June 2026 US-Iran MOU collapsed in July. Indirect talks via Qatar and Pakistan continue but remain stalled on sequencing (Iran demands blockade/sanctions relief and related conditions first; US prioritizes strait reopening and related security steps). Trump has indicated no major US strikes on Iran before the 3 November midterms.

AIS-visible commercial traffic is ~4% of the ~85/day pre-crisis baseline (3.6/day recent 7-day average; hormuz.now). CENTCOM and Lloyd’s List report higher numbers of facilitated or non-Iranian-linked transits in some weeks (e.g., claims of ~400 in a week or 100+ non-Iranian in mid-October periods), largely under US coordination, often dark, hugging Omani routes, or via STS transfers in the Gulf of Oman. Iran claims control, maintains a non-compliance list, and requires permits. October has seen the highest attack numbers of the war (IMO/USNI: 27 commercial ship attacks so far in October; multiple reports of 12–15 in single weeks; at least two sinkings including GFS Pride). Mine claims by IRGC are frequent but often disputed by CENTCOM (primary lanes reported cleared). 

Oil flows through Hormuz (including dark/STS) have recovered to roughly 70–82% of pre-war levels in recent Kpler/related estimates (~11–14 mb/d range in various weekly figures), with overall Middle East exports sometimes matching or exceeding pre-war totals via bypass pipelines (Saudi East-West, Emirati). Tanker freight rates remain extremely elevated (~12× pre-war for some routes). Brent is in the $108–113 range (five-month highs), well above pre-war ~$70–80 levels, with a clear war-risk premium. 

War-risk insurance for regional tankers is 6–10% of hull value (up from ~0.25% pre-war; multiple of 15–40× reported in various broker/FT/Insurance Business accounts), with claims estimated in the $2–3 billion range. Cover is available at a price. Houthi activity has expanded threats into the Red Sea and against Saudi infrastructure. US forces continue escorts, mine clearance, and the Iranian blockade; IRGC remains active. Evidence quality is mixed: AIS undercounts dark traffic; attack numbers and mine claims are partially disputed; oil flow trackers diverge; official statements from both sides are maximalist.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant classes (outside-in):

- 1980s Tanker War (Iran-Iraq, ~1984–88): Sustained attacks on neutral shipping (hundreds of incidents), high insurance, US reflagging/escorts (Operation Earnest Will 1987–88). Shipping continued at reduced/risk-adjusted levels. Full political end came with the August 1988 ceasefire; escorts wound down shortly after. Recovery of commercial normality took months post-ceasefire. Relevant for endurance of maritime pressure and role of escorts; breaks down because it was a bilateral regional war without a nuclear dimension or modern drone/missile density, and ended via mutual exhaustion + UN process. Suggests multi-year disruption possible; functional shipping recovery mainly after political settlement.
- 2023–24 Red Sea Houthi campaign: Prolonged elevated risk, insurance spikes, partial rerouting (Cape), naval escorts, incomplete normalization even after a year. Relevant for proxy persistence and insurer caution; breaks down because Hormuz has fewer easy bypasses and higher oil concentration. Suggests 12–18+ months of elevated disruption is common absent resolution.
- Brief 2019–20 Iran tanker incidents and similar spikes: Insurance and traffic normalized within weeks to a few months once kinetic activity subsided. Relevant for rapid reversion if activity stops; breaks down for sustained multi-month wars.
- 1991 Gulf War: Short, high-intensity; shipping recovered quickly post-conflict. Relevant for rapid post-war reversion; less applicable to a prolonged, attritional, multi-domain standoff.
- Suez Canal closures (1956, 1967–75): Multi-year physical or effective closures with permanent rerouting adaptations. Relevant for chokepoint duration; less so for a contested but not fully closed Hormuz.

Base-rate prior (before current-evidence adjustment), for a multi-month active chokepoint conflict involving a nuclear-capable state, proxies, and a major power: very low short-term probability of the full operational definition being met; rising slowly with time conditional on de-escalation. Approximate prior: 1 week ~2%, 1 month ~5%, 3 months ~12%, 6 months ~25%, 1 year ~40%, 18 months ~55%. These reflect historical tendency for such disruptions to outlast initial expectations and for commercial/insurance normalization to lag military pauses.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

Ranked: (1) Mutual exhaustion and economic costs (high insurance, lost Iranian export revenue, elevated global energy prices); (2) US electoral calendar creating a window for de-escalation post-midterms; (3) Partial technical success of US escorts/STS/bypass systems keeping some oil moving and reducing urgency for total closure; (4) Continued mediation channels (Qatar, Pakistan).

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

Ranked: (1) Iranian incentive to maintain Hormuz leverage as primary bargaining chip (explicit conditions for reopening); (2) Demonstrated Iranian capacity and willingness to sustain attacks (October peak eight months in); (3) Proxy activation and multi-front risk (Houthis); (4) Insurer and operator caution that lags any military pause; (5) Sequencing distrust and history of MOU collapse; (6) Risk of post-election escalation.

**8. Decomposed Sub-Forecasts**

Rough probabilities that the sub-component is met by each horizon (these are inputs, not averages):

- Major reduction in direct strikes: 1w 5%, 1m 15%, 3m 30%, 6m 45%, 1y 60%, 18m 70%.
- Sustained safe commercial transit ≥60–70% without routine escorts: 1w 2%, 1m 8%, 3m 20%, 6m 35%, 1y 50%, 18m 65%.
- Meaningful insurance normalization (≤1% / ≤4×): 1w 1%, 1m 5%, 3m 15%, 6m 30%, 1y 45%, 18m 60%.
- Oil/LNG export recovery to definition: 1w 10%, 1m 25%, 3m 40%, 6m 55%, 1y 70%, 18m 75% (partial recovery already exists).
- Proxy restraint: 1w 10%, 1m 20%, 3m 35%, 6m 50%, 1y 60%, 18m 70%.
- Force drawdown (blockade lift + reduced posture): 1w 2%, 1m 8%, 3m 20%, 6m 35%, 1y 50%, 18m 60%.
- No new major escalation trigger: 1w 40%, 1m 35%, 3m 40%, 6m 50%, 1y 55%, 18m 60%.

Gating factors are insurance normalization, transit without escorts, and force posture/blockade—these lag kinetic pauses and require sustained political settlement. Oil flows are the least gating.

**9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon**

Main gating factor: Ongoing direct attacks and insurance pricing.  
What would raise this forecast materially: Immediate verified multi-day halt in attacks + rapid insurance quotes falling.  
What would lower this forecast materially: Further sinkings or confirmed mine damage.  
Confidence in this estimate: High

Main gating factor: Insurance and sustained transit without escorts.

What would lower this forecast materially: Post-election escalation or new proxy front.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued contested traffic under US facilitation with elevated attacks through late 2026, possible limited de-escalation window after midterms, incomplete political deal by early-mid 2027, gradual (not full) commercial recovery with persistently elevated insurance and residual risk into 2027. Full functional normality more likely in the 12–18 month window than sooner, contingent on political resolution.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Post-midterm deal that sequences blockade relief with attack halt, rapid mine clearance confirmation, and insurer response within weeks. Oil flows already partially recovered accelerate the rest. Probability of this path is low but non-zero if economic pain on both sides is judged decisive.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Iran maintains calibrated attacks to preserve leverage; proxies remain active; insurance stays high; any pauses collapse as in the June MOU. Bypass adaptations and STS become semi-permanent. Functional normality delayed well beyond 18 months. Consistent with Tanker War endurance and Red Sea experience.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Strongest case for faster normalization: Partial oil recovery already shows resilience; economic costs are high; electoral calendar creates a natural pause; insurers can reprice quickly once attacks stop. Strongest case for prolonged instability: Iran has shown it can sustain attacks eight months in; Hormuz is its strongest lever; proxies add independent risk; historical insurance lags are long; sequencing distrust is deep. I may be slightly underestimating short-term (1–3 month) probabilities if a quiet period begins immediately after midterms and is sustained. I may be overestimating longer-horizon probabilities if low-level attacks become a chronic equilibrium rather than a temporary bargaining tool. Load-bearing assumptions: that political settlement is necessary for the full definition, and that Iranian leverage incentives remain strong absent major regime pressure.
