**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by the horizons of 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months (from 7 August 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality is defined as the simultaneous, observable satisfaction of all the following conditions for a sustained period of at least 14 consecutive days (not a single-day snapshot or temporary pause):

- Direct military activity: No major reciprocal strikes (missile, drone, air, or naval) between Iran and the US/Israel/Gulf states involving more than isolated incidents; no sustained campaign of the scale seen in July–early August 2026.
- Proxy activity: No significant Houthi, Iraqi militia, or Hezbollah attacks on shipping, energy infrastructure, or US/Gulf forces beyond pre-war baseline levels.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Commercial vessels transit without routine kinetic attacks, mining threats, or forced diversions; AIS “dark” transits fall below 20% of total; UKMTO/JMIC threat level returns to moderate or lower and remains there.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits through Hormuz average ≥80% of the pre-war baseline (~100–120+ vessels/day, depending on source), sustained.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums return to ≤2–3% of hull value (pre-war range was typically 0.5–2% in quiet periods; recent levels 7.5–10%); cover is widely available without exclusion or extreme deductibles for standard Gulf/Hormuz routes.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Combined crude/condensate and LNG flows from Gulf producers (Saudi, UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Iraq, Iran where permitted) recover to ≥85% of February 2026 pre-war averages on a 7-day moving average, with no major terminal outages attributable to conflict.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent 30-day realized volatility returns to levels consistent with non-crisis periods (typically <30–35 annualized in quiet Gulf conditions); no sustained risk premium >$10–15/bbl attributable to Hormuz insecurity.
- Force posture of major actors: US/CENTCOM force levels and operational tempo in the Gulf reduce from wartime surge to a steady-state presence not dominated by daily strike packages or active blockade enforcement; Iranian coastal anti-ship and FAC posture is no longer in continuous high-alert interdiction mode.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No successful large-scale attack on a major energy facility, carrier/strike group, or population center that restarts a multi-day reciprocal campaign; no formal declaration of full closure or permanent Iranian administrative control that is enforced kinetically.

A temporary ceasefire, reduced oil prices alone, or a battlefield pause without commercial and insurance normalization does **not** meet the definition. All domains must be concurrently functional.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant reference classes (selected for chokepoint disruption + regional conflict intensity):

1. **Iran-Iraq War tanker war phase (1984–1988)**: Sustained attacks on shipping in Gulf/Hormuz approaches. Partial commercial adaptation occurred within months via convoys and rerouting, but full functional normality (low premiums, high voluntary transit, low kinetic risk) required war end + years. Suggests multi-year residual risk even after major combat declines. Breaks down because no full US-Iran direct war and different oil market structure.
2. **2019 Gulf tanker attacks / Abqaiq-Khurais / Iranian seizures**: Discrete Iranian/proxy actions produced weeks-to-months of elevated premiums and cautious transit; normalization of commercial behavior in 1–3 months once kinetic tempo dropped, but underlying deterrence remained fragile. Breaks down due to far lower intensity and no multi-month full conflict.
3. **Houthi Red Sea campaign (late 2023–ongoing into 2026)**: Shipping volumes collapsed, premiums spiked, many operators avoided the route for 12–18+ months despite intermittent de-escalation talks and limited kinetic reduction. Partial recovery only after sustained naval presence + some operational degradation of threat. Closest modern chokepoint analogue for commercial/insurance lag. Breaks down because Red Sea is not the primary Gulf energy artery and attacker is a proxy rather than a state with nuclear threshold status.
4. **1990–91 Gulf War + residual Iraqi threats**: Major combat phase short; shipping and insurance normalized within months of ceasefire, but residual mining/strike risk and force posture elevated for longer. Breaks down because coalition victory was clearer and Iraqi residual capability lower.
5. **Post-2003 Iraq insurgency maritime/energy attacks and 2011–2015 Libyan disruptions**: Commercial recovery lagged kinetic peaks by 6–18 months due to insurance and operator risk aversion. Useful for second-order commercial lag.
6. **Broader post-major Middle East wars (1973, 1980s, 2006 Lebanon)**: Energy market risk premiums and shipping caution often persisted 6–24 months after major fighting subsided.

**Base-rate prior (pre-case-specific evidence) for meeting the full multi-domain definition**:
- 1 week: ~1–3%
- 1 month: ~5–10%
- 3 months: ~15–25%
- 6 months: ~30–40%
- 1 year: ~45–55%
- 18 months: ~55–65%

These reflect the historical lag between kinetic reduction and commercial/insurance normalization, plus the high bar of the operational definition. Gulf conflicts rarely produce clean, rapid multi-domain resets.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

Ranked by importance:
1. Mutual exhaustion + political incentives for a durable Hormuz arrangement (US domestic costs, Iranian economic damage, Gulf state pressure).
2. Successful Iran-Oman (or broader) technical route + coordination mechanism that both sides actually implement and that operators trust.
3. Sustained reduction in kinetic tempo allowing insurance markets and shipowners to recalibrate.
4. Visible degradation of Iranian maritime interdiction capacity from repeated US strikes.
5. Gulf state mediation and quiet security guarantees that reduce proxy activation risk.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

Ranked by importance:
1. Core Iranian interest in retaining de facto veto/control leverage over Hormuz as strategic compensation for military losses and regime survival narrative.
2. Fragility of prior ceasefires/MOUs and rapid return to tit-for-tat after perceived violations.
3. Commercial and insurance lag: even after kinetic reduction, operators and underwriters require sustained evidence of safety (weeks to months of clean transit data).
4. Proxy autonomy and residual capability (Houthis, others) that can independently disrupt.
5. Domestic political constraints on both sides (US “win” narrative vs Iranian hardliner/IRGC resistance to perceived capitulation).
6. Residual mines, coastal defenses, and the difficulty of verifying full demining/stand-down.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Intermittent negotiation progress produces one or more temporary reductions in kinetic tempo and partial transit recovery over the next 1–3 months. Full multi-domain normality remains elusive through mid-2027 because Iranian incentives to retain leverage, commercial/insurance lag, and proxy residual risk prevent simultaneous satisfaction of all definitional elements. Gradual improvement continues, with the definition most likely first met in the 12–18 month window if no major new trigger occurs.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

A durable Iran-Oman (or expanded) arrangement is implemented quickly, US measures ease in parallel, kinetic tempo drops sharply and stays low for 4–6 weeks, insurers reprice rapidly on clean data, and Gulf states quietly reinforce deterrence against proxies. Probability of this path producing definition-level normality by 3–6 months: roughly 15–20% conditional on early deal success.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Talks stall or produce only cosmetic agreements; low-level ship attacks or mining incidents continue; premiums stay elevated; operators continue partial avoidance; periodic US-Iran strike cycles resume after perceived violations; proxies exploit the environment. This keeps the definition unmet beyond 18 months with meaningful probability (~25–30%).

**13. Red-Team Critique**

The forecast may **overestimate** normalization at 6–18 months by under-weighting Iran’s demonstrated willingness to absorb costs for Hormuz leverage and the stickiness of insurance/operator behavior (Red Sea precedent). It may **underestimate** short-to-medium-term progress if political incentives on the US side produce a more durable arrangement than prior MOUs and if repeated strikes have already degraded Iranian maritime interdiction more than publicly visible. Load-bearing assumptions include: (a) commercial/insurance domains lag kinetic ones, (b) Iranian core interest in control persists, and (c) the operational definition’s simultaneity requirement is binding. Evidence that would most falsify the central view: sustained high transit volumes + rapid premium collapse while kinetic activity remains non-zero, or conversely a clean kinetic pause that still fails to move insurance/volumes for >6 months.
