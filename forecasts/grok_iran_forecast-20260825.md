**1. Forecast Question**  
Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by the horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months (from ~25 August 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
Functional normality is achieved only when **all** of the following hold simultaneously and sustainably (not as a temporary pause or partial rebound):

- Direct military activity: No sustained US/Israeli/Coalition airstrikes on Iranian territory or Iranian missile/drone/coastal attacks on US/partner forces or major Gulf infrastructure for ≥30 consecutive days.
- Proxy activity: No significant Houthi or other Axis-linked attacks on commercial shipping in the Red Sea/Bab al-Mandeb or Gulf approaches that disrupt routes at scale.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Zero successful attacks on commercial vessels for ≥30 days; mines cleared or effectively neutralized in primary lanes; no active IRGC enforcement of exclusive “approved” routes via force or boarding.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily commodity vessel transits through Hormuz sustained at ≥70% of pre-war baseline (~95–130/day range, targeting ≥70–90/day average over a rolling 14-day period), with majority of traffic using transparent AIS and conventional TSS/IMO-adjacent routes rather than “dark” or highly constrained corridors.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums for Hormuz transit ≤1.0–1.5× peacetime baseline (pre-war ~0.15–0.25% of hull value); cargo war risk and capacity broadly available without US government backstops as primary enabler; JWC listings normalized or premiums no longer prohibitive for mainstream operators.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Combined Persian Gulf crude/condensate + LNG exports via Hormuz or equivalent reliable alternatives sustained at ≥80% of pre-war volumes (~15–20 mbpd liquids equivalent range), without force majeure declarations dominating Qatar/UAE/Saudi/Kuwait/Iraqi flows.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent/WTI not systematically elevated >$20–25 above pre-war trend solely due to Hormuz risk premium; implied volatility and crack spreads reflecting structural rather than acute chokepoint fear.
- Force posture of major actors: US/Coalition naval presence in the Gulf/Oman Sea reduced from wartime blockade/escort intensity toward pre-war levels or a stable, lower-intensity presence; Iranian coastal and naval forces not postured for immediate mining/boarding campaigns.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new large-scale Iranian attacks on GCC states, no US “economic D-Day” secondary sanctions cascade that collapses remaining Chinese offtake of Iranian barrels in a way that forces kinetic response, and no nuclear breakout or regime-collapse dynamics that restart full-scale exchanges.

A temporary ceasefire, partial Omani-route corridor under escort, lower oil prices from demand destruction, or battlefield pause do **not** constitute normality.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot (as of ~25 August 2026)**  
The war began ~28 February 2026 with US-Israeli strikes that killed Supreme Leader Khamenei and degraded Iranian military/nuclear infrastructure. Iran responded by effectively closing/severely disrupting the Strait of Hormuz via threats, attacks, and mines. A June 17 US-Iran Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) produced a temporary rebound (transits averaging ~36/day during the window; oil flows briefly higher) but collapsed after 8 July when attacks resumed and Trump declared the MOU “over.”  

As of late August: commodity transits average ~10–15/day (vs ~95 pre-war), with large shares “dark”; outbound oil flows estimated ~5 mbpd or lower (vs ~15–20 mbpd pre-war); war-risk premiums reported at ~40× peacetime or high single-digit percent of hull value, pricing most commercial traffic out; ~400–500 vessels and thousands of seafarers remain stranded or delayed; Brent ~$91–94; US maintains a naval blockade of Iranian ports and claims “total control” of the strait (including rhetoric treating it as US territory); Iran insists on sovereignty/control and conditions (lift blockade, sanctions relief, assets) before reopening; low-level vessel attacks continue; Oman and Pakistan mediate; US Treasury has launched “economic D-Day” measures. **4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant classes (with breakdowns):

1. **1980s Tanker War (Iran-Iraq)**: Prolonged attacks on shipping in the Gulf; insurance spiked; traffic continued under risk but never fully normalized until the 1988 ceasefire and subsequent political settlement. Partial commercial adaptation occurred within months; full functional normality took 1–3+ years post-ceasefire. Breaks down because it was bilateral Iran-Iraq without superpower direct involvement or nuclear overlay, and no full closure.
2. **2019–2020 Gulf tanker attacks / Abqaiq / Soleimani period**: Iranian-linked attacks caused insurance spikes and temporary diversions; rapid US/partner naval response and de-escalation restored near-normal traffic within weeks to a few months once kinetic intensity dropped. Breaks down: limited duration and no sustained Hormuz closure or leadership decapitation.
3. **2023–2025 Red Sea / Houthi campaign**: Persistent proxy attacks reduced traffic dramatically; insurance elevated; partial rerouting and naval task forces produced incomplete recovery lasting >18 months with traffic still degraded. Breaks down: secondary chokepoint, not primary energy artery, and lower state capacity.
4. **2011–2012 and episodic Iranian Hormuz threats**: Verbal threats and limited mining/boarding produced short-lived spikes; markets and shipping largely ignored after days/weeks when no sustained action followed. Breaks down: no actual multi-month closure or major war.
5. **Post-major-war maritime recovery (e.g., after 1991 Gulf War mine clearance, or post-WWII European ports)**: Physical clearance and political settlement enabled 50–80% recovery in 1–6 months; full commercial/insurance normality often 6–18 months. Breaks down: clearer victors and less contested sovereignty claims.
6. **Prolonged sanctions + limited kinetic (Iran 2018–2023 “maximum pressure”)**: Shadow fleets and dark shipping adapted; formal commercial/insurance channels stayed constrained for years. Relevant for the residual Iranian export channel.

**Base-rate prior (pre-case evidence, approximate cumulative probability of full functional normality):**  
1 week: <1–2%; 1 month: 5–10%; 3 months: 15–25%; 6 months: 30–45%; 1 year: 45–60%; 18 months: 55–70%. These reflect that major chokepoint wars and contested sovereignty disputes rarely resolve to commercial normality inside one quarter and often leave residual risk for 12–24 months.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Mutual economic pain (high oil prices, Iranian isolation, GCC losses, US midterm/political pressure) creating incentive for a limited Hormuz governance deal.  
2. US naval superiority enabling a protected southern/Omani corridor that gradually de-risks traffic even without full Iranian consent.  
3. Third-party mediation (Oman, Pakistan, Qatar) producing a technical route-management arrangement that both sides can claim as victory.  
4. Insurance market adaptation + possible US/government reinsurance facilities lowering effective costs.  
5. Iranian domestic economic strain under sustained blockade/sanctions forcing pragmatism on the strait.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Zero-sum contest over sovereignty/control of Hormuz (Iran demands recognition/role; US rejects any Iranian administrative authority or tolls).  
2. Residual Iranian asymmetric capacity (mines, drones, coastal missiles, small boats) that can reimpose costs at low cost even after a pause.  
3. Political incentives on both sides to avoid appearing to concede (US “total control” rhetoric; Iranian hardline consolidation post-Khamenei).  
4. Secondary effects (Houthi activation, Lebanon front, Chinese offtake decisions under secondary sanctions) that can re-escalate.  
5. Insurance and shipowner memory effects: once risk is priced at 40×, reversion is slow even after kinetic activity drops.  
6. Nuclear and regime-survival dimensions that keep the conflict existential for Tehran.

- Main gating factor: Absence of any credible de-escalatory agreement or sustained zero-attack window.

- What would lower: New vessel attacks or major kinetic exchange.

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Continued low-intensity contest over Hormuz governance for several more months. Occasional vessel incidents and diplomatic flurries. Gradual increase in escorted or dark southern-route traffic under US naval cover. Insurance remains elevated. A limited technical arrangement (Oman-involved) emerges in the 3–9 month window that raises volumes toward 40–60% of normal without resolving sovereignty. Full multi-domain normality arrives, if at all, closer to the 12–18 month mark via fatigue and market adaptation rather than a clean political victory.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
A package deal in the next 4–8 weeks: US eases blockade/sanctions pressure in exchange for Iranian acceptance of free transit without exclusive control or tolls; Oman guarantees a dual-route system; visible demining and 45+ days of zero attacks trigger rapid insurance relief and shipowner return. Volumes recover to 70%+ by month 3–4. Probability of this path: low (~15–20% conditional on near-term diplomacy succeeding).

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Positions harden. “Economic D-Day” secondary sanctions collapse remaining Iranian oil offtake; Tehran responds with intensified mining/drone campaigns or proxy activation. US maintains high naval presence. Traffic stays <30% for 9–12+ months; insurance stays prohibitive; energy markets price a structural Gulf risk premium. Normality delayed beyond 18 months. Probability: meaningful (~25–30%).

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Overestimation risk: The definition is strict; markets and navies have historically adapted faster than pure base rates suggest once kinetic intensity falls (2019 precedent). US naval dominance may create a de-facto safe corridor faster than modeled, and Iranian economic pain is severe. Underestimation risk: The sovereignty dispute is more intractable than prior Tanker War or Red Sea cases; insurance memory and secondary sanctions feedback loops can lock in elevated risk for years; nuclear and succession dynamics inside Iran can reintroduce high-stakes escalation at any time. The forecast may under-weight the possibility that “functional normality” is simply redefined downward by markets to a permanently higher-risk, lower-volume equilibrium.
