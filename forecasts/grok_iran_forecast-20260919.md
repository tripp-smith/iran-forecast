**1. Forecast Question**  
Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by horizons of 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months (as of 19 September 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
Functional normality requires simultaneous, observable satisfaction of all the following for a sustained period (not a single-day snapshot or temporary pause):

- Direct military activity: No sustained ballistic missile, cruise missile, or large-scale drone salvos by Iran against Gulf states, US/allied bases, or commercial shipping; no reciprocal major US/coalition strikes on Iranian territory or maritime assets aimed at degrading Hormuz control. Isolated incidents do not disqualify if frequency and scale are at or below pre-February 2026 baseline.
- Proxy activity: No significant Houthi, Iraqi militia, or other Iran-aligned attacks that materially disrupt Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb or Gulf shipping/energy infrastructure beyond pre-war levels.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Freedom of navigation restored such that commercial vessels transit without systematic Iranian permit/toll regimes, mine threats, or attack risk at levels that deter major operators. Confirmed transit rates (AIS + independent trackers such as IMF PortWatch/Kpler) sustainably ≥60–70% of pre-war daily average (~85 commodity vessels/day baseline).
- Commercial transit levels: Sustained recovery of tanker, LNG, and dry bulk volumes through Hormuz approaching pre-war norms, with clearance of major backlogs and resumption of regular liner/container services by major carriers.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull and cargo war-risk premiums return to ≤2–3× pre-war baseline (pre-war ~0.1–0.25% of hull value per voyage) and remain available from major markets without exclusion or extreme loading that prices out most commercial traffic.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Persian Gulf crude/products and Qatari/UAE LNG exports recover to ≥85–90% of pre-war volumes on a sustained basis (accounting for bypass pipelines), with force-majeure declarations largely lifted and production not materially constrained by security.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent and related benchmarks no longer embed a large, persistent Hormuz-risk premium; volatility measures normalize relative to non-crisis periods; strategic stock draws and emergency measures wind down.
- Force posture of major actors: Significant de-escalation in US/CENTCOM and Iranian naval/missile posture in the Gulf (reduced high-tempo escort, blockade enforcement, or IRGC “Strait Authority” operations); no new permanent escalatory basing or exclusion zones.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new large-scale attacks on critical energy infrastructure (pipelines, ports, desalination, nuclear facilities), no regime-destabilizing internal Iranian events that prompt external intervention, and no expansion that re-closes alternative routes (Red Sea/Yanbu, Fujairah).

A temporary ceasefire, lower oil prices alone, battlefield pause, or partial traffic recovery without insurance/commercial confidence does **not** qualify. All domains must move together; shipping/insurance/export continuity are gating.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot (19 September 2026, ~Day 203)**  
War began ~28 February 2026 with US/Israeli strikes. Multiple ceasefires and a June Islamabad MOU (60-day negotiating window) collapsed amid renewed shipping attacks, base strikes, and Iranian assertions of control over Hormuz. As of mid-September: Hormuz traffic ~5–9% of pre-war (IMF PortWatch ~5 arrivals/day 7-day average; single-digit daily commodity transits common); recent fire pause of ~8–9 days with no major Iranian ballistic/drone salvos on host states; intermittent merchant vessel strikes continue; Saudi East-West pipeline attacked (early September, temporarily shut); Houthis consolidating Red Sea coast control, complicating bypasses; war-risk premiums still elevated (reports of 15× baseline or higher in places, historically peaking near 10% of hull); Brent ~$99–106 range with residual risk premium; Gulf production/exports still materially constrained relative to February baseline despite some bypass utilization and non-Gulf offsets; Iranian economy under severe stress; US midterms in ~7 weeks; no durable political settlement. Confirmed facts (traffic data, pause duration, pipeline incident) outweigh contested claims of “control” or “reopening.”

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant classes (adjusted for scale, duration, and multi-domain commercial impact):

- Tanker War (Iran-Iraq, 1980–88, especially 1984–88 peak): Prolonged asymmetric attacks on shipping; traffic reduced but never fully halted for months; insurance spikes; full commercial normalization took years after the 1988 ceasefire and required political settlement + force protection. Suggests multi-year tail for full normality after major phase ends. Breaks down: no modern precision missiles/drones at scale, different global energy market.
- Red Sea/Houthi disruption (2023–ongoing, intensified 2024–25): Partial chokepoint closure via proxy; major carriers suspended; insurance spiked; traffic down sharply for 12+ months; only partial recovery with escorts and some de-escalation. Suggests 6–18+ months for meaningful commercial recovery even after kinetic peak. Breaks down: secondary route, not primary oil artery; less direct great-power involvement.
- 2019 Gulf tanker attacks + Abqaiq: Short, sharp disruption; insurance spiked briefly; rapid return once kinetic risk subsided and attribution clear. Suggests weeks–months if political will and deterrence restore confidence quickly. Breaks down: limited duration/scale, no sustained closure or multi-front war.
- Suez Canal blockages/closures (historical, e.g., 1956/1967/1973 periods and 2021 Ever Given): Physical or political closure; traffic recovers in days–weeks once physical access restored, but insurance and routing confidence lag. Suggests faster commercial recovery than kinetic war. Breaks down: no ongoing combatant control or mine/missile threat.
- Broader post-major Middle East wars (1991 Gulf War aftermath, 2003 Iraq invasion early phases): Energy infrastructure and shipping normalized over 3–12 months once major combat ended and security guarantees held, but residual risk premiums and force posture lingered. Suggests 6–18 months for multi-domain normality.

**Base-rate prior (before case-specific evidence):** Very low short-horizon probabilities for full multi-domain normality given the sustained nature of prior disruptions. Approximate priors: 1 week <5%; 1 month ~5–10%; 3 months ~15–25%; 6 months ~30–40%; 1 year ~45–55%; 18 months ~55–65%. These are pulled downward by the Tanker War/Red Sea classes (long commercial tails) and upward modestly by shorter 2019-style recoveries when kinetic pressure eases.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**  
Ranked: (1) Durable political settlement or extended, verifiable ceasefire that removes Iranian incentive/ability to contest Hormuz commercially; (2) Sustained absence of attacks + mine clearance + credible escort/force posture that restores insurer and operator confidence; (3) Clearance of shipping backlog and return of major carriers; (4) Iranian economic pressure + US political calendar creating deal space; (5) Alternative route stabilization (Red Sea, pipelines).

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**  
Ranked: (1) Iranian strategic interest in retaining Hormuz as leverage (nuclear, sanctions, regime survival); (2) Mutual distrust and history of collapsed agreements; (3) Proxy/secondary fronts (Houthis, militias) that can re-escalate cheaply; (4) Insurance market inertia and operator risk aversion after 200+ days; (5) Domestic US political constraints and Iranian internal dynamics that reward hardline postures; (6) Physical residual threats (mines, degraded but residual capabilities).

**8. Decomposed Sub-Forecasts** (approximate probabilities of each component holding by horizon; gating factors noted)

- Major reduction in direct strikes: 1w 25%; 1m 40%; 3m 55%; 6m 65%; 1y 70%; 18m 75%.  
- Sustained safe commercial transit (≥60–70% pre-war): 1w <5%; 1m 10%; 3m 25%; 6m 40%; 1y 55%; 18m 65%. (Gating.)  
- Meaningful insurance normalization (≤2–3× baseline, available): 1w <5%; 1m 10%; 3m 20%; 6m 35%; 1y 50%; 18m 60%. (Gating; lags kinetic.)  
- Oil/LNG export recovery (≥85–90%): 1w 10%; 1m 20%; 3m 35%; 6m 50%; 1y 65%; 18m 75%.  
- Proxy restraint: 1w 30%; 1m 40%; 3m 50%; 6m 55%; 1y 60%; 18m 65%.  
- Meaningful force drawdown/posture shift: 1w <10%; 1m 15%; 3m 30%; 6m 45%; 1y 55%; 18m 65%.  
- No new major escalation trigger: 1w 60%; 1m 50%; 3m 45%; 6m 40%; 1y 35%; 18m 30% (declining as time accumulates opportunity for shocks).

Synthesis weights shipping security + insurance + export continuity most heavily as commercial gates; kinetic pause alone is insufficient.

**9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon**

**1 Week**  
- Probability: 2%  
- Base-rate anchor: <5%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight downward (traffic still single digits, insurance elevated, recent strikes history)  
- Core rationale: No mechanism exists for multi-domain recovery in 7 days; even a perfect pause leaves insurance, backlog, and operator confidence untouched.  
- Main gating factor: Insurance and operator willingness.  
- What would raise: Sudden, verified comprehensive deal + immediate traffic surge + premium collapse.  
- What would lower: Any new strike or pipeline/Red Sea incident.  
- Confidence: High  

**1 Month**  
- Probability: 8%  
- Base-rate anchor: 5–10%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Neutral to slight downward  
- Core rationale: Possible kinetic pause extension and partial traffic uptick, but commercial/insurance normalization historically lags and current backlog + risk aversion are severe.  
- Main gating factor: Credible, sustained transit security + insurance response.  
- What would raise: Verified extended MOU implementation + mine clearance progress + major carrier returns.  
- What would lower: Renewed shipping attacks or Houthi escalation.  
- Confidence: Medium-High  

**3 Months**  
- Probability: 22%  
- Base-rate anchor: 15–25%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight downward (midterms uncertainty, proxy risks)  
- Core rationale: Window for post-midterm deal-making or exhaustion-driven pause; still requires insurance and operator confidence that have proven sticky.  
- Main gating factor: Political settlement durability + insurance normalization.  
- What would raise: Binding agreement with monitoring + sustained traffic ≥50% + premium compression.  
- What would lower: Post-election escalation or major infrastructure attack.  
- Confidence: Medium  

**6 Months**  
- Probability: 38%  
- Base-rate anchor: 30–40%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Neutral  
- Core rationale: Time allows for negotiated off-ramp, force posture adjustment, and partial commercial recovery if kinetic intensity stays low; reference classes show this is plausible but not majority.  
- Main gating factor: Iranian willingness to relinquish effective commercial control of Hormuz.  
- What would raise: Durable settlement + clear force drawdown signals.  
- What would lower: Entrenchment of dual control / toll regimes or proxy expansion.  
- Confidence: Medium  

**1 Year**  
- Probability: 52%  
- Base-rate anchor: 45–55%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Neutral  
- Core rationale: Cumulative economic pressure, political calendars, and war fatigue raise odds of a workable arrangement; still below high confidence because Iranian leverage incentives and insurance/operator memory persist.  
- Main gating factor: Whether a political settlement survives testing.  
- What would raise: Verified multi-year framework + sustained high transit + normalized premiums.  
- What would lower: Regime hardening or new external shocks.  
- Confidence: Medium  

**18 Months**  
- Probability: 63%  
- Base-rate anchor: 55–65%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight upward (longer horizon allows adaptation and settlement)  
- Core rationale: Most historical chokepoint/war disruptions eventually see commercial normalization within this window once major combat ends, but residual risk and new baselines (higher insurance floors, alternative routing) mean “functional normality” under the strict definition is still not highly probable.  
- Main gating factor: Absence of renewed major escalation after any interim deal.  
- What would raise: Structural settlement + demonstrated multi-month safe transit.  
- What would lower: Protracted low-intensity contest or second-front expansion.  
- Confidence: Medium-Low (long-horizon uncertainty)

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Extended low-intensity contest or fragile pauses through late 2026/early 2027, with partial traffic recovery on escorted or selective routes, elevated but gradually easing insurance, and production constrained relative to pre-war. Full multi-domain normality most likely only after a political settlement that survives several tests, probably post-US political calendar resolution and under sustained economic pressure on Iran. Bypass and non-Gulf supply continue to cushion global markets, delaying but not eliminating the need for Hormuz recovery.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Rapid, verified comprehensive deal (nuclear restraints + sanctions relief + clear Hormuz arrangements) shortly after midterms, followed by rapid mine clearance, insurance market response, and operator return. Requires Iranian decision that leverage has peaked and US willingness to lock in gains. Probability of this path materializing is low but would lift shorter-horizon forecasts substantially.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Iran retains de-facto commercial veto via residual capabilities and proxies; US maintains pressure posture; insurance and operators treat the Gulf as permanently higher-risk; secondary fronts (Red Sea) compound. Normalization under the strict definition delayed beyond 18 months. Supported by Iranian strategic culture around Hormuz and history of collapsed interim deals.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Strongest case I am overestimating normalization: Iranian incentives to keep Hormuz contested are structural; insurance markets and operators have long memories; proxy options are cheap and deniable; reference classes with similar duration show multi-year commercial tails. Strongest case I am underestimating: Severe Iranian economic stress + US political incentives + demonstrated ability of markets to adapt via bypasses could produce faster practical recovery than historical kinetic wars. Load-bearing assumptions: (1) commercial/insurance domains remain gating even after kinetic pause; (2) no sudden regime or leadership change that resets Iranian policy; (3) Houthi/Red Sea front remains manageable. Evidence that would prove the forecast wrong: sustained ≥60% traffic + premium collapse to near-baseline for 30+ consecutive days without a formal deal, or a verified durable settlement that is tested and holds.
