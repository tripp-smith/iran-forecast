**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each of these horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality requires simultaneous satisfaction of all of the following observable conditions for a sustained period (not a single-day snapshot):

- Direct military activity: No kinetic exchanges (missile, drone, air, naval, or special operations strikes) between the US/coalition and Iran (or Iranian forces) inside Iranian territory, Gulf waters, or against US/partner bases in the Gulf region for at least 14 consecutive days, with no credible threats of imminent resumption.
- Proxy activity: Material reduction in Houthi, Iraqi militia, or other Iran-aligned attacks on commercial shipping, energy infrastructure, or US/partner forces in the Red Sea, Gulf of Oman, or Iraq/Syria theater (attacks at or below pre-February 2026 baseline rates).
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Commercial vessels transit without systematic harassment, boarding, drone/missile attacks, or mine threats; effective freedom of navigation restored on both Iranian and Omani-adjacent routes without compulsory Iranian approval or unilateral routing schemes enforced by force.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits through the Strait of Hormuz recover to ≥80% of pre-war baseline (~100–130 vessels/day range, depending on source) for a rolling 14-day average, including substantial non-Iranian/Chinese-flagged tanker and LNG traffic.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums for Hormuz transits fall to ≤1% of hull value (from current multi-percent levels) and remain available from mainstream London/US markets without widespread capacity withdrawal or special government reinsurance schemes as the primary mechanism.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf (Saudi, UAE, Kuwait, Qatar, Iraq) oil exports via Hormuz recover to ≥85% of pre-war volumes, with Qatari LNG loadings/transits similarly restored; Iranian exports may continue under whatever sanction regime exists but do not substitute for broader Gulf recovery.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent crude sustained below levels that reflect acute Hormuz risk premium (roughly consistent with fundamentals plus normal geopolitical risk, not the $90+ range driven by closure fears); forward curves and volatility metrics (e.g., option-implied vol) no longer price high probability of prolonged physical disruption.
- Force posture of major actors: No sustained elevated US/coalition naval blockade of Iranian ports or large-scale continuous strike packages; Iranian IRGC naval small-boat and coastal missile posture returns to non-blockade posture; no new major permanent basing or force deployments triggered solely by the crisis.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No Iranian nuclear breakout declaration/steps that trigger new strikes, no major attack on Gulf energy infrastructure (Abqaiq-scale or equivalent), no significant US or Israeli ground or expanded air campaign, and no effective secondary closure of Bab el-Mandeb that compounds Hormuz effects.

A temporary ceasefire, partial traffic recovery under Iranian-controlled routes, lower oil prices alone, or a battlefield pause without commercial and insurance normalization does not satisfy the definition.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant reference classes (3–6):

1. **Iran-Iraq Tanker War (1984–1988)**: Closest maritime precedent. Hundreds of attacks on shipping; traffic continued at reduced levels under escort/protection regimes (Earnest Will); full commercial normalization and risk-premium collapse only after the August 1988 ceasefire ended the broader war. Partial functional recovery of shipping took months to years under high protection costs; true insurance/commercial normality lagged the ceasefire. Suggests multi-month to multi-year timelines for full normality even after kinetic intensity drops. Breaks down because the current conflict involves direct US-Iran kinetic exchanges, nuclear overlay, and modern precision/ISR capabilities rather than primarily Iran-Iraq attrition.

2. **Houthi Red Sea campaign (2023–ongoing, intensified phases)**: Demonstrates how proxy maritime disruption can persist for 1–2+ years with only partial traffic recovery (Cape of Good Hope diversion as adaptation); insurance remains elevated; full normalization requires either sustained military suppression or political settlement with the sponsor. Relevant for proxy endurance and second-order insurance/shipping effects. Breaks down because Hormuz is a narrower, higher-volume oil chokepoint with greater global stakes and direct great-power involvement.

3. **Gulf War 1990–91 and post-liberation Kuwait oil recovery**: Rapid military resolution (weeks to months) followed by relatively fast commercial and energy recovery once kinetic phase ended and mines cleared, but insurance and residual risk lingered. Suggests that decisive military outcomes can compress timelines to 3–12 months for functional normality. Breaks down because 2026 features no clear decisive victory path and Iran retains significant residual denial capabilities.

4. **Post-2019 tanker attacks / Soleimani period and episodic Hormuz tensions**: Short spikes in insurance and traffic hesitation resolved in weeks to a few months once kinetic intensity fell and deterrence stabilized. Suggests faster partial recovery is possible under limited aims. Breaks down because the current war involves sustained high-intensity exchanges, regime survival stakes, and nuclear issues.

5. **Broader post-major-war chokepoint recoveries (e.g., Suez post-1956/1973, various mined-waterway clearances)**: Full commercial normality often requires 6–18+ months after kinetic end for demining, insurance reset, and shipper confidence.

Base-rate prior (before current-case adjustment), probability of meeting the full operational definition:

- 1 week: ~1–3%
- 1 month: ~5–10%
- 3 months: ~15–25%
- 6 months: ~30–40%
- 1 year: ~45–55%
- 18 months: ~55–65%

These reflect the historical pattern that maritime commercial normality lags military pauses, especially when one side views chokepoint control as core leverage and proxies remain active.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

Ranked by importance: (1) Mutual recognition that continued high-intensity kinetic activity imposes intolerable costs (oil prices, US domestic/political, Iranian economic/military attrition); (2) Durable agreement on strait management that allows free transit without Iranian veto or US blockade; (3) Effective suppression or restraint of proxies (Houthis especially); (4) Insurance market re-entry once kinetic pause exceeds ~2–4 weeks; (5) Gulf Arab states’ pipeline/rerouting capacity and political pressure for de-escalation; (6) Chinese interest in stable Iranian/Gulf oil flows.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

Ranked: (1) Iranian regime/IRGC view of Hormuz control as core residual strategic leverage and deterrent after conventional losses; (2) Domestic Iranian hardliner incentives and post-Khamenei succession dynamics favoring resistance narrative; (3) US domestic/political incentives under Trump administration for visible strength and incomplete deals; (4) Proxy autonomy and low-cost harassment options; (5) Nuclear issue linkage raising stakes of any settlement; (6) Structural insurance market stickiness (“quick up, slow down”); (7) Risk of accidental escalation or miscalculation in dense maritime/air environment.

Approximate probabilities that each major component is largely satisfied (by horizon):

- Major reduction in direct US-Iran strikes: 1w ~5%; 1m ~15%; 3m ~35%; 6m ~50%; 1y ~60%; 18m ~65%.
- Sustained safe commercial transit (freedom of navigation, high volumes): 1w <5%; 1m ~10%; 3m ~25%; 6m ~40%; 1y ~55%; 18m ~60%.
- Meaningful insurance normalization (≤1% hull, broad capacity): 1w ~0%; 1m ~5%; 3m ~20%; 6m ~35%; 1y ~50%; 18m ~55%.
- Oil/LNG export recovery (≥85% pre-war Gulf volumes via Hormuz): 1w ~5%; 1m ~15%; 3m ~30%; 6m ~45%; 1y ~55%; 18m ~60%.
- Proxy restraint: 1w ~10%; 1m ~20%; 3m ~35%; 6m ~45%; 1y ~55%; 18m ~60%.

Gating factors are shipping security + insurance + transit volumes; military pause is necessary but far from sufficient.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued low-to-medium intensity kinetic cycles and diplomatic fits-and-starts through late 2026. Partial traffic recovery under constrained conditions (Iranian-influenced routes, high insurance, selective shippers). Gradual adaptation via pipelines, strategic stocks, and demand destruction. Functional normality most likely arrives in the 9–18 month window if a more durable arrangement emerges from mutual exhaustion; earlier only under surprise diplomatic breakthrough.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Decisive US degradation of Iranian coastal missile/drone/small-boat systems combined with a face-saving diplomatic formula (Omani or multilateral management language that Iran can claim as recognition). Insurance capacity floods back within weeks of a verified pause. Probability of this path is low (~15–20% by 6 months) given current Iranian incentives and incomplete prior degradation.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Iran successfully enforces a de-facto approval/control regime; US maintains intermittent strikes and blockade elements; proxies sustain harassment; insurance stays elevated for 12+ months. Oil markets adapt via higher prices and substitution but never fully normalize commercial risk. This is the strongest alternative path (~30–40% probability of still failing the definition at 18 months).

**13. Red-Team Critique**

The forecast may overestimate longer-horizon normalization by underweighting Iranian regime survival incentives and the stickiness of insurance markets after multi-month high premiums. It may underestimate short-term recovery potential if a single durable pause triggers rapid shipper/insurance response (as briefly seen in late June). Load-bearing assumptions: (1) Iran values Hormuz leverage highly even after conventional losses; (2) full commercial normality requires more than a military pause; (3) proxies can be restrained or suppressed on relevant timelines. Evidence that would falsify: rapid, sustained traffic recovery to near-baseline under free navigation within 30–60 days without Iranian approval mechanisms.
