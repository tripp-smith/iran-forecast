**1. Forecast Question**  
Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each horizon: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
Functional normality requires simultaneous, sustained satisfaction of the following observable thresholds for at least 30 consecutive days (or the equivalent continuous period at longer horizons):

- **Direct military activity**: No sustained kinetic exchanges (airstrikes, missile/drone barrages, or naval engagements) between US/Israeli forces and Iranian forces beyond isolated defensive incidents; no large-scale Iranian mining or anti-ship campaigns.
- **Proxy activity**: Houthis, Iraqi militias, and residual Hezbollah elements refrain from major attacks on Gulf shipping, Saudi/UAE infrastructure, or US assets; limited low-level activity does not disrupt commercial patterns.
- **Shipping security (Strait of Hormuz and adjacent waters)**: Commercial vessels transit the strait and Gulf of Oman under standard routing (not exclusively night/Omani-coastal convoys or with AIS off) without frequent projectile, mine, or seizure incidents (incident rate near pre-February 2026 baseline of near-zero).
- **Commercial transit levels**: Daily AIS-detected or reported crossings approach 70–80+ (pre-war ~100+), with oil-related traffic no longer heavily dependent on ship-to-ship transfers outside the strait or extreme routing deviations.
- **War-risk insurance**: Additional premiums fall to ≤1–2% of hull value (from current 6–10%), with capacity available without government backstops and cancellation notices withdrawn for routine Gulf voyages.
- **Oil and LNG export continuity**: Hormuz-linked flows (including STS) recover to ≥90% of 2025 baseline (~17–20 mbpd oil equivalent for relevant streams), with alternative routes (East-West pipeline, Fujairah/Oman) no longer carrying a structural majority of bypass volume under duress.
- **Energy market stress and volatility**: Brent and regional gas prices reflect no persistent multi-dollar war premium; realized volatility returns to pre-crisis ranges absent new triggers.
- **Force posture of major actors**: US carrier/naval presence reduces from multi-carrier blockade/escort intensity; Iranian IRGC naval posture returns to peacetime patrol levels without declared “control” or toll regimes.
- **Absence of major new escalation triggers**: No collapse of mediation channels, no major regime-internal shocks forcing Iranian escalation, and no proxy activation cascade that reopens Red Sea or Gulf fronts.

A temporary ceasefire, partial oil-flow rebound via workarounds, or lower spot prices alone do not qualify. Normality is commercial and operational, not merely a battlefield pause.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot** (as of ~3 November 2026)  
The war began 28 February 2026 with US-Israeli strikes; Ali Khamenei was killed and replaced by Mojtaba. An April ceasefire and June Islamabad MOU both collapsed; the conflict has continued at varying intensity for ~8 months, centered on Hormuz interdiction, a US naval blockade of Iranian ports, and intermittent strikes. Shipping through Hormuz remains suppressed (recent Kpler/IEA-linked figures show Hormuz oil flows ~10–14 mbpd or ~50–80% of baseline depending on metric and week, with heavy use of Omani-coastal routes, STS transfers, and alternatives; total Gulf+alternatives sometimes near or above pre-war). Proxies remain active (Houthi advances in Yemen, residual regional threats). Oil prices recently swung sharply on talk optimism then rebounded on strike reports (Brent ~$105–106). **4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant classes (partial stabilization timelines):

- **1980s Tanker War (Iran-Iraq, 1984–88)**: Sustained attacks on shipping with US escort/reflagging. Relevant for protracted maritime coercion under naval pressure. Breakdown: lower absolute volumes, no full closure attempt, different political stakes. Suggests multi-year disruption possible but traffic never fully halted; functional recovery followed ceasefire + force degradation (months after major naval actions).
- **2019–2021 Hormuz/Red Sea limpet-mine and seizure episodes**: Limited, deniable attacks. Relevant for insurance spikes and temporary routing changes. Breakdown: far lower intensity and duration. Recovery measured in weeks to low months once incidents ceased.
- **2023–present Houthi Red Sea campaign**: Prolonged chokepoint disruption with insurance and routing effects. Relevant for persistent proxy maritime pressure and slow commercial adaptation. Breakdown: alternative Cape route available (Hormuz has no equivalent full bypass). Partial normalization lagged ceasefires by months; full insurance/routing recovery slower.
- **Gulf War I (1990–91) and limited 1990s tanker incidents**: Rapid post-conflict recovery once military dominance clear. Relevant for force-posture and insurance response. Breakdown: decisive conventional outcome.
- **2025 12-day Israel-Iran exchanges**: Brief spike. Relevant for market and short-term shipping reaction. Recovery near-immediate after pause.

Base-rate prior (probability of meeting the full operational definition by horizon, before case-specific updating): 1 week ~5%; 1 month ~12%; 3 months ~25%; 6 months ~40%; 1 year ~55%; 18 months ~65%. These reflect historical pattern that maritime/commercial normalization lags kinetic pauses, insurance and routing habits persist, and chokepoint wars often require political settlement or decisive degradation.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**  
- Active mediation channel and Iranian conditional reopening proposals.  
- Demonstrated commercial adaptability (convoy/STS/alternative routes) keeping total energy flows resilient.  
- US ability to degrade Iranian maritime capabilities over time and protect selected traffic.  
- Domestic/economic pressures on Iran from blockade and sanctions.  
- Buyer (Asia/Europe) and Gulf-state interest in stable flows.  

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**  
- Unresolved core issues (nuclear, sanctions, blockade sequencing) and history of collapsed deals.  
- Persistent high insurance costs and commercial risk aversion.  
- Elevated US force posture and post-midterm escalation risk.  
- Iranian residual capacity for mining, missiles, and proxy activation.  
- Regime incentives to retain Hormuz leverage; internal stability concerns.  
- Infrastructure damage and second-order effects (routing habits, insurer capital).

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Continued low-to-medium intensity pressure through early 2027, with intermittent mediation and partial commercial workarounds. A durable pause or limited framework emerges in the 3–9 month window if post-midterm strikes remain limited, allowing gradual insurance and routing improvement. Full multi-domain normality is more likely after 6–12 months than before, contingent on no major new trigger.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Post-midterm limited strikes or credible Iranian concessions produce a sequenced deal (Hormuz reopening + partial blockade/sanctions relief). Incident rate collapses within weeks; insurers reprice downward within 1–2 months as data accumulates; force posture begins drawdown by month 3–4. Probability of the overall event rises materially at the 1- and 3-month marks under this path.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Talks fail; intense campaign resumes after midterms; Iran retains or regenerates maritime/proxy capacity; insurance remains elevated; alternative-route dependence becomes semi-permanent. Normality delayed beyond 18 months. This path is supported by prior deal collapses and reported strike options.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Strongest case that probabilities are too high: commercial metrics (insurance, normal routing) have lagged kinetic pauses in every comparable case, and the nuclear/sanctions linkage has repeatedly blocked deals. Strongest case they are too low: total energy flows have already shown surprising resilience, US protective capacity is high, and Iranian economic pressure is cumulative. Load-bearing assumptions: mediation remains viable; Iranian residual capability does not produce a decisive new interdiction campaign; US domestic politics do not force prolonged high-intensity operations. Evidence that would falsify the central view: sustained multi-month incident-free normal routing with premiums <2% by month 3 (too low) or collapse of total flows below 50% baseline with expanded proxy war by month 6 (too high).
