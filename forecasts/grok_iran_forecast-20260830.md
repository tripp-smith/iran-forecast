**1. Forecast Question**  
Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf returns to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by the horizons of 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months (from ~30 August 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
Functional normality requires simultaneous satisfaction of all of the following observable conditions for a sustained period (not a single-day snapshot or temporary pause):

- Direct military activity: No major US/Israeli or Iranian strikes on Iranian territory, Gulf bases, or critical energy/shipping infrastructure for ≥30 consecutive days; residual low-level activity below pre-war baseline thresholds.  
- Proxy activity: No significant attacks by Iran-aligned proxies (Houthis, Hezbollah, PMF, etc.) on shipping, energy infrastructure, or US/allied forces that materially affect Gulf operations.  
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Free navigation on internationally recognized routes (including the Traffic Separation Scheme) without mandatory Iranian coordination, escorts as the default, or active mine/drone/missile threats; vessel-tracking and maritime security reports confirm risk levels comparable to pre-February 2026.  
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits through Hormuz recover to ≥80% of pre-war averages (~100–130 vessels/day; energy carriers dominant).  
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums return to ≤2–3× peacetime baseline (~0.1–0.25% of hull value) and remain available without widespread exclusions or case-by-case withdrawals for standard commercial operators.  
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf (Saudi, Iraq, UAE, Kuwait, Qatar, Iran) crude/condensate and LNG exports recover to ≥85–90% of pre-war volumes (~15–20 mb/d crude liquids through Hormuz range), with Iranian exports no longer subject to active naval blockade.  
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent price and implied volatility reflect supply continuity rather than chronic Hormuz risk premium; no sustained 20%+ dislocation attributable to Gulf disruption.  
- Force posture of major actors: Material drawdown or stabilization of US/CENTCOM and Iranian naval/missile postures from peak wartime levels; no new major force surges.  
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No successful large-scale attacks, regime-collapse events, nuclear threshold crossings, or third-party interventions that reset the conflict.

A temporary ceasefire, partial escorted transits, lower oil prices alone, or battlefield pauses do **not** meet the definition. Commercial and insurance normalization are gating factors and lag kinetic pauses.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot (as of ~30 August 2026)**  
The war began 28 February 2026 with US-Israeli strikes that killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei. It is day ~183. A 60-day MoU (signed mid-June) collapsed in early July; major kinetic exchanges resumed then largely paused (≈30 consecutive nights without confirmed US/Israeli strikes inside Iran and limited Iranian launches).  

Hormuz traffic remains severely suppressed: averages of ~5–20 vessels/day (vs. ~100–130 pre-war), with many dark/AIS-off or US-facilitated/escorted movements. US CENTCOM asserts mines cleared from international routes and momentum building (claims of assisted transits totaling hundreds of vessels and hundreds of millions of barrels); IRGC asserts full control and requires coordination. Recent incidents (e.g., tanker strikes) continue at low frequency.  

Iranian crude exports collapsed under the reimposed US naval blockade to ~260 kb/d in August (from pre-war ~1.7 mb/d and brief June rebound). Broader Gulf crude flows through Hormuz are estimated well below pre-war (~half or less in some assessments). Brent trades ~$88/bbl (down from peaks >$110–118 earlier). War-risk hull premiums remain elevated at roughly 3–8% of hull value (15–40× peacetime in various broker quotes), rendering unescorted commercial transit uneconomic for most operators. Thousands of sailors and hundreds of vessels remain stranded or delayed. Economic pressure on Iran is intense (rial at record lows, fuel shortages, sanctions “Economic D-Day”). No successor diplomatic framework is in place; Trump administration signals no hurry for talks and prioritizes pressure. **4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant classes (3–6):

1. **1980s Iran-Iraq Tanker War (1984–88)**: Hundreds of attacks; Hormuz never fully closed; flows largely maintained via escorts/reflagging. Partial commercial normalization occurred while fighting continued; full post-war commercial/insurance recovery took months after the 1988 ceasefire. Relevance: same waterway, asymmetric attacks on shipping. Breaks down: no regime decapitation, no US blockade of one side’s ports, lower modern precision weapons/insurance sensitivity. Suggests: kinetic reduction possible in months; commercial normality 6–18+ months after major fighting ends.  

2. **2019–2021 Gulf tanker attacks / tanker seizures + temporary mining threats**: Short spikes in risk premiums and traffic caution; rapid partial recovery once kinetic intensity dropped and escorts/diplomacy intervened. Relevance: modern Hormuz risk pricing. Breaks down: far lower intensity and duration. Suggests: 1–3 months for partial shipping recovery after sustained pause.  

3. **Red Sea/Houthi disruption (2023–ongoing)**: Prolonged high war-risk premiums, rerouting, and suppressed traffic despite intermittent pauses. Commercial recovery incomplete years later. Relevance: insurance stickiness and proxy persistence. Breaks down: alternative Cape route exists (unlike Hormuz). Suggests: elevated premiums and caution can persist 12–24+ months.  

4. **Suez closures (1956, 1967–75) and major chokepoint wars**: Full physical closures lasted years; recovery required political settlement + infrastructure clearance. Relevance: chokepoint economics. Breaks down: Hormuz is not fully closed and has partial workarounds (dark fleet, escorts). Suggests: multi-year tails if control contest continues.  

5. **Post-ceasefire energy recovery after 1990–91 Gulf War / 2003 Iraq War phases**: Oil flows recovered faster than political stability once kinetic threat to shipping ended. Relevance: market adaptation. Breaks down: no ongoing contest over the strait itself.  

**Base-rate prior (pre-current-evidence, pure reference-class average for functional commercial normality):**  
- 1 week: ~1–3%  
- 1 month: ~5–10%  
- 3 months: ~15–25%  
- 6 months: ~30–40%  
- 1 year: ~45–55%  
- 18 months: ~55–65%  

These are deliberately conservative given the demanding multi-domain definition and historical stickiness of insurance/shipping behavior.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Sustained kinetic pause + credible mutual restraint (gating).  
2. Diplomatic framework restoring free navigation without Iranian veto/tolls.  
3. Insurance market response to demonstrated safety (capacity return).  
4. US decision to lift/relax naval blockade of Iranian ports.  
5. Gulf state / Asian buyer confidence restoring commercial volumes.  
6. Force posture stabilization.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Iranian insistence on control/coordination over Hormuz as residual leverage.  
2. US maximalist economic pressure / no-deal posture.  
3. Stickiness of war-risk insurance and operator risk aversion.  
4. Residual mining/drone/missile capability and low-level incidents.  
5. Proxy reactivation risk and leadership succession dynamics inside Iran.  
6. Backlog of stranded vessels and damaged commercial confidence.

**9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon**

**1 Week**  
- Probability: 2%  
- Base-rate anchor: 1–3%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Minimal upward (pause exists) offset by zero commercial progress.  
- Core rationale: Even if the kinetic pause holds, traffic, insurance, and export volumes cannot recover in days.  
- Main gating factor: Commercial transit + insurance.  
- What would raise: Sudden comprehensive deal + immediate free transit confirmation.  
- What would lower: New major attack or formal re-escalation.  
- Confidence: High  

**1 Month**  
- Probability: 8%  
- Base-rate anchor: 5–10%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight upward from pause and moderated oil prices; downward from insurance stickiness and no diplomacy.  
- Core rationale: Partial escorted traffic possible; full functional normality (free routes + normal premiums + volume recovery) remains improbable.  
- Main gating factor: Insurance + free navigation without Iranian coordination.  
- What would raise: Binding navigation agreement + sustained zero incidents + premium collapse.  
- What would lower: Resumed strikes or new tanker attacks.  
- Confidence: Medium-High  

**3 Months**  
- Probability: 22%  
- Base-rate anchor: 15–25%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Modest upward if pause holds; downward from structural contest over Hormuz control.  
- Core rationale: Kinetic reduction is plausible; commercial/insurance lag of 1–3 months after any deal is historically normal and currently still missing.  
- Main gating factor: Diplomatic settlement on navigation rights.  
- What would raise: Successor framework to the MoU with verified free transit.  
- What would lower: Collapse of pause or intensified blockade.  
- Confidence: Medium  

**6 Months**  
- Probability: 38%  
- Base-rate anchor: 30–40%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight upward from adaptation capacity; downward from maximalist US pressure and Iranian leverage retention.  
- Core rationale: Base rates improve; still requires resolution of the control contest and insurance reset.  
- Main gating factor: End of active contest over Hormuz administration.  
- What would raise: Regime concession or mutually hurting stalemate leading to deal.  
- What would lower: Prolonged low-intensity campaign or proxy escalation.  
- Confidence: Medium  

**1 Year**  
- Probability: 52%  
- Base-rate anchor: 45–55%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Near neutral; long-horizon uncertainty dominates.  
- Core rationale: Markets and operators adapt; political settlement becomes more likely under economic pressure, but residual risk premium can persist.  
- Main gating factor: Political settlement + demonstrated multi-month safety.  
- What would raise: Clear victory/defeat or brokered grand bargain.  
- What would lower: Frozen conflict with permanent elevated risk zone.  
- Confidence: Medium-Low  

**18 Months**  
- Probability: 63%  
- Base-rate anchor: 55–65%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight upward for adaptation and fatigue effects.  
- Core rationale: Reference classes show most comparable disruptions resolve commercially within 12–24 months once major fighting ends; full multi-domain normality remains incomplete in a non-trivial share of cases.  
- Main gating factor: Absence of new triggers + insurance market reset.  
- What would raise: Sustained free navigation + premium collapse to near-baseline.  
- What would lower: Entrenched Iranian control claims or repeated proxy activation.  
- Confidence: Low-Medium  

Synthesis logic: Shipping security, insurance, and export continuity are sequential gating factors that lag kinetic pause by weeks to months. Probabilities are not simple averages; they are constrained by the slowest components. Long-horizon figures remain well below 80% because the operational definition is deliberately demanding and historical chokepoint recoveries leave residual risk.

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Continued low-intensity contest: intermittent pauses and limited incidents, US economic pressure, Iranian residual leverage over Hormuz, gradual but incomplete recovery of escorted/dark traffic, persistently elevated (though slowly declining) war-risk premiums, and partial Gulf export recovery. Functional normality is reached only after a political settlement or mutual exhaustion, most probably in the 9–18-month window.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Probability of path: ~20–25%. Triggered by a rapid, verified navigation agreement (Iran drops coordination demands; US lifts blockade), 60+ days of zero incidents, and insurance capacity flood. Traffic and premiums could normalize inside 2–4 months.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Probability of path: ~30–35%. Triggered by Iranian insistence on permanent control claims, US refusal to negotiate from strength, repeated low-level attacks or proxy activation, and insurance market structural repricing. Elevated risk zone and suppressed volumes persist beyond 18 months.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Overestimation risk: Under-weighting Iranian regime survival incentives to retain Hormuz leverage and US domestic incentives for continued pressure; insurance markets have shown multi-year stickiness in the Red Sea. Underestimation risk: Under-weighting commercial adaptation (dark fleets, escorts, alternative sourcing) and economic pain on both sides that historically forces off-ramps faster than pure political analysis predicts. Load-bearing assumptions: (1) Iranian leadership prioritizes survival over maximalist control claims under severe economic stress; (2) US will eventually accept a navigation-focused deal rather than regime-collapse objectives; (3) no major new escalation (nuclear, large proxy war, third-party intervention).
