**1. Forecast Question**  
Probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months (from 26 September 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
All of the following must hold simultaneously for a continuous period of at least 14 days (to distinguish from temporary pauses):

- Direct military activity: No US/coalition strikes on Iranian territory or Iranian ballistic-missile/drone strikes on US/Gulf-host-state bases or territory.  
- Proxy activity: No sustained Houthi or other Iranian-aligned attacks that close or significantly disrupt Bab al-Mandeb / Red Sea commercial traffic or Saudi energy infrastructure.  
- Shipping security (Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters): No successful attacks on commercial vessels; clear, internationally recognized transit corridors free of mines or active interdiction; UKMTO/IMO advisories no longer listing the area as high-risk for kinetic attack.  
- Commercial transit levels: Daily commodity-vessel transits ≥70% of pre-war baseline (~85–90 of the ~125 large commercial vessels/day).  
- War-risk insurance: Additional war-risk premiums ≤0.5% of hull value (pre-war ~0.125–0.25%; current multiples still elevated).  
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Persian Gulf oil exports ≥18 mb/d (pre-war ~20 mb/d through Hormuz) and Qatar/UAE LNG loadings near pre-war rates, without reliance on ship-to-ship transfers as the primary workaround.  
- Energy market stress: Brent crude < $85/bbl on a sustained basis and realized 30-day volatility below the levels observed during the active phases of the war.  
- Force posture: Material drawdown or repositioning of major US naval/air assets from surge levels in the Gulf; Iranian coastal anti-shipping systems not actively reconstituted at pre-July 2026 threat density.  
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new Iranian nuclear breakout steps, no major third-party intervention that reopens kinetic exchange, and no collapse of any interim political understanding that restarts the tanker war.

A temporary ceasefire, lower oil prices alone, or a battlefield pause without commercial/insurance recovery does **not** qualify.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant classes (with breakdowns):

1. **1980–88 Iran–Iraq Tanker War / Operation Earnest Will**: Multi-year intermittent attacks on shipping; US reflagging and escorts; commercial traffic never fully normalized until ceasefire. Partial stabilization after major US intervention took months to years; full commercial recovery lagged military pause. Suggests 6–18+ months for functional normality after major escalation ends.  
2. **2019 Gulf tanker attacks / Iran–US near-war**: Short, high-intensity interdiction period; insurance spiked; traffic recovered within weeks–months once kinetic risk subsided and diplomatic de-escalation occurred. Faster recovery possible when political will exists and damage is limited.  
3. **Houthi Red Sea campaign 2023–25**: Sustained proxy disruption of a chokepoint; traffic diversion (Cape of Good Hope); insurance elevated for >12–18 months; partial recovery only after sustained kinetic pressure + diplomacy, still incomplete. Shows proxy persistence can extend commercial abnormality well beyond kinetic peaks.  
4. **Suez Canal closures / major chokepoint disruptions (1956, 1967–75)**: Full or near-full closures lasted months to years; recovery of traffic and insurance lagged formal reopenings by weeks–months due to risk perception.  
5. **Post-ceasefire Gulf or Levant conflicts with maritime dimensions** (e.g., 2006 Lebanon, earlier Iran proxy cycles): Military pauses often preceded commercial normalization by 1–6 months; insurance and force posture lagged further.

**Base-rate prior (pre-current-evidence adjustment)**:  
- 1 week: <5%  
- 1 month: 8–12%  
- 3 months: 20–30%  
- 6 months: 35–45%  
- 1 year: 50–60%  
- 18 months: 60–70%  

These reflect that commercial/insurance recovery historically trails military pauses, and prolonged proxy or unresolved political disputes keep risk elevated.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Mutual political incentive for a transactional deal (US midterms; Iranian economic pain).  
2. Sustained kinetic pause reducing immediate risk perception.  
3. Possible limited Iran–Oman corridor arrangement accepted by enough commercial actors.  
4. Gradual insurance capacity return once incidents fall.  
5. Asian buyer pressure for restored volumes.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Unresolved political fundamentals (blockade, assets, corridor sovereignty, nuclear).  
2. Insurance and risk-perception lag (historical pattern).  
3. Proxy (Houthi) autonomy and Red Sea expansion.  
4. Gulf Arab opposition to deals that legitimize Iranian control claims.  
5. Ability of either side to reconstitute anti-shipping capability quickly.  
6. Second-order market effects (STS, pipeline risk, volatility) that sustain elevated premiums.

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Continued low-level maritime friction and elevated insurance through late 2026; possible limited transactional arrangement around or after US midterms that restores partial transit (40–60% of pre-war) by early–mid 2027; full functional normality (per definition) more likely only in H2 2027 or later if proxy and political issues are managed. Oil prices ease gradually but volatility remains elevated until insurance normalizes.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Decisive US–Iran understanding that includes blockade lift, corridor rules acceptable to commercial operators, and credible proxy restraint signals. Transit jumps within weeks; insurers rapidly expand capacity. Probability of this path ~15–20% by 6 months.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Talks fail; intermittent attacks resume; Houthi Red Sea control hardens; Gulf states harden against any Iranian legitimization of control claims. Dual-chokepoint disruption persists into 2027–28. Probability of this path ~30–35% at 18-month horizon.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Possible over-estimation of normalization: Insurance and risk-perception lags have been deeper and longer in recent chokepoint cases than pure military timelines suggest; Houthi autonomy may be higher than modeled; Gulf Arab veto power on deals may be stronger.  
Possible under-estimation: Mutual economic pain + US domestic calendar could produce a faster transactional deal than reference classes imply; Asian demand pressure could force commercial actors to accept higher residual risk faster than expected.  
Load-bearing assumptions: Kinetic pause remains fragile; political fundamentals are not easily bridged; insurance reacts with multi-month lag.
