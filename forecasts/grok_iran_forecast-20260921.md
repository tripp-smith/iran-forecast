**1. Forecast Question**  
Probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War (initiated 28 February 2026) by the horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months from 21 September 2026.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
All of the following must hold concurrently and sustainably (not a single-day snapshot or temporary pause):

- Direct military activity: No ballistic missile, cruise missile, or large drone strikes by Iran or coalition forces against host-state territory, major bases, or critical energy infrastructure in the Gulf for ≥30 consecutive days; residual small-scale incidents below pre-war baseline intensity.
- Proxy activity: No significant Houthi or other proxy attacks on commercial shipping, Saudi/UAE energy infrastructure, or Bab al-Mandeb that disrupt flows beyond pre-2026 Red Sea levels.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: No successful attacks on commercial vessels; effective demining of main TSS lanes; freedom of navigation without Iranian “permit/toll” regime enforcement or forced diversions.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily commodity vessel transits ≥70% of pre-war baseline (~85–130 vessels/day depending on tracker; IMF PortWatch/Kpler/UKMTO consistent measures).
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums ≤0.5% of hull value per transit (down from current ~1.5%/15× peacetime) and readily available without widespread cancellations or JWC listed-area expansion remaining in force at crisis levels.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Persian Gulf crude + products exports ≥18–20 mb/d (pre-war ~21–23 mb/d) with Iranian loadings recovering toward 1.5+ mb/d; Qatar LNG near pre-war capacity without force majeure.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent sustained < $85 (current ~$101–102) with realized volatility returning to pre-war norms; no structural risk premium >$15–20.
- Force posture of major actors: Material drawdown of surge U.S./coalition naval and air assets from peak levels; Iranian coastal and missile forces no longer on continuous high alert with active mining/anti-ship posture.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new major kinetic events, nuclear-related escalations, or formal closure declarations that reset the risk environment.

A temporary ceasefire, lower oil prices alone, or battlefield pause without commercial and insurance normalization does **not** meet the definition.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant classes (Gulf/chokepoint/maritime disruption + regional war):

1. **1980s Tanker War (Iran-Iraq)**: Multi-year shipping attacks in Gulf/Hormuz; insurance spikes; traffic never fully stopped but risk premium persisted years. Functional commercial normalization took 2–4+ years after major kinetic phases subsided; partial stabilization 6–18 months after intensity peaks. Breaks down: state-on-state vs current asymmetric + nuclear overlay + modern insurance/sanctions.
2. **1990–91 Gulf War + aftermath**: Rapid kinetic phase; Hormuz never fully closed; commercial recovery within weeks–months of ceasefire, but residual risk and force posture elevated 1–2 years. Breaks down: clear coalition victory vs current stalemate.
3. **2019 tanker attacks / Abqaiq / Iran sanctions pressure**: Short spikes; insurance rose then fell within 1–3 months of de-escalation; traffic largely recovered quickly once kinetic risk dropped. Breaks down: limited duration/intensity vs 7-month war with mining and formal closure claims.
4. **2023–25 Red Sea/Houthi disruption**: Traffic collapse, insurance spike, rerouting; partial recovery over 6–12+ months even with continued low-level attacks; full pre-crisis normality not restored. Closest recent maritime proxy case. Breaks down: secondary chokepoint vs primary energy artery.
5. **Black Sea grain/oil corridor post-2022**: Negotiated corridors + insurance facilities allowed partial recovery in months; full commercial confidence lagged 12–18 months amid ongoing war. Relevant for insurance + corridor mechanics.
6. **Suez 1956 / 1967–75 closures**: Full closures lasted months to years; recovery after political settlement was relatively rapid once physical access restored, but political risk premium lingered.

**Base-rate prior (pre-case evidence)** for functional normality meeting the multi-domain definition:  
1 week: <5%  
1 month: 10–15%  
3 months: 25–35%  
6 months: 40–50%  
1 year: 55–65%  
18 months: 65–75%  

Anchored in historical lag between kinetic pause and commercial/insurance recovery (typically 3–12+ months even after clear de-escalation).

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Sustained kinetic pause + mutual economic pain creating bargaining space.  
2. Active third-party mediation and Gulf-state interest in de-risking.  
3. Demonstrated ability of some volumes to move via alternative routes/pipelines (reduces absolute leverage).  
4. Historical pattern that insurance and traffic lag but eventually follow clear de-escalation.  
5. U.S. political calendar (midterms) potentially favoring off-ramps.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Iranian determination to retain effective control/toll regime over Hormuz as core strategic asset.  
2. Deep mutual distrust after collapsed MOUs; high bar for verification of demining/safe passage.  
3. Insurance and shipowner confidence require months of incident-free operation, not just declarations.  
4. Proxy (Houthi) and secondary fronts can independently sustain risk.  
5. Nuclear file and domestic Iranian politics raise escalation thresholds.  
6. U.S. blockade and sanctions architecture hard to unwind quickly.  
7. Force posture and stockpile depletion create incentives for limited continued pressure.

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Kinetic pause continues unevenly with intermittent low-level maritime incidents. Negotiations produce temporary or partial corridors allowing traffic to rise to 30–50% over 3–6 months. Insurance declines slowly. Full multi-domain normality (especially insurance ≤0.5% and consistent ≥70% traffic without Iranian toll regime) takes 9–18 months and remains incomplete. Oil stabilizes in $80–95 range with residual risk premium. Force postures stay elevated relative to 2025.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Clear, verified agreement on free transit + demining within weeks, followed by 60+ incident-free days, rapid premium compression, and traffic recovery to 60%+ by 3 months. Requires Iranian strategic shift away from Hormuz leverage and U.S. willingness to ease blockade/sanctions meaningfully. Probability of this path materializing: ~15–20%.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Pause breaks; mining or vessel attacks resume; Iranian toll regime hardens; Houthi pressure intensifies; oil stays >$100 with high volatility; insurance remains elevated. Functional normality delayed beyond 18 months. Driven by Iranian perception that Hormuz control is non-negotiable and domestic politics. Probability of this path: ~25–30%.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Possible overestimation of normalization: Under-weights Iranian doctrinal commitment to Hormuz as irreversible strategic asset and the extreme stickiness of marine insurance after multi-month crisis (historical Tanker War and Red Sea experience). Commercial actors may demand longer clean periods than political actors assume.  

Possible underestimation: Under-weights severity of Iranian economic collapse and U.S./Gulf interest in off-ramps before further stockpile or political costs. Base rates from shorter crises may be too pessimistic for a war already 7 months old with demonstrated mutual exhaustion signals.

Load-bearing assumptions: (1) No major new kinetic trigger in next 1–3 months; (2) Insurance markets require sustained evidence rather than political declarations; (3) Iranian leadership prioritizes regime survival over maximalist Hormuz control if costs rise further.
