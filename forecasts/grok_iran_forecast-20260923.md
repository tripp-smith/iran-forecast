**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each horizon: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months (as of 23 September 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality requires simultaneous satisfaction of all the following observable thresholds for a sustained period (minimum 14 consecutive days without material breach):

- Direct military activity: No major state-on-state missile, drone, or naval strikes between Iran and the US/Israel/coalition forces targeting bases, energy infrastructure, or naval assets in the Gulf region. Isolated intercepts or single small-boat incidents do not count as breach if contained.
- Proxy activity: No sustained proxy campaigns (Houthis, Iraqi militias, Hezbollah-linked) producing damage to commercial shipping, energy infrastructure, or GCC territory at a rate exceeding one successful strike per week on average.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Commercial transit free of kinetic attacks, mining threats, or coercive boarding/interception requiring naval escort as the default. US/coalition facilitation of transit falls below 20% of total volume.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily two-way commercial vessel transits (AIS/satellite-tracked, IMF PortWatch or equivalent) average ≥60% of pre-war baseline (~50–55+ vessels/day vs. ~85–90 pre-war). Sustained for 14 days.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums for standard VLCC/Suezmax Hormuz transit ≤0.5% of hull value (vs. current ~1.5% / 15× peacetime; pre-war ~0.1%). Capacity freely available without special government facilities.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Combined crude + condensate + LNG loadings from Persian Gulf terminals (ex-Iran under blockade if still in force) recover to ≥85% of pre-war volumes on a 30-day average, with no force-majeure declarations by major exporters (Saudi, UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Iraq).
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent 30-day realized volatility ≤25% annualized and no sustained risk premium >$10/bbl attributable to Hormuz closure risk (current Brent ~$95–100 range with elevated risk pricing).
- Force posture of major actors: Material reduction in surge deployments (carrier groups, additional Patriot/THAAD batteries, IRGC naval concentration) relative to peak war levels; no new permanent forward basing announced as wartime necessity.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No nuclear threshold crossing, no large-scale mining of the strait, no direct attack on a major LNG train or desalination plant, and no formal declaration of closed strait by either side.

A temporary ceasefire, partial transit recovery under escort, or oil-price decline alone does not meet the definition. Commercial and insurance normalization are gating factors; military pause is necessary but insufficient.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant classes (selected for Gulf/chokepoint/maritime disruption and regional war duration):

1. Iran-Iraq Tanker War (1984–1988 phase): Multi-year attacks on shipping; strait never fully closed but risk elevated for years. Stabilization required war end + years of residual risk. Suggests functional commercial normality takes 1–3+ years post-major fighting.
2. 2019 Gulf shipping crisis (tanker attacks, mine incidents): ~4–5 months of elevated risk; insurance and transit largely normalized within 3–6 months of de-escalation after US response and diplomatic signaling. Breaks down because scale was far smaller (no full war, no blockade, limited infrastructure damage).
3. Red Sea/Houthi disruption (2023–ongoing): Partial closure effects; insurance elevated for >12–18 months even after partial de-escalation; commercial avoidance persists. Suggests insurance and voluntary transit lag military pause by 6–18 months.
4. 1990–91 Gulf War energy disruption: Oil spike and regional risk resolved in months after decisive military outcome and ceasefire; commercial flows recovered rapidly once Kuwait liberated and risk premium collapsed. Breaks down because clear victory and rapid coalition dominance.
5. Post-ceasefire chokepoint recoveries (e.g., Suez 1956/1967/1973 analogs, limited mine-clearance cases): Physical reopening often weeks; full commercial/insurance confidence 3–12 months. Mine clearance alone historically 40–50+ days in contested waters.
6. Broader post-major Middle East wars (1973, 1980–88 residual, 2003 Iraq): Energy market stress often persists 6–24 months after kinetic peak due to infrastructure damage, force posture, and residual proxy risk.

Base-rate prior (pre-current-evidence, timeline probability of meeting the full operational definition):  
- 1 week: 1–3%  
- 1 month: 5–10%  
- 3 months: 15–25%  
- 6 months: 30–40%  
- 1 year: 45–55%  
- 18 months: 55–65%  

These reflect that commercial/insurance lag military pause, historical Gulf disruptions rarely normalize fully inside 6 months, and multi-year residual risk is common without decisive settlement.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

Ranked by importance:  
1. Diplomatic breakthrough at/around UNGA producing credible, mutual de-escalation (end blockade + Iranian transit restraint).  
2. Sustained kinetic pause (>30–60 days) reducing perceived attack probability.  
3. Insurance market response to demonstrated safety (capacity return, rate compression).  
4. Clearance of any residual mines/threats and backlog clearance producing visible transit recovery.  
5. US domestic political calendar (midterms) incentivizing de-escalation if costs rise.  
6. GCC economic pressure for reopening and energy revenue restoration.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

Ranked by importance:  
1. Structural disagreement over Hormuz control, blockade, frozen assets, and proxy wars (Iran views control as strategic gain; US rejects tolls/control).  
2. Residual attack capability and demonstrated willingness (Iran asymmetric tools; US strike capacity).  
3. Insurance and shipowner risk aversion lagging any political pause (historical pattern).  
4. Proxy autonomy and activation risk (Houthis already active).  
5. Infrastructure repair timelines and force-posture inertia.  
6. Domestic hardline audiences on both sides punishing compromise.  
7. Potential for new triggers (nuclear file, major infrastructure hit, election-driven escalation).

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Overestimation risk: Treating recent kinetic pauses as durable; under-weighting Iranian strategic interest in Hormuz control as wartime gain; assuming insurance markets respond linearly to political signals (they historically lag and require demonstrated loss-free periods). Underestimation risk: Under-weighting US domestic cost pressures and GCC economic urgency that could force faster compromise; possible rapid mine-clearance and escorted recovery producing de-facto commercial normality faster than pure base rates. Load-bearing assumptions: that commercial transit + insurance are true gating factors (correct per definition) and that no decisive military outcome occurs (consistent with current attrition). Strongest contrary evidence would be rapid, verified transit recovery to 50%+ with simultaneous rate collapse without a formal deal.
