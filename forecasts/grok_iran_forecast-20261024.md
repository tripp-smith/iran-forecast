**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each of these horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months. (As of 24 October 2026; war began 28 February 2026, ~day 238.)

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

“Return to functional normality” requires sustained, observable multi-domain conditions over a continuous 14-day window (to exclude temporary pauses), meeting all of the following thresholds simultaneously:

- **Direct military activity**: No large-scale U.S./Israeli strikes on Iranian territory or Iranian ballistic/cruise missile or large drone barrages against regional targets (isolated residual incidents <5% of peak rates acceptable if not escalating).
- **Proxy activity**: Houthi and other Iran-linked proxy attacks on shipping, energy infrastructure, or Gulf states reduced to sporadic/low-intensity levels (≤2 confirmed incidents per week across the region) with no coordinated campaigns.
- **Shipping security (Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters)**: AIS-visible commercial transits averaging ≥60% of pre-war baseline (~85/day); no systematic mining, drone/missile attacks on commercial vessels, or IRGC “authorization” enforcement that effectively deters non-coordinated traffic; dark/escorted lanes not required as the dominant mode.
- **Commercial transit levels**: Sustained volumes of crude, products, LNG, and dry bulk through Hormuz and Gulf ports at ≥70% of pre-war levels (adjusting for bypasses like East-West pipeline).
- **War-risk insurance**: Premiums for Hormuz/Gulf transits ≤1.5–2× pre-war baseline (~0.25–0.5% of hull value per voyage) with broad underwriter availability and no mass P&I cancellations or force-majeure clustering by majors.
- **Oil and LNG export continuity**: Aggregate Gulf crude/condensate exports ≥16–17 Mb/d (near pre-war); Iranian crude loadings resumed at commercial scale without blockade; Qatari LNG force majeure largely lifted and exports ≥70% of pre-war.
- **Energy market stress and volatility**: Brent sustained below ~$85–90 (absent major non-Gulf shocks), with realized volatility and freight as % of FOB price returning near pre-war norms; no systemic buyer stockpiling or force-majeure cascade.
- **Force posture of major actors**: U.S. carrier/amphibious presence and blockade posture reduced from wartime peaks; Iranian IRGC Navy not enforcing exclusion zones; no active mutual high-alert nuclear/missile postures.
- **Absence of major new escalation triggers**: No new large strikes, mine campaigns, proxy offensives, or diplomatic collapses that reset the above within the window.

Temporary ceasefires, price dips alone, or battlefield pauses without commercial/insurance/export recovery do **not** count. The definition is demanding by design: commercial normalization lags military pauses.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot**

War ongoing without formal ceasefire. Trump has pledged no U.S. strikes before 3 November midterms, but blockade of Iranian ports (reimposed ~14 July) continues and preparations for post-election options are reported. Claims of control and attack attribution are contested; confirmed high attack rate and low visible traffic are robust.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant classes (3–6):

- **1984–88 Tanker War (Iran-Iraq)**: ~450 ship attacks over years; strait never fully closed; flows largely maintained via reflagging/escorts (Operation Earnest Will 1987–88). Relevant for persistence of low-level attacks without total closure. Breaks down: no sustained U.S. blockade of one side’s ports; different power balance. Suggests multi-year partial disruption possible but commercial flows resilient under protection. Stabilization of attacks only with broader ceasefire (1988).
- **2019 Gulf of Oman/Hormuz incidents**: Short spike in attacks and war-risk premia (10× jump); traffic continued. Relevant for rapid insurance reaction and resilience. Breaks down: far lower intensity and no blockade or sustained campaign. Timeline: weeks to months for premia to ease once incidents stopped.
- **2023–24 Red Sea/Houthi campaign**: Months of attacks reducing Red Sea transits sharply; insurance elevated; partial recovery only with naval protection and later intensity drop. Relevant for proxy maritime disruption and slow commercial return. Breaks down: not a full great-power war or Hormuz-scale oil volume. Timeline: 6–12+ months for meaningful normalization even after intensity falls.
- **Post-ceasefire maritime recovery in other conflicts (e.g., limited Iran-Iraq pauses, or historical chokepoint reopenings)**: Insurance and traffic lag military pauses by weeks–months due to residual risk, mines, and underwriter caution. Suggests 1–3 months minimum after credible de-escalation for partial commercial recovery; 6–12+ for full.
- **Sustained great-power naval blockade or high-intensity Gulf campaigns**: Rare full analogues; historical blockades (e.g., earlier Iran-Iraq phases) show prolonged export disruption until political resolution. Suggests low probability of quick full normality without deal.

**Base-rate prior** (before current evidence, averaging across classes with weight on Tanker War resilience + modern insurance sensitivity + proxy persistence):

- 1 week: ~2–5%
- 1 month: ~8–12%
- 3 months: ~20–30%
- 6 months: ~40–50%
- 1 year: ~55–65%
- 18 months: ~65–75%

These reflect that full multi-domain commercial normality is slow even after intensity drops, and Gulf oil systems show resilience under protection but insurance/traffic lag.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

1. Economic and political exhaustion on both sides (Iran revenue hit hard; U.S. midterms and domestic costs).
2. Active mediation tracks and repeated proposals (even if stalled).
3. Demonstrated resilience of partial flows via escorts, dark shipping, and bypasses.
4. Trump’s explicit pre-midterm pause and stated preference for a deal over indefinite war.
5. Shared interest among Gulf producers and Asian buyers in restoring volumes.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

1. Ongoing high-tempo shipping attacks and Iranian enforcement attempts (gating for insurance and traffic).
2. U.S. blockade of Iranian ports and zero Iranian crude exports (core leverage and revenue pressure).
3. Proxy (Houthi) activity and risk of further activation.
4. Deep distrust, sequencing disputes (blockade lift vs. Hormuz control vs. nuclear), and post-election strike options.
5. Elevated force postures and residual mine/drone risk that keep underwriters cautious even after pauses.
6. LNG and refined-product systems less flexible than crude bypasses.

Ranked by importance: shipping security/attacks and insurance lag > blockade and export continuity > proxy and force posture > diplomacy.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued contested Hormuz with elevated but not total disruption through early 2027. Partial crude flows persist via escorts/bypasses; Iranian exports stay constrained until a deal or blockade adjustment. Insurance remains high until attack rate drops for weeks. Post-midterm diplomacy produces a limited or temporary understanding by spring 2027 that reduces large strikes and some attacks, allowing gradual (not full) commercial recovery by mid-2027. Proxies continue at lower intensity. Full multi-domain normality only after sustained de-escalation, not immediately.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Post-midterm deal that sequences blockade relief with verified Hormuz opening and nuclear restraints; attack cessation verified within days; underwriters respond within 2–4 weeks; Gulf states and buyers rapidly restore volumes. Probability mass concentrated in 3–6 month window. Requires high trust and rapid verification that current evidence does not strongly support.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Post-election strikes resume; Iran sustains intermittent shipping attacks and proxy pressure as leverage; blockade remains; insurance stays elevated indefinitely; traffic and LNG recover only partially. Frozen conflict resembling prolonged Tanker War phase or Red Sea campaign. Extends high disruption beyond 12–18 months.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Overestimation of normalization risk: May underweight Iranian ability to sustain low-cost shipping harassment and proxy activation even under pressure, and underwriter conservatism (claims already ~$2B+). AIS undercounts dark traffic, so resilience may be higher than visible data suggest—potentially raising near-term probabilities. Underestimation of normalization: May overweight current attack tempo as permanent rather than tactical (pre-midterm); economic exhaustion and mediation could produce faster political resolution than base rates. Load-bearing assumptions: Attack rate is the dominant commercial gate; blockade remains leverage until a deal; no sudden regime or leadership shock. Falsifiers: Sustained attack halt + insurance drop within weeks (raises all near horizons); major post-election campaign or new mine barrage (lowers all).
