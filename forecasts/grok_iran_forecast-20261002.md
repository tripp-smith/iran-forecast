I will ground the forecast in current reporting on the 2026 Iran war, Hormuz transit, war-risk insurance, and energy flows before setting base rates and probabilities.The Persian Gulf is not near functional normality. Seven months into the war, the Strait of Hormuz is still a restricted, contested system: AIS-visible traffic is about 5 percent of the pre-crisis baseline, war-risk pricing remains an order of magnitude above peacetime, attacks were reported this week, and US–Iran talks are stalled over sequencing.

## 1. Forecast Question

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality, as defined below, by each horizon from 2 October 2026: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months.

## 2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”

Normality means all of the following have held for at least 14 consecutive days ending on the horizon date. A ceasefire, a lower oil price, or a one-week traffic spike does not qualify.

- Direct military activity: no kinetic strikes by the United States, Israel, or Iran on each other’s territory, bases, or warships, and no declared closure of Hormuz.
- Proxy activity: attacks by Hezbollah, the Houthis, or other Iran-linked groups on Gulf shipping, Gulf energy infrastructure, or US forces at or below the pre-February 2026 baseline. An isolated incident does not fail the test; a renewed pattern does.
- Shipping security: the traffic separation scheme is usable without a belligerent permit or toll as a condition of passage. The United States is not blockading non-sanctioned commercial calls at Gulf ports. Residual mine risk is not producing casualties or repeated aborts.
- Commercial transit: AIS-visible commercial crossings average at least 70 percent of the pre-crisis baseline of about 85 vessels a day (roughly 60 or more), including oil-major and mainline container operators, not only shadow-fleet or selectively tolerated hulls.
- War-risk insurance: additional hull war-risk premium at or below about 0.5 percent of hull value (about twice the pre-war 0.25 percent), with broad market capacity rather than a thin specialty facility.
- Oil and LNG continuity: combined Gulf crude, condensate, products, and LNG exports, excluding Iran’s pre-war sanctions baseline, within 15 percent of the Q4 2025 run-rate, moving on ordinary commercial cover.
- Energy-market stress: Middle East–Asia VLCC freight no longer structurally war-priced (not on the order of tens of dollars per barrel), and Brent is not being driven by an active Hormuz risk premium. A lower price alone is not sufficient.
- Force posture: routine transit does not require a combat escort surge, and Iranian fast-attack or mining forces are not actively interdicting the southern corridor.
- Escalation triggers: no new major trigger in the prior 14 days, including a leadership strike, a renewed mining campaign, an attack on Gulf-state energy infrastructure, or reimposition of either blockade.

## 4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior

1. Tanker War, 1984–88. Relevant because it is the only modern Gulf shipping war with sustained attacks, elevated insurance, and escort operations. It breaks down because the strait was never fully closed and volumes largely continued. Commercial confidence normalized only after the August 1988 ceasefire, with a lag of months. Suggests 6–18 months after a political settlement, not after a pause in fire.

2. Red Sea / Houthi campaign, 2023–25. Relevant because insurers and liners treated an unresolved threat as structural and rerouted for more than a year despite lulls. It breaks down because Bab el-Mandeb has substitutes and Hormuz does not. Suggests 6–18 months after attacks clearly stop before blue-chip traffic and peacetime war-risk pricing return.

3. 2019 Gulf tanker and Abqaiq attacks. Relevant as a short shock to the same insurance market. It breaks down because there was no war, no mining campaign, and no blockade. Recovery in weeks to about two months once the attack pattern stopped. This is the fast-normalization ceiling, not the base case.

4. Suez closures, 1956 and 1967–75. Relevant if control of the waterway becomes a sovereignty claim rather than a wartime tactic. The 1967 closure lasted about eight years. It breaks down because Hormuz is Iranian and Omani territorial waters with a transit regime, and both Washington and the Gulf exporters have stronger incentives to force a corridor. Suggests a long right tail if the permit regime sticks.

5. 1990–91 Gulf War. Relevant for oil-market behavior after a regional war. It breaks down because Hormuz stayed open. Prices eased within months of the ceasefire; Kuwaiti production took years. Useful for energy stress, weak for chokepoint normality.

6. Operation Earnest Will, 1987–88. Relevant because escorted transit is not commercial normality. Insurance and routing normalized after the political end of the war, not when escorts began.

Outside-in prior for this definition, before case-specific updating: 1 week 3 percent; 1 month 6 percent; 3 months 12 percent; 6 months 22 percent; 1 year 40 percent; 18 months 55 percent. The prior already assumes that full closure cases last longer than raid cases, and that insurance lags any ceasefire.

## 6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization

1. Mutual economic pain. Iran’s ports are blockaded; Gulf exporters and Asian buyers need the strait. That is the strongest reason a deal eventually exists.
2. A live mediator channel (Qatar, Pakistan, Oman) and an already-drafted June framework to return to.
3. Partial physical workarounds already proven: a southern corridor, some US-coordinated transits, and dark Iranian exports. The system is degraded, not destroyed.
4. US ability to suppress Iranian coastal radars and fast-attack craft, which raises the cost of a total closure.
5. Chinese interest in stable Gulf supply, visible in diplomatic support for a return to the June understanding.

## 7. Key Drivers Against Normalization

1. Sequencing deadlock. Iran wants the blockade, sanctions, assets, and Lebanon quiet before it reopens the strait. Washington wants the strait and nuclear concessions first. This is the main gate.
2. Active use of shipping as leverage. Attacks this week show the weapon has not been retired.
3. Insurance memory. Premiums at several percent of hull value keep oil majors and liners out even if a corridor is technically open.
4. Proxy network. Houthis have already hit Saudi targets during this war; a Hormuz deal need not bind them or Hezbollah.
5. Domestic incentives on both sides to look undefeated. A permit regime is now an Iranian institutional claim, not only a tactic.
6. Mine and remining risk, which can veto large-ship traffic after a paper ceasefire.
7. Reported US political calendar risk around the midterms. Unconfirmed, but consistent with the current standoff.

## 9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon

Synthesis rule: the headline probability tracks the joint event, gated by commercial transit plus insurance plus a political arrangement that has survived 14 days. It is below the strike-pause odds and close to, but not identical with, the transit odds, because a traffic recovery without insurance or with a fresh escalation fails the definition.

### 1 week
- Probability: 2%
- Base-rate anchor: 3 percent. Even short Gulf shocks rarely clear insurance and routing inside seven days.
- Current-evidence adjustment: down slightly. Attacks were reported around 1 October, talks just failed a public test, and AIS is at 5 percent.
- Core rationale: a 14-day clean window cannot exist inside seven days unless the prior week was already normal. It was not.
- Main gating factor: active interdiction and insurance.
- What would raise this forecast materially: an immediate, verified mutual halt and a public lifting of both the permit regime and the port blockade, followed by oil-major fixtures. Still unlikely to meet the 14-day test inside a week.
- What would lower this forecast materially: a further ship strike or a announced US strike package.
- Confidence in this estimate: High

### 1 month
- Probability: 5%
- Base-rate anchor: 6 percent.
- Current-evidence adjustment: slightly down. Alcon-type assessments and Reuters both describe an October deal as unlikely; Iran’s condition set was rejected.
- Core rationale: mediators can produce a paper pause. Insurers and majors will not treat October as peacetime after this week’s attacks and seven months of broken truces.
- Main gating factor: sequencing. Iran will not reopen unconditionally while the blockade stands.
- What would raise this forecast materially: a signed return to the June MoU with blockade relief and a week of uneventful, insured transits by non-shadow tonnage.
- What would lower this forecast materially: another multi-ship attack wave or Houthi strikes on Saudi export facilities.
- Confidence in this estimate: High

### 3 months
- Probability: 10%
- Base-rate anchor: 12 percent.
- Current-evidence adjustment: slightly down. Three months covers the US post-midterm window and another winter of energy stress, both escalation risks.
- Core rationale: enough time for a fragile deal and a first mine-clearance pulse, not enough for insurers to reprice to 0.5 percent or for liners to rebuild networks. Container recovery after the June deal was already estimated in months, and that deal failed.
- Main gating factor: insurance and operator re-entry, even if a corridor opens.
- What would raise this forecast materially: a deal in October–November that holds, with AIS above 40 a day and war-risk under 1 percent by December.
- What would lower this forecast materially: remining, or a renewed air campaign after the US elections.
- Confidence in this estimate: Medium

### 6 months
- Probability: 20%
- Base-rate anchor: 22 percent.
- Current-evidence adjustment: small down. The failed June–August cycle shows that a memorandum can raise traffic briefly and still not stick.
- Core rationale: six months is the first horizon at which a real settlement, if reached in the next one to two months, could have produced a 14-day commercial normal. The probability of that settlement itself is well below half.
- Main gating factor: a durable bargain on blockade versus strait control, plus proxy quiet.
- What would raise this forecast materially: blockade lift, end of the permit regime, and no ship attacks for 60 days.
- What would lower this forecast materially: institutionalization of tolls, or a second collapse after a winter deal.
- Confidence in this estimate: Medium

### 1 year
- Probability: 38%
- Base-rate anchor: 40 percent.
- Current-evidence adjustment: small down. Nuclear issues, Lebanon, and the permit claim are unresolved; partial oil flow reduces the urgency that would otherwise force a faster bargain.
- Core rationale: a year is long enough for war-weariness and Asian buyer pressure to produce a working arrangement, and for insurance to follow 60–90 days of clean transit. It is not long enough to treat relapse as unlikely. The June precedent is a direct warning.
- Main gating factor: whether control of Hormuz has been converted from a wartime lever into a standing political claim.
- What would raise this forecast materially: a verified nuclear side-understanding and Gulf-state sign-off on a demilitarized transit scheme.
- What would lower this forecast materially: leadership change in Tehran toward a harder line, or a US decision to keep the port blockade as permanent sanctions enforcement.
- Confidence in this estimate: Low-medium

### 18 months
- Probability: 52%
- Base-rate anchor: 55 percent.
- Current-evidence adjustment: small down. Suez-style sovereignty disputes and Houthi-style persistent threats justify a fat right tail.
- Core rationale: by spring 2028 the modal path is a degraded peace or a managed corridor that finally meets the 14-day test. Just under half the probability mass still sits in renewed closure, a toll regime the market will not call normal, or proxy relapse. Above 80 percent would require assuming the political conflict ends cleanly. Nothing in the file supports that.
- Main gating factor: absence of a new escalation cycle, not the technical state of the waterway.
- What would raise this forecast materially: 90 days of majors, peacetime war-risk, and no proxy attacks on Gulf energy.
- What would lower this forecast materially: a frozen conflict with periodic ship strikes, which can last years.
- Confidence in this estimate: Low

## 10. Most Likely Path

Through October and into early winter, the standoff continues. The US blockade of Iranian ports stays. Iran keeps a selective permit regime and intermittent attacks on non-coordinated hulls. AIS-visible traffic remains a small fraction of normal, while some barrels move dark or under naval coordination. Brent stays war-sensitive. A mediator text in late 2026 is plausible; implementation that survives contact with Lebanon, mines, and the blockade is not the base case before year-end.

The modal 2027 path is a second, narrower arrangement: blockade eased against Iranian non-interference, a monitored corridor, war-risk still above peacetime but falling, and Gulf exports recovering in steps. Functional normality under the definition above is a late-2027 outcome if that arrangement holds, not a 2026 outcome. Proxies remain the most likely spoiler after a paper deal.

## 11. Faster Normalization Case

Washington accepts a sequenced pause: blockade eased first, strait opened under the June text, nuclear talks deferred but with a uranium freeze. Iran stops ship attacks for 45 days. Insurers cut hull war-risk under 1 percent. Oil majors return on Gulf-state cargoes. That path can clear the definition around the six-month horizon and would push the one-year probability toward 60 percent. It requires both sides to reverse stated conditions. Probability of this path inside six months is low, on the order of 15 percent.

## 12. Prolonged Instability Case

Talks fail, a post-election strike round hits Iranian coastal and naval targets, Iran remine and the Houthis hit Saudi or UAE energy sites, and the permit regime hardens into law. Insurers withdraw again. Traffic stays in the single digits to low tens, with only shadow and state-tolerated hulls. This can persist through 18 months and beyond, on the Tanker War plus Red Sea model. This path is less likely than a messy partial reopening, but it is the main reason the 18-month probability stays near a coin flip rather than a strong majority.

## 13. Red-Team Critique

The forecast may understate normalization if AIS at 5 percent is a tracking artifact and true commercial flows are already close to the higher late-summer facilitated counts. If those counts are real and majors are quietly returning, the insurance gate could open faster than the Red Sea analogue implies. It may overstate normalization if Iranian control of the strait has become a regime-survival symbol after Khamenei’s death and the loss of the June deal. In that world, 18-month odds belong closer to 30–35 percent. The load-bearing assumptions are that neither side wants an indefinite total closure, and that insurers will follow 60 days of clean water. Both are reasonable and both have already been wrong once in 2026.

## 14. Critical Signals to Monitor

- Daily AIS and JMIC transits, split by flag, operator, and laden status, not a single headline number.
- Hull war-risk quotes from Marsh or Lloyd’s syndicates, and whether oil majors fix Gulf stems.
- Any text that lifts the US port blockade before, or only after, Iranian nuclear steps.
- UKMTO and JMIC incident reports, especially mining and attacks on non-Iranian tankers.
- Houthi attacks on Saudi or UAE energy facilities.
- Israeli strikes in Lebanon tied by Tehran to the strait.
- Ballast inbound counts. Falling ballast is an early signal that owners have stopped believing a reopening.
