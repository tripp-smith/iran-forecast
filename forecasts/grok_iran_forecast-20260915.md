**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months (from 15 September 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality requires simultaneous satisfaction of all the following observable conditions for a sustained period (not a single-day snapshot or temporary lull):

- Direct military activity: No sustained US/Iran or Iran/Gulf-state kinetic exchanges targeting military or dual-use coastal/maritime assets; no active mining or anti-ship campaigns in the Strait of Hormuz (SoH) or approaches.
- Proxy activity: No significant Iran-aligned proxy attacks (Houthis, Iraqi militias, etc.) on Gulf energy infrastructure, shipping, or US/partner bases that materially affect transit or export confidence.
- Shipping security: Commercial vessels can transit SoH and nearby waters without military escort as the default; attack/incident rate on commercial shipping returns to near pre-February 2026 baseline (<1 confirmed kinetic incident per month on average).
- Commercial transit levels: Daily two-way commercial vessel transits through SoH sustain at ≥70–80% of pre-war baseline (~85–100+ vessels/day per IMF PortWatch/Kpler-type tracking), including meaningful tanker and LNG volumes, for at least 2 consecutive weeks.
- War-risk insurance: Additional war-risk premiums for SoH/Gulf transit fall to ≤2–3× pre-war levels (pre-war ~0.1–0.25% of hull value; current ~1.5%+ or 10–15×+ multiples) and remain available without widespread exclusions or capacity withdrawal.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf producers (Saudi, UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Iraq, residual Iran) restore aggregate crude + condensate + LNG flows through or around SoH to ≥85% of pre-war levels without reliance on emergency pipeline surges or dark fleet dominance; no major terminal or pipeline outages from conflict.
- Energy market stress: Brent crude and related benchmarks no longer embed a large, persistent conflict risk premium driven by Hormuz/Gulf insecurity (prices and volatility consistent with non-conflict fundamentals); no sustained $20+/bbl war premium.
- Force posture: Major actors (US, Iran, Gulf states) have drawn down from wartime forward posture to levels consistent with routine presence or post-conflict stabilization (no dual-carrier sustained presence oriented on Iran, no active large-scale blockade enforcement as primary mission).
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new mine-laying campaigns, large-scale attacks on energy infrastructure, or formal assertions of exclusive Iranian administrative control that re-close commercial access.

A temporary ceasefire, lower oil prices from demand destruction, or escorted “facilitated” transits do **not** count. Battlefield pauses without commercial and insurance normalization do **not** count.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant classes (3–6):

1. **Iran-Iraq “Tanker War” phase (1984–1988)**: Prolonged attacks on shipping in Gulf/SoH approaches. Traffic never fully normalized until war end; insurance elevated for years; recovery of commercial confidence took months after ceasefire. Suggests multi-month to multi-year commercial lag after kinetic reduction. Breaks down: lower intensity, no full-scale great-power involvement, different insurance market.

2. **1990–91 Gulf War / Kuwait invasion aftermath**: Rapid coalition military victory; SoH/Gulf shipping recovered within weeks–months once combat ended and mines cleared, but insurance and residual risk lingered. Suggests 1–3 months for transit recovery post-decisive outcome, longer for full insurance/posture normalization. Breaks down: clear conventional victory and regime change in Kuwait; Iran not the primary antagonist controlling the littoral.

3. **Houthi Red Sea / Bab el-Mandeb campaign (2023–2025+)**: Persistent low-cost attacks caused sustained traffic collapse, insurance spikes, and re-routing despite intermittent pauses. Normalization incomplete years later without full proxy restraint. Suggests months-to-years for insurance and commercial recovery even after major reduction in attacks. Highly relevant to proxy dimension and insurance lag.

4. **Iran nuclear deal / JCPOA period and prior Hormuz threats (2011–12, 2018–19)**: Threats and limited seizures produced short spikes in risk premium and some traffic caution; rapid partial recovery when threats subsided. Suggests weeks for partial transit recovery if pure signaling. Breaks down: no sustained multi-month kinetic war or closure.

5. **Post-conflict maritime recovery after major wars (Falklands, broader post-WWII chokepoint reopenings)**: Physical clearance (mines) + political settlement required; commercial/insurance normalization lagged military pause by 3–12+ months.

Base-rate prior (pre-current-evidence adjustment, treating this as a high-intensity, multi-month interstate + proxy conflict with chokepoint control contest):

- 1 week: ~2–5%
- 1 month: ~8–12%
- 3 months: ~20–30%
- 6 months: ~35–45%
- 1 year: ~50–60%
- 18 months: ~60–70%

These are already lower than pure “ceasefire then reopen” cases because of the demanding multi-domain definition and the historical lag between kinetic reduction and commercial/insurance recovery.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

Ranked by importance:
1. Sustained mutual interest in ending economic pain (oil prices, Iranian revenue, US midterm/political costs) leading to durable political settlement that removes Iranian incentive to contest SoH control.
2. Effective degradation of Iranian anti-ship/mine/coastal surveillance capability reducing ability to enforce restrictions.
3. Gulf-state alignment against Iranian administrative claims + Omani mediation success.
4. Insurer capacity return once attack rates drop for several consecutive weeks.
5. Bypass infrastructure recovery (pipelines) buying time and reducing leverage of pure Hormuz control.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

Ranked by importance:
1. Iranian regime view of SoH control as core residual leverage and survival asset post-leadership changes and attrition; refusal to accept pre-war status quo.
2. Low-cost asymmetric tools (drones, mines, proxies) that allow persistent disruption even under pressure.
3. Insurance and commercial confidence lag: underwriters and owners require multi-week clean record + political clarity, not just military pause.
4. Second-order expansion (Houthi Bab el-Mandeb, Iraqi militia pipeline attacks) creating multi-chokepoint stress.
5. Domestic political incentives on both sides (US midterms, Iranian hardliner consolidation) that reward visible toughness over quiet normalization.
6. Residual mining, dark fleet dynamics, and disputed route schemes that keep risk elevated.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued low-to-medium intensity contest through late 2026 into early 2027, with intermittent pauses, facilitated/escorted traffic, elevated insurance, and partial bypass utilization. Gradual erosion of Iranian denial capacity and mutual economic pain produce a political arrangement by mid-to-late 2027 that allows commercial recovery, but full multi-domain normality (especially insurance and posture) lags the political announcement by months. Traffic and exports recover faster than insurance and force posture.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Decisive political settlement in the next 4–8 weeks that explicitly restores unrestricted navigation, combined with rapid, verified degradation of Iranian coastal anti-ship systems and a clean multi-week incident record. Insurers respond within 4–6 weeks; traffic rebounds to 60%+ by 3 months. Probability of this path is low (~15–20% conditional on early settlement).

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Iran prioritizes SoH leverage indefinitely; proxies expand attacks on pipelines and secondary chokepoints; insurance market structurally reprices Gulf risk higher; US maintains elevated posture through 2027. Traffic remains <30–40% of baseline for 12+ months. This path is plausible (~25–30%).

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Possible overestimation of normalization: The definition is deliberately demanding; historical tanker-war and Red Sea cases show commercial/insurance recovery can lag kinetic reduction by years. Iranian internal logic may treat SoH control as non-negotiable after leadership attrition. Underestimation risk: Mutual economic damage and US political calendar could force a faster settlement than base rates suggest; capability degradation may be more effective than visible. Load-bearing assumptions include continued Iranian regime coherence and the persistence of asymmetric tools. Evidence would falsify the forecast if traffic sustains >60 vessels/day with premiums <3× for 3+ consecutive weeks without a political deal, or if a deal produces immediate commercial recovery contrary to historical lag.
