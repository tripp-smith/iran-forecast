**1. Forecast Question**  
Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each horizon: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
Functional normality requires simultaneous satisfaction of all of the following observable conditions for a sustained period (not a temporary pause):

- Direct military activity: No sustained Iranian strikes on GCC energy/military infrastructure, no major US/coalition strikes on Iranian territory or shipping, and no reciprocal large-scale naval exchanges in the Gulf/Hormuz for ≥14 consecutive days.
- Proxy activity: No significant Houthi or other Iran-aligned attacks on commercial shipping or GCC energy assets that materially disrupt flows; no major activation of Iraqi militias or Hezbollah that spills into Gulf energy corridors.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Physical transit lanes free of active mining, systematic drone/missile attacks on commercial vessels, and coercive boarding/seizure campaigns. UKMTO/JMIC risk assessments at “moderate” or lower with no cluster of incidents in a 7-day window.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits through Hormuz recovering to ≥70% of pre-war baseline (pre-war ~120–140+ vessels/day; target ≥85–100), including meaningful crude and LNG tanker volumes, with AIS-visible and dark traffic both rising without US military escort dependency as the primary mode.
- War-risk insurance conditions: War-risk premiums for Hormuz/Gulf voyages declining to levels that allow major commercial insurers and P&I clubs to offer cover at commercially viable rates (not prohibitive multiples of pre-war), with reinstatement of standard cover rather than specialized wartime markets only.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf crude and LNG loadings/export volumes recovering toward pre-war norms without sustained force majeure declarations or reliance on ship-to-ship transfers outside the strait as the dominant workaround; Qatari LNG and major GCC crude flows no longer structurally constrained by Hormuz risk.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent volatility and risk premia normalizing (not locked in elevated levels driven by Hormuz closure risk); no persistent $20–40+ war premium relative to non-Gulf benchmarks solely from Gulf disruption.
- Force posture of major actors: US/coalition and Iranian naval postures shifting from active interdiction/blockade and high-tempo combat operations toward deterrence/monitoring; no active “steel wall” blockade or Iranian “prohibited zone” enforcement as primary tools.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new large-scale attacks on critical energy infrastructure (pipelines, terminals, desalination, nuclear plants), no carrier/group-level engagements, and no expansion of the conflict that re-closes commercial access.

A temporary ceasefire, lower oil prices from demand destruction or alternative routing, or a battlefield pause alone do **not** meet the standard. Commercial and insurance normalization must accompany any military de-escalation.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant classes (selected for Gulf/chokepoint/maritime disruption + regional war dynamics):

1. **Tanker War phase of Iran-Iraq War (1980s)**: Prolonged attacks on shipping; partial recovery only after sustained diplomatic/military pressure and war end. Functional commercial normalization lagged military pauses by many months to years. Suggests multi-year tail risk for full recovery.
2. **2019–2021 Gulf tanker attacks / Abqaiq / Fujairah incidents + Houthi Red Sea campaign (2023–2025)**: Discrete spikes followed by insurance spikes and routing avoidance; recovery to near-normal required sustained de-escalation and credible security guarantees (months for partial, longer for full insurance normalization).
3. **Suez Canal closures / major chokepoint disruptions (1956, 1967–75, 2021 Ever Given)**: Physical reopening ≠ commercial normality. Insurance, charterer confidence, and volumes lagged. Suggests 1–6+ months after physical access restores before volumes and rates normalize.
4. **Post-ceasefire maritime recovery in other high-intensity conflicts (e.g., elements of Gulf War 1991, limited post-2003 Iraq maritime)**: Military pauses did not immediately restore commercial traffic; residual mine risk, proxy activity, and insurance inertia delayed full normality by 3–12+ months.
5. **Houthi Red Sea disruption peak-to-partial recovery cycles**: Even after US/UK strikes and temporary pauses, commercial volumes and insurance took extended periods to recover; full pre-disruption normality often incomplete while underlying political conflict persisted.

**Breakdown points**: Current case involves simultaneous interstate war, active mutual blockades/claims of control, nuclear-adjacent stakes, multiple chokepoints (Hormuz + Bab al-Mandeb), and energy infrastructure targeting—more severe and multi-domain than pure tanker-war or discrete attack episodes.

**Base-rate prior (before current evidence adjustment)**: Very low near-term recovery probabilities that rise only gradually. Approximate prior probabilities of meeting the full operational definition: 1 week <5%; 1 month ~5–10%; 3 months ~15–25%; 6 months ~30–40%; 1 year ~45–55%; 18 months ~55–65%. These already embed the historical lag between military de-escalation and commercial/insurance normalization.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Exhaustion + mutual interest in revenue restoration (Iran needs oil exports; GCC needs reliable transit; US political calendar).  
2. Diplomatic track (Oman, Qatar, possible UN/GCC forums) producing a workable transit regime.  
3. Credible security guarantees or de facto mutual restraint that restores insurer confidence.  
4. Alternative routing and stock draws that reduce immediate pressure, creating space for deals.  
5. Houthi restraint if separate understandings hold.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Fundamental disagreement over who controls Hormuz transit rules (Iran claims vs US freedom of navigation).  
2. Residual military capability and willingness on both sides to resume strikes.  
3. Proxy and secondary chokepoint risks (Houthis, Iraqi militias).  
4. Insurance market inertia and charterer risk aversion—commercial recovery lags military pauses.  
5. Domestic political incentives (US midterms rhetoric, Iranian hardline factions, regime survival narratives).  
6. Infrastructure damage and force posture that take time to reverse.

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Managed instability / “no war, no peace” for several more months: intermittent low-level maritime and proxy incidents, partial traffic recovery under escort or dark sailing, elevated but fluctuating oil prices, and repeated diplomatic attempts that produce temporary pauses rather than the full operational package. Gradual improvement in volumes and insurance only after a more durable political arrangement, most likely post-US midterms or after further mutual attrition.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Decisive mutual restraint triggered by economic pain + political calendars, producing a monitored interim transit regime within 1–3 months that insurers rapidly price in. Traffic rebounds toward 70%+, premiums fall, and proxy activity is contained. Requires both sides to prioritize revenue and domestic politics over maximalist control claims.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Unresolved dispute over Hormuz rules + residual capabilities keep the strait commercially impaired for 12–18+ months. Periodic attacks, Houthi pressure on the Red Sea, and insurance aversion maintain a structural risk premium. Gulf states hedge further; energy markets adapt via longer routes and stock management rather than full Hormuz recovery.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Possible overestimation of normalization: Historical chokepoint recoveries often take longer than expected once insurance and charterer confidence are damaged; current mutual claims of control create a harder coordination problem than pure tanker-war episodes. Possible underestimation: Mutual economic incentives and US political calendar could produce faster tactical de-escalation than base rates imply; adaptive workarounds (STS transfers, pipelines, dark fleets) already blunt some pressure. Load-bearing assumptions include continued Iranian capacity to threaten shipping and continued insurer risk aversion. The forecast could be wrong if a sudden comprehensive deal materializes or if one side suffers a decisive military/political collapse.
