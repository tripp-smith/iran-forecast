**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each of these horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality requires the simultaneous, sustained satisfaction of the following observable conditions for the relevant horizon (not a temporary pause or partial recovery). Temporary ceasefires, lower oil prices alone, or battlefield pauses do not qualify.

- **Direct military activity**: No sustained U.S./coalition kinetic strikes on Iranian territory and no major Iranian ballistic-missile or large-scale drone campaigns against U.S. forces, Gulf states, or critical infrastructure for at least 45 consecutive days.
- **Proxy activity**: Houthi and other Iranian-backed proxy attacks on Saudi (and other Gulf) airports, energy infrastructure, and shipping reduced to sporadic, non-systemically disruptive levels (≤2 successful significant strikes per month) for ≥30 days.
- **Shipping security (Hormuz and nearby waters)**: No successful attacks on commercial vessels in the Strait of Hormuz, Gulf of Oman, or adjacent Gulf waters for ≥30 days; Joint Maritime Information Centre (JMIC)/UKMTO threat assessment reduced from Severe/Critical to Moderate or lower; free transit without Iranian permit requirements, systematic mining, or projectile threats.
- **Commercial transit levels**: Estimated daily commercial vessel transits (AIS-visible plus dark/estimated) recover to ≥75–80% of pre-war baseline (~100–115 of ~130–140 daily).
- **War-risk insurance**: Additional war-risk premiums (AWRP) fall to ≤0.5–1% of hull value with multi-voyage cover available on market terms without heavy reliance on government backstops or single-voyage restrictions.
- **Oil and LNG export continuity**: Total Gulf oil exports (Hormuz + pipeline bypasses such as Saudi East-West and UAE Fujairah) sustain ≥18 mb/d; Hormuz oil itself ≥12–15 mb/d; Qatar LNG exports recover to ≥70% of pre-war capacity on a sustained basis (accounting for any unrepaired physical damage to trains).
- **Energy market stress and volatility**: Hormuz-attributable risk premium in Brent < ~$10/bbl sustained; 30-day volatility returns toward historical norms rather than being dominated by chokepoint shocks.
- **Force posture of major actors**: U.S. carrier presence reduced to ≤1 strike group in the CENTCOM area; no active naval blockade of Iranian commercial ports; Iranian forces not systematically interdicting commercial traffic.
- **Absence of major new escalation triggers**: No clear nuclear breakout signals, major new multi-front openings, or large-scale proxy expansions for ≥60 days.

All elements must hold together. Partial military quiet or partial oil recovery is insufficient.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot** (as of mid-October 2026, campaign day ~229–231)

The U.S.-Israel campaign against Iran began around 28 February 2026. Direct U.S. strikes on Iranian territory are currently paused (Trump statement of 8 October that none would occur before the 3 November midterms while talks continue). Iranian and proxy activity has not paused. Hormuz shipping attacks reached war highs (reports of 10–12+ vessels struck in single weeks in early–mid October; container ship sinkings and fires reported). AIS-visible commercial traffic remains severely depressed (~8–10% of pre-war in recent weekly averages; hundreds of vessels waiting). Oil flows through Hormuz and via bypasses have partially recovered (recent weekly estimates fluctuating roughly 9–15+ mb/d against a pre-war ~17–21 mb/d baseline) but remain fragile and sensitive to attack spikes. Qatar LNG exports remain heavily constrained (force majeure extended; September volumes a fraction of pre-war; some physical damage to Ras Laffan trains reported). Houthi attacks on Saudi airports (including repeated strikes on Riyadh’s King Khalid International, with dozens killed and hundreds wounded) and other targets have intensified. War-risk insurance is available but on restricted/single-voyage terms with elevated premiums (reports ranging from ~1.5% to 5–10% of hull value in recent periods). Talks exist but remain deadlocked on core issues including Hormuz control and enrichment. U.S. force posture includes multiple carrier strike groups with additional reinforcements en route or overlapping. Brent has traded in the ~$100–107 range recently. Confirmed facts and claims by parties frequently diverge; independent tracking (Kpler, TankerTrackers, UKMTO, AIS aggregators) is weighted more heavily than unilateral assertions of control or closure.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant historical cases:

- **Iran-Iraq Tanker War (1984–88)**: Sustained attacks on hundreds of vessels; flows largely continued under protection/reflagging; full commercial normalization followed the end of major hostilities. Relevant for residual attack risk and insurance lag. Breaks down because today’s precision weapons, dark shipping, and Iranian dependence on (or willingness to disrupt) the strait differ, and the current war involves direct U.S. involvement plus active proxies. Suggests multi-month lag after military de-escalation.
- **Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb Houthi campaign (late 2023–ongoing)**: Large-scale carrier diversion and persistent elevated risk/insurance years later despite military responses. Relevant for proxy persistence and incomplete commercial recovery. Breaks down because Hormuz has fewer practical sea alternatives for Gulf oil/LNG originators. Suggests years of partial normalization is possible.
- **2019 Gulf of Oman tanker attacks**: Brief disruption followed by relatively rapid commercial recovery after de-escalation. Relevant for short-lived spikes. Breaks down given the scale and duration of the 2026 war. Suggests weeks-to-months if threat truly ends.
- **Suez Canal closures (1956, especially 1967–75)**: Multi-year physical and political closure. Relevant for political commitment to disruption. Breaks down because Hormuz has never been fully sealed for long periods and has partial pipeline bypasses. Suggests long tails when political will persists.
- **Ever Given Suez blockage (2021)**: Days-long physical block with rapid recovery. Low relevance beyond illustrating short mechanical events.
- **Brief post-MoU windows in the current war (June 2026)**: Partial traffic and insurance easing that reversed quickly. Illustrates fragility of pauses.

**Base-rate prior** (outside-in, before case-specific updates): Military pauses in regional wars with maritime dimensions often produce partial commercial recovery within 1–3 months but full functional normality (insurance, transit volumes, proxy restraint, force posture) commonly takes 6–18+ months when residual threats and political incentives remain. Prior probabilities of full functional normality:

- 1 week: ~5%
- 1 month: ~15%
- 3 months: ~35%
- 6 months: ~55%
- 1 year: ~70%
- 18 months: ~80%

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

- Declared U.S. pause until midterms and existence of a talks channel (near-term military quiet possible).
- Partial oil recovery via U.S.-protected transit, dark shipping, and pipeline bypasses (Saudi East-West, UAE Fujairah), demonstrating commercial adaptation.
- Market and insurer capacity that has not fully withdrawn (cover available at a price).
- Shared interest in avoiding total energy shock and further economic damage.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

- Sustained high tempo of Iranian attacks on commercial shipping (record levels) and explicit Iranian political leverage over Hormuz.
- Escalating Houthi campaign against Saudi airports and infrastructure (proxy front active and expanding).
- Elevated, restricted insurance market and operator risk aversion (transit far below pre-war).
- Stalled negotiations on core issues (Hormuz regime, enrichment, blockade).
- Residual U.S. force buildup and Iranian reconstitution of strike capabilities.
- Physical constraints on full LNG recovery and second-order effects on buyer confidence and Gulf state politics.
- Political calendar (U.S. midterms) and incentive for Iran to maintain pressure.

Ranked by importance: (1) Hormuz attack tempo and residual interdiction capacity; (2) proxy (Houthi) restraint; (3) insurance and operator confidence; (4) diplomatic resolution durability; (5) force-posture drawdown.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued military pause or limited exchanges around the U.S. midterms, followed by either a fragile diplomatic arrangement or renewed pressure. Oil flows remain partial and volatile via protected/dark transit and bypasses. Shipping volumes and insurance recover slowly and incompletely while residual attack capability and Houthi activity persist. Full multi-domain normality is more likely in the 9–18 month window than earlier, contingent on a political resolution that reduces Iranian incentives to use Hormuz as leverage.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

A post-midterm agreement that includes verifiable Hormuz security arrangements, proxy restraint, and force drawdowns, combined with rapid insurer and operator re-entry. Historical short-spike recoveries (2019-type) plus current adaptation capacity support this as possible if political will aligns. Probability of this path is non-trivial but secondary.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Talks fail, low-level attacks and Houthi pressure continue as leverage, insurance remains restricted, and commercial traffic stays depressed for a year or more (Red Sea analogy plus Iranian political incentives). Physical LNG constraints and buyer diversification reinforce the lag. This path is plausible and keeps long-horizon probabilities well below certainty.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

The forecast may understate near-term oil resilience (trackers already show substantial dark/protected flows) and overstate the durability of Iranian interdiction capacity if U.S. protection scales. Conversely, it may understate proxy persistence and insurance hysteresis—the Red Sea experience shows commercial operators and underwriters can remain risk-averse for years even after intensity drops. Load-bearing assumptions are that residual Iranian strike capacity remains usable for leverage and that Houthi activity is not quickly decoupled. Evidence of a verified multi-week attack halt plus multi-voyage insurance availability would force an upward revision; sustained high attack rates or new fronts would force a downward one.
