**1. Forecast Question**  
Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each horizon: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
Functional normality requires simultaneous satisfaction of the following observable conditions over a continuous 30-day period ending on or before the horizon date (temporary pauses, lower oil prices, or escorted limited traffic do not qualify):  
- No confirmed major direct kinetic strikes by US/Israeli forces on Iranian territory/bases or by Iranian forces on US/partner Gulf bases or territory.  
- No confirmed Iranian or proxy (Houthi, Iraqi militia, etc.) attacks on commercial shipping in the Strait of Hormuz, Persian Gulf, or Gulf of Oman.  
- Commercial AIS-visible vessel transits through Hormuz ≥70% of the 2025 baseline (~60+ per day; pre-war AIS averages were roughly 80–90 commercial vessels/day).  
- War-risk insurance for Hormuz transits commercially available at or near pre-war levels (additional premiums ≤1–2% of hull value) without primary reliance on government reinsurance facilities as the market’s main support.  
- Combined oil and LNG export volumes from Gulf producers (via Hormuz, pipelines, and STS) ≥80% of 2025 average levels on a sustained basis.  
- US force posture reduced to routine peacetime presence (no sustained multi-carrier surge or active naval blockade of Iranian ports).  
- Proxies not conducting attacks on shipping or Gulf states.  
- No major new escalation triggers (large missile barrages, confirmed nuclear breakout progress, or significant hits on Gulf energy infrastructure).  

**3. Current Situation Snapshot**

Intermittent US strikes (including recent hits on Qeshm Island) continue alongside Iranian drone/missile attacks on US-related facilities in Bahrain and Kuwait and ongoing incidents against commercial shipping. A June memorandum of understanding has expired; talks are stalemated. The US maintains a naval blockade targeting Iranian-origin cargoes and has expanded rules on ship-to-ship transfers.  

AIS-visible and facilitated commercial traffic through Hormuz is a small fraction of the 2025 baseline (pre-war ~85 daily commercial AIS transits in some series; current facilitated averages cited in the 20s, with much lower AIS-visible counts). **4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
- **Tanker War (1984–1988)**: Prolonged maritime attacks during the Iran-Iraq War. Relevant for Gulf shipping disruption under active conflict. Breaks down because volume impact was limited (~2% disruption at peak) and full recovery required the 1988 ceasefire. Suggests multi-year instability until political resolution.  
- **Houthi Red Sea campaign (2023–2025+)**: Proxy attacks on shipping caused sustained rerouting even after pauses; commercial return lagged attack cessation by months. Relevant for insurance and behavioral lag. Breaks down as smaller scale and different chokepoint. Suggests 3–12+ months post-pause for meaningful normalization.  
- **Limited Gulf crises / mine threats (e.g., 1980s mine responses, later threats)**: Temporary spikes resolved faster with escort operations once political signals clarified. Relevant for force posture effects. Breaks down because current case has active kinetic exchanges and blockade. Suggests weeks to months once strikes stop.  
- **Broader regional wars with chokepoint effects**: Oil shocks often persist until clear de-escalation.  

**Base-rate prior (pre-current-evidence adjustment)**: 1 week ~1%; 1 month ~5%; 3 months ~15%; 6 months ~30%; 1 year ~50%; 18 months ~65%. These reflect historical patterns where commercial and insurance normalization trail military pauses and require political settlement.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**  
- US political constraints from midterm losses and Democratic Congress (limits large new campaigns).  
- Shared interest in oil flows (US statements on keeping Hormuz open; partial traffic continues).  
- Economic pressure on Iran and fatigue on all sides.  
- Government insurance facilities as temporary bridge.  

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**  
- Active low-intensity kinetic exchanges and shipping attacks (primary).  
- Unresolved political disputes (nuclear issues, sanctions, blockade, Hormuz management).  
- Insurance market caution and behavioral lag by shippers.  
- Proxy residual capacity and force posture.  
- Sequencing disagreements in talks.

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Continued low-intensity exchanges and elevated risk through early 2027, with intermittent facilitated traffic. Gradual political movement (driven by US domestic constraints and economic costs) produces a durable pause or limited agreement in the 3–9 month window, after which commercial traffic and insurance recover over subsequent months. Full multi-domain normality is more likely in the 6–18 month range than sooner.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Rapid mutual pause in the next 1–2 months driven by US political pressure and Iranian economic pain, followed by sequenced talks that produce shipping assurances. Insurance markets respond within weeks of a clean period; traffic recovers to 70%+ by month 3–4. Probability of this path: ~15–20%.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Persistent low-level attacks, failed talks, and periodic escalations keep insurance elevated and traffic suppressed for 12–18+ months. Proxy activity or nuclear disputes re-ignite cycles. Probability of this path: ~25–30%.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Strongest case against higher long-horizon probabilities: Political settlement may remain elusive even with time; insurance markets are highly conservative after repeated losses, and residual proxy capacity can restart attacks quickly. Strongest case for faster normalization: Midterm results and oil interests create stronger incentives than historical analogs, and partial traffic already shows adaptation. Load-bearing assumptions: that the current intensity does not escalate into major new campaigns, and that commercial actors will treat a sustained pause as durable. Evidence that would prove the view wrong includes a major new strike campaign or a sudden comprehensive agreement.
