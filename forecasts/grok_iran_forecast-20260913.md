**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War (began 28 February 2026) by: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months from 13 September 2026.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

“Return to functional normality” requires simultaneous, sustained satisfaction of all of the following observable criteria for at least 14 consecutive days (to exclude temporary pauses):

- Direct military activity: No ballistic missile, cruise missile, or large-scale drone strikes by Iran or the US/Israel coalition against each other’s territory, bases, or major military assets in the Gulf region; no major kinetic exchanges between state forces.
- Proxy activity: No significant attacks by Iranian-aligned groups (Houthis, Iraqi militias, residual Hezbollah elements) on commercial shipping, Gulf energy infrastructure, or major US/coalition assets that cause material disruption or casualties.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Open, unescorted commercial transit with incident rates (attacks, mine encounters, boardings, or seizures) at or below pre-war baseline levels; no active “prohibited zones,” tolls, or forced routing claims enforced by force.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily AIS-visible + estimated dark transits averaging ≥80–90% of the pre-war baseline of ~120–140 large commercial vessels (including a substantial share of crude and LNG carriers).
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums for Hormuz/Persian Gulf transit returned to ≤2–3× pre-crisis baseline (pre-crisis ~0.1–0.25% of hull value per voyage); cover widely available without capacity rationing or extreme exclusions.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Combined Gulf crude + condensate + LNG export volumes (Hormuz + bypass routes) recovered to ≥85–90% of pre-war levels, with Iran’s exports no longer subject to an effective near-total blockade and major producers (Saudi, UAE, Kuwait, Qatar, Iraq) operating without force majeure or large shut-ins driven by security.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent crude trading in a range consistent with structural supply/demand (no persistent war-premium spike above ~$20–25/bbl attributable to Hormuz risk); realized volatility and risk premia in energy markets no longer dominated by Gulf kinetic headlines.
- Force posture of major actors: Material reduction in elevated US/coalition naval and air presence dedicated to Hormuz escort/mine-clearance/blockade enforcement relative to peak 2026 levels; Iranian naval/IRGC posture no longer oriented toward interdiction of the strait.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new leadership assassinations, nuclear threshold crossings, or third-party interventions that reset the conflict trajectory.

A temporary ceasefire, partial transit recovery under escort, lower oil prices driven by demand destruction or non-Gulf supply, or a battlefield pause without commercial and insurance normalization does **not** qualify.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant historical reference classes (selected for maritime chokepoint disruption + regional war elements):

1. **1980s Iran-Iraq Tanker War (1984–1988 peak phase)**: Systematic attacks on shipping in the Gulf/Hormuz approaches. Traffic never fully stopped but was heavily disrupted; US reflagging/escorts (Earnest Will) and kinetic responses (Praying Mantis) occurred. Functional commercial recovery lagged the 1988 ceasefire by months; elevated risk persisted. Timeline suggestion: partial stabilization 3–9 months after major de-escalation; full commercial normality closer to 12–18+ months. Breaks down because the current conflict involves direct US-Iran state-on-state exchanges, nuclear dimensions, and far higher economic stakes for global oil markets.

2. **2019 Gulf tanker attacks + seizures (Iran attributed)**: Limited number of incidents; traffic continued with heightened insurance and some rerouting. Recovery of confidence was relatively rapid (weeks to a few months) once attacks subsided. Timeline: functional shipping normality within 1–3 months of cessation. Breaks down due to far lower intensity and absence of sustained blockade or large-scale war.

3. **2023–2025 Red Sea/Houthi disruptions**: Prolonged low-intensity attacks caused major rerouting (Cape of Good Hope), elevated insurance, and volume drops on the Bab el-Mandeb route. Partial recovery occurred with naval task forces and intermittent de-escalation, but residual risk and higher costs persisted for 12–24+ months. Timeline: meaningful volume recovery 6–12 months after peak intensity declines; full insurance/commercial normality longer. Relevant for proxy persistence and insurance stickiness; less relevant for a true chokepoint closure involving major powers.

4. **1990–91 Gulf War oil disruption**: Rapid military resolution of the core conflict; oil production recovery within months, though infrastructure and market effects lingered. Timeline: functional energy flows within 3–6 months of ceasefire. Breaks down because the current war lacks a clear decisive military endpoint and involves Iranian control of the primary chokepoint.

5. **Post-ceasefire maritime recoveries after major conflicts (generalized from WWII Atlantic, various post-colonial wars)**: Commercial shipping and insurance typically lag kinetic cessation by 3–12 months due to residual mines, risk perception, and capacity rebuilding. Base rates favor slower commercial/insurance normalization than military pauses.

**Base-rate prior (before case-specific evidence)** derived from the above, weighting Tanker War and Red Sea more heavily given duration and intensity:  
- 1 week: <5%  
- 1 month: 8–12%  
- 3 months: 20–30%  
- 6 months: 40–55%  
- 1 year: 60–75%  
- 18 months: 75–85%  

These reflect that kinetic pauses often arrive before commercial normality, and full multi-domain recovery is slow.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

1. Economic exhaustion on both sides (Iranian currency collapse, oil revenue loss; US/Gulf fiscal and political costs of prolonged high energy prices). Rank: High.  
2. Existence of diplomatic channels (Oman meeting; prior MOU precedent). Rank: Medium-High.  
3. US military dominance at sea enabling gradual corridor enforcement if Iranian interdiction capacity is further degraded. Rank: Medium.  
4. Gulf states’ strong preference for de-escalation and export recovery. Rank: Medium.  
5. Global demand destruction and non-Gulf supply growth reducing the strategic value of prolonged disruption. Rank: Medium.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

1. Iranian strategic reliance on Hormuz leverage as residual power after conventional and nuclear degradation. Rank: Highest.  
2. Mutual distrust and repeated collapse of prior de-escalation attempts (June MOU). Rank: Highest.  
3. Proxy networks (Houthis, Iraqi militias) that can sustain low-cost disruption independently. Rank: High.  
4. Insurance and commercial risk perception lag (stickiness of elevated premiums and vessel avoidance even after kinetic pauses). Rank: High.  
5. Domestic political incentives in Iran and the US/Israel for continued pressure or demonstration of resolve. Rank: Medium-High.  
6. Potential for new triggers (pipeline attacks, nuclear developments, leadership incidents). Rank: Medium-High.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Intermittent diplomacy produces temporary pauses and partial transit recovery under continued elevated risk and insurance costs. Full multi-domain normality arrives gradually between the 6- and 18-month marks if economic costs force Iranian recalibration and no major new trigger resets the conflict. Shipping and insurance lag kinetic developments by months.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

A successful Oman process expands into a verified, monitored ceasefire with mutual de-escalation steps, rapid mine clearance confirmation, transit surge above 50–60% within weeks, and insurer response. Probability of reaching the definition by 3 months rises toward 40–45% in this branch; by 6 months toward 65–70%. Requires Iranian willingness to trade leverage for sanctions relief and US willingness to ease the blockade without total Iranian capitulation.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Iran treats Hormuz control as non-negotiable residual leverage; proxies sustain disruption; nuclear file or leadership dynamics produce new triggers. Transit remains depressed, insurance elevated, and exports structurally impaired. Probabilities fall to single digits at 3 months, ~25% at 6 months, and ~45–50% at 18 months. This path is supported by the June MOU collapse and current Iranian rhetoric.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

The forecast may overestimate normalization speed by under-weighting Iranian domestic politics and the regime’s demonstrated willingness to absorb economic pain for strategic autonomy. It may underestimate speed if US military pressure plus Gulf diplomacy produces a more decisive Iranian climb-down than historical analogs suggest. The strict multi-domain definition is deliberately demanding; looser definitions focused only on reduced kinetic activity would produce higher probabilities. Load-bearing assumptions include continued US naval presence and the absence of a catastrophic escalation (e.g., major base strike with mass casualties or nuclear threshold event). Evidence base for precise current transit and insurance figures remains somewhat fragmented across trackers and brokers.
