I will ground the forecast in current reporting on the 2026 Iran conflict, Hormuz traffic, war-risk insurance, and energy flows, then build the probabilities from reference classes outward.The Persian Gulf is not near functional normality. On 3 October 2026 the disruption is in roughly day 217–218. A June memorandum produced a brief traffic rebound and then failed. Direct strikes on shipping resumed in September and continued into early October. Oil barrels have partially recovered under escort and dark transit; commercial conditions have not.

## 1. Forecast Question

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months from 3 October 2026.

## 2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”

All of the following must hold at the same time, for at least 14 consecutive days, and must not depend on ad-hoc naval escort or selective political permission as the operating system:

- Direct military activity: no state kinetic strikes on commercial vessels or Gulf energy infrastructure in the prior 14 days, and no active declared closure or toll regime enforced by force.
- Proxy activity: successful proxy strikes on Gulf shipping or energy infrastructure at or below the pre-February 2026 baseline.
- Shipping security: standard international lanes in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby Gulf of Oman usable without mine threat requiring convoy, and without a requirement to use an IRGC-designated northern corridor.
- Commercial transit: AIS-visible plus independently corroborated transits at or above 70 percent of the pre-crisis norm (about 50 or more commercial vessels per day against a baseline of roughly 74–85).
- War-risk insurance: additional war-risk premium within about 2 times the peacetime baseline, and major International Group P&I clubs restored to ordinary cover rather than cancellation or voyage-by-voyage exception.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: combined Gulf crude and LNG exports at or above 85 percent of the February 2026 baseline, without blockade waivers or selective exemptions as the binding mechanism.
- Energy-market stress: the Hormuz-specific risk premium in crude no longer the dominant price driver (illustratively under about $5–8 per barrel on a sustained basis), and freight and insurance no longer pricing the route as a war zone.
- Force posture: no active U.S. blockade of Iranian ports and no ongoing strike campaign; a residual naval presence is compatible with normality, a wartime interdiction regime is not.
- Escalation triggers: no new mine campaign, no fresh strike on critical Gulf-state energy infrastructure, and no collapse of whatever political arrangement exists into a renewed open campaign.

A ceasefire, a single strong oil-export day, or lower headline prices do not meet this definition.

## 3. Current Situation Snapshot

The war began with U.S. and Israeli strikes on 28 February 2026. Traffic collapsed within days. A memorandum in mid-June produced a short recovery: JMIC recorded 54 transits on 24 June, the high since the outbreak, and cumulative transits rose through the summer. That arrangement collapsed on 8 July. Fighting and shipping attacks resumed in early September.

As of 1–3 October:

- Live AIS monitors put visible commercial transits at roughly 3 to 6 per day, about 3–6 percent of the pre-crisis norm of about 74–85. IMF PortWatch’s lagged 7-day average was about 3 per day into late September. Some U.S.-facilitated and dark-transit counts are higher: one monitor put late-September facilitated traffic near the mid-20s per day, and Windward logged 119 transits in the week of 23–30 September, more than a third dark.
- Iran has struck commercial ships in the strait in late September and early October, including tankers transiting dark off Musandam and the Kuwait-flagged Kazimah III on 1 October. The New York Times, citing a Western security official, reported roughly 30 drone and 10 anti-ship missile launches per week against commercial ships since August, with at least four ships hit in the prior 10 days. CENTCOM has answered with strikes and described the shipping attacks as ceasefire violations.
- A U.S. naval blockade of Iranian ports remains in effect. UANI reported 125 commercial vessels redirected by 30 September.
- War-risk pricing is still extreme. Trackers cite roughly 15 times to 40 times peacetime premia; earlier in the war, seven International Group clubs cancelled Gulf war-risk cover and hull premia moved from about 0.25 percent to about 3 percent of vessel value.
- Kpler data cited by Al Jazeera put September Middle East crude exports near 16.3 million barrels per day, just under 80 percent of the February level and about 3.2 million barrels per day short. That recovery is fragile: late-September attacks and the East–West pipeline strike have already reversed confidence. LNG remains far more impaired than crude in available reporting.
- Mines, a selective Iranian permit system, Houthi pressure on the Bab el-Mandeb, and at least one reported strike on Saudi bypass infrastructure are all live second-order constraints.

Confirmed: the war is in its eighth month, a ceasefire already failed, shipping attacks and U.S. retaliation occurred in the last two weeks, and open commercial traffic is a small fraction of normal. Disputed: exact daily transit counts, because dark traffic and facilitated transits diverge sharply from AIS. Inferred: insurance, not naval presence alone, remains the binding constraint on full commercial return.

## 4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior

1. Tanker War, 1984–1988. Relevant because a state actor used limited attacks on commercial shipping to raise insurance costs without a full physical blockade. It breaks down because that campaign sat inside a longer Iran–Iraq war and ended only with the political end of the war. Partial traffic continued for years under threat. Suggests functional commercial normality only after a settlement, often 12–36 months after the political decision, not during active strikes.

2. 2019 Gulf tanker and Abqaiq attacks. Relevant as a short Gulf shipping and energy shock with an insurance spike. It breaks down because attacks were episodic, not embedded in an open U.S.–Iran war with a blockade. Traffic and premia normalized within weeks to a few months once the immediate threat receded. Suggests a 1–3 month recovery only if strikes stop and stay stopped.

3. Red Sea / Houthi campaign, late 2023 into 2025. Most useful commercial analogue. Attacks did not have to be daily to keep major liners off the route; Cape diversion and elevated war risk persisted well over a year after the peak. It breaks down because Hormuz has fewer substitutes than Suez. Suggests 6–18 months from the last credible attack to anything like insurance and liner normality, and longer if attacks recur.

4. Operation Earnest Will, 1987–1988. Relevant because escorted transit restored some flow while the threat continued. It breaks down because escort is not the definition used here. Suggests barrels can move in months while commercial normality waits on the end of the threat.

5. Black Sea grain corridor, 2022–2024. Relevant as a politically brokered, insurance-dependent reopening that repeatedly failed. Suggests intermittent openings are the base case, not durable normality, until the military facts change.

6. 1990–91 Gulf War. Weak fit. Hormuz itself stayed open. Useful only as a reminder that a short, decisive campaign can limit chokepoint damage; this war has already not taken that path.

Base-rate prior before case-specific updating, for a chokepoint war still short of settlement:

- 1 week: 3 percent
- 1 month: 7 percent
- 3 months: 15 percent
- 6 months: 28 percent
- 1 year: 42 percent
- 18 months: 55 percent

## 6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization

1. Both sides have already shown they can pause. The June window produced a real transit rebound, so the operational path exists.
2. Crude exports recovered to roughly 80 percent of pre-war in September, which shows producers and some charterers will move barrels when protection is credible.
3. A de facto hotline and limited retaliatory strikes suggest neither side currently wants uncontrolled escalation.
4. Gulf producers have bypass capacity and a commercial incentive to restore flows; Asian buyers have an incentive to receive them.
5. U.S. domestic sensitivity to fuel prices is a recurring constraint on how long Washington sustains a full closure regime.

## 7. Key Drivers Against Normalization

1. Insurance is the binding constraint. Clubs have already cancelled once. Repeated post-ceasefire attacks reset the trust clock. Kpler’s own risk note makes this explicit.
2. The political issue is unresolved. Iran links reopening to lifting the blockade; Washington links relief to Iranian behavior. Alcon judged an October reopening deal unlikely.
3. Iran retains a cheap disruption option: intermittent strikes, mines, and a permit regime. That option is being used.
4. One ceasefire has already failed. Each failure raises the evidence threshold for the next recovery.
5. Second-order theaters are active: Houthis, the Saudi bypass pipeline, and Gulf-state exposure to Iranian retaliation.
6. Dark transit and escorted convoys can move oil without restoring the commercial system this definition requires.

Ranked, insurance confidence and the absence of a durable political bargain dominate naval tactical control.

## 9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon

Synthesis rule: functional normality requires the commercial gates (open transit and insurance) plus a quiet military baseline. Oil recovery is necessary but not sufficient. Probabilities are therefore pulled down from the oil sub-forecast toward the insurance and transit sub-forecasts, with an additional penalty for the already-failed ceasefire.

### 1 week
- Probability: 1%
- Base-rate anchor: 3 percent
- Current-evidence adjustment: down, because strikes and retaliation are current, not residual
- Core rationale: Attacks occurred in the last 10 days. Insurance is still in extreme territory. No political arrangement is in force.
- Main gating factor: active kinetic exchange
- What would raise this forecast materially: an immediate, verified mutual halt and a club statement restoring cover
- What would lower this forecast materially: a mine incident or a strike on a Gulf export terminal
- Confidence in this estimate: High

### 1 month
- Probability: 4%
- Base-rate anchor: 7 percent
- Current-evidence adjustment: down, given Alcon’s judgment that an October deal is unlikely and the fresh attack cycle
- Core rationale: A tactical pause is plausible. Fourteen days of open-lane traffic plus insurance normalization is not. Charterers will not treat a one-week lull as a regime change.
- Main gating factor: insurance and lane confidence
- What would raise this forecast materially: a signed reopening arrangement plus two clean weeks and premium collapse
- What would lower this forecast materially: another tanker strike or expansion of the blockade
- Confidence in this estimate: High

### 3 months
- Probability: 10%
- Base-rate anchor: 15 percent
- Current-evidence adjustment: down, because the June–July failure is the relevant within-case base rate
- Core rationale: By early January a winter political pause is possible. The Red Sea reference class says insurers and liners will still be waiting for a longer clean period. LNG and mine clearance alone can consume much of this window.
- Main gating factor: durability of any pause beyond 60 days
- What would raise this forecast materially: 45 or more days without a shipping attack and P&I restoration
- What would lower this forecast materially: renewed strikes on Gulf-state bases or a second pipeline hit
- Confidence in this estimate: Medium

### 6 months
- Probability: 20%
- Base-rate anchor: 28 percent
- Current-evidence adjustment: down, for unresolved blockade-for-access bargaining and proxy theaters
- Core rationale: Enough time for a limited deal and a partial mine-cleared lane, but not enough to erase two failed or fragile openings unless the military threat is visibly dismantled. Escorted oil flow at high levels remains more likely than the joint definition.
- Main gating factor: political bargain that removes Iran’s incentive to tax the strait
- What would raise this forecast materially: blockade relief tied to a verified shipping halt and club re-entry
- What would lower this forecast materially: a spring escalation cycle similar to September
- Confidence in this estimate: Medium

### 1 year
- Probability: 36%
- Base-rate anchor: 42 percent
- Current-evidence adjustment: modestly down
- Core rationale: A year is long enough for war-weariness, fuel-price politics, and a narrow deal to produce a real commercial reset. It is also long enough for another collapse. The Tanker War and Red Sea cases both say residual premia and routing changes often outlast the headlines.
- Main gating factor: whether a settlement survives its first test
- What would raise this forecast materially: six continuous months of open traffic and premia near baseline
- What would lower this forecast materially: Iranian leadership conclusion that strait pressure is the only remaining leverage
- Confidence in this estimate: Medium

### 18 months
- Probability: 50%
- Base-rate anchor: 55 percent
- Current-evidence adjustment: slightly down
- Core rationale: By April 2028 the modal path is a damaged but functioning corridor, either after a formal arrangement or after both sides accept a managed, lower-threat status quo. The probability stays near even because structural bypass investment, dark-fleet habits, and the option to re-mine do not disappear with a ceasefire. Long-horizon certainty above 80 percent is not justified: the operational bar includes insurance and open lanes, not merely the absence of daily strikes.
- Main gating factor: whether the threat capability is constrained or merely unused
- What would raise this forecast materially: verified drawdown of coastal anti-ship systems and a full club return
- What would lower this forecast materially: a frozen conflict in which Hormuz stays a licensed waterway
- Confidence in this estimate: Low

## 10. Most Likely Path

Through late 2026, intermittent shipping attacks and limited U.S. retaliation continue. Crude moves in bursts via escorted and dark transits, somewhere in a wide band below the pre-war level, while LNG and container traffic stay impaired. Insurance remains the choke point. A fragile political arrangement is more plausible in the first half of 2027 than in October 2026. Even then, functional normality as defined here arrives only after a clean multi-month period, not on the day a memorandum is signed. The modal state at 12–18 months is partial commercial recovery with a residual risk premium, not full normality. The probability that the full definition is met by 18 months is about even.

## 11. Faster Normalization Case

A bargain in which Washington relaxes the port blockade, Iran stops permit enforcement and attacks, and Gulf states finance a mine-clearance and reinsurance bridge. The June transit spike shows the physical response can be fast. If that package held for 60 days, the 6-month probability would move toward 40 percent and the 1-year probability toward 60 percent. This case requires both sides to trade leverage they are currently using.

## 12. Prolonged Instability Case

Iran treats limited strikes and a toll regime as sustainable leverage. The United States keeps the blockade. Houthis and Iraqi militias periodically hit bypass routes. Insurers never fully return. By 18 months the corridor is a managed risk zone at 30–50 percent of former open traffic, with oil moving and normality unmet. Under that case the 18-month probability falls toward 25 percent. Foreign Affairs and Flint descriptions of a widening, no-off-ramp conflict are the skeleton of this case.

## 13. Red-Team Critique

The forecast may understate normalization if dark and facilitated traffic is already doing more commercial work than AIS implies, and if markets will accept escort-based flow as “normal” faster than this definition allows. The September crude recovery is the best evidence for that critique.

The forecast may overstate normalization if the failed June–July opening is the true base rate. Repeated attacks after a ceasefire do more damage to insurer confidence than the initial closure. A licensed strait can persist for years without looking like the Tanker War’s endpoint. The 18-month figure of 50 percent is the softest number in the set and should be revised down if another opening fails in the next quarter.

Load-bearing assumptions: Iran can still be induced to stop attacking shipping; U.S. fuel-price politics eventually cap escalation; mines are a clearance problem measured in months, not a permanent closure; proxies do not independently veto a deal.
