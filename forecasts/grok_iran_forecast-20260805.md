**1. Forecast Question**

Probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War, by horizons of 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months (from 5 August 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality requires simultaneous satisfaction of all of the following observable conditions for a sustained period (not a single-day snapshot):

- Direct military activity: No US/Iran or Iran/Israel kinetic strikes on each other’s territory, bases, or forces in the Gulf region for at least 14 consecutive days; no major IRGC or coalition naval/air operations targeting the other.
- Proxy activity: No significant Houthi, Iraqi Shia militia, or Hezbollah kinetic actions against shipping, energy infrastructure, or US/partner forces attributable to Iranian direction or facilitation.
- Shipping security (Strait of Hormuz and approaches): Zero successful or attempted attacks on commercial vessels for 21 consecutive days; no active mining, no IRGC “permission regime” or mandatory coordination enforced by force; freedom of navigation without unilateral Iranian administrative control or tolls/fees.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits through Hormuz sustained at ≥80% of 2024–early 2025 pre-war average (~100–120+ vessels/day, with oil/LNG share restored proportionally); AIS tracking and Kpler/Windward-type data confirm recovery without dark fleet dominance or systematic avoidance.
- War-risk insurance: Hull war-risk premiums for Hormuz voyages returned to ≤0.5–1% of hull value (pre-war baseline ~0.1–0.3%; wartime peaks 5–10%); coverage widely available without exclusion or 7-day-only terms; Joint War Committee listing no longer treats the area as extreme risk requiring special premiums.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf (ex-Iran) crude + condensate exports ≥90% of pre-war levels via Hormuz or equivalent pipelines; Qatari/UAE/Saudi LNG loadings and sailings near pre-war; Iranian exports (if any) not the sole residual flow under shadow-fleet conditions.
- Energy market stress: Brent volatility (30-day realized) and risk premium normalized such that prices no longer carry a sustained Hormuz-closure premium of >$10–15/bbl relative to non-Gulf fundamentals; refined-product cracks and LNG spot premiums no longer elevated solely due to Gulf disruption.
- Force posture: No major new US carrier/amphibious or Iranian coastal missile/drone reinforcements specifically for Hormuz contest; existing postures stable or reduced without signaling imminent escalation.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No leadership assassinations, major infrastructure hits (Kharg, Ras Laffan, desalination, bridges), or formal blockade reinstatement that restarts the kinetic cycle.

A temporary ceasefire, partial reopening under contested Iranian administration, lower oil prices from demand destruction, or battlefield pause without commercial/insurance recovery does **not** meet the standard.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant classes (with breakdowns):

1. **1980–88 Tanker War (Iran-Iraq)**: Multi-year attacks on shipping in Gulf/Hormuz approaches; hundreds of vessels hit; insurance spikes; traffic never fully stopped but risk elevated. Partial commercial adaptation within months; full de-risking only after ceasefire + years. Suggests multi-year elevated risk; functional commercial normality lagged formal ceasefire by 6–18+ months.
2. **2019–2021 Iran tanker attacks / max-pressure period**: Discrete attacks, seizures, mining scares; insurance rose sharply then partially normalized with escorts and de-escalation. Recovery of confidence: weeks to a few months after last major incident + diplomatic easing. Breaks down because scale was far smaller than full war + blockade.
3. **Houthi Red Sea campaign (2023–ongoing into 2026 context)**: Sustained asymmetric attacks on shipping; traffic collapse on primary route; Cape rerouting; insurance spikes. Partial recovery only with sustained naval presence + reduced attack frequency; full normality not achieved after 2+ years. Relevant for proxy persistence and insurance stickiness.
4. **Suez closures (1956, 1967–75)**: Full physical closure then reopening. Traffic recovered within weeks–months of clearance, but political risk lingered. Breaks down: pure physical vs. contested sovereignty + asymmetric capability.
5. **Post-major Gulf wars / sanctions (1991, 2003, various Iran sanctions)**: Military pauses often preceded commercial recovery by months; insurance and buyer caution lagged. Suggests 3–12 months for insurance/transit normalization after kinetic pause.
6. **Broader chokepoint disruptions (e.g., Black Sea 2022 grain corridor)**: Political deals produce partial flows quickly; full commercial/insurance normality slower and reversible.

**Base-rate prior (pre-current-evidence adjustment)** for meeting the full operational definition:
- 1 week: ~2–5%
- 1 month: ~8–15%
- 3 months: ~20–30%
- 6 months: ~35–45%
- 1 year: ~50–60%
- 18 months: ~60–70%

Anchored on historical lag between kinetic pause and multi-domain commercial/insurance recovery, plus high reversibility of Gulf arrangements.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

1. Mutual economic pain (oil/LNG disruption costs on global markets, Iran sanctions/oil revenue loss, US political pressure on energy prices).
2. Mediation channels (Oman, Qatar, Pakistan) and floating technical compromises on routes.
3. US military superiority and ability to impose costs; Iranian capacity constraints after sustained strikes.
4. Gulf states’ preference for stability and free navigation.
5. Historical pattern that prolonged high-intensity phases eventually produce temporary pauses.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

1. Core sovereignty/control dispute over Hormuz (Iran views as primary remaining leverage; US/GCC insist on free navigation). This is the primary gating factor.
2. High reversibility of prior MOUs; low trust.
3. Iranian domestic politics and IRGC institutional interest in retaining strait role.
4. Asymmetric residual capabilities (drones, missiles, small boats, mines) that allow low-cost harassment even after major strikes.
5. Insurance market stickiness and commercial risk aversion (second-order effect).
6. Proxy networks and secondary fronts (Houthis, etc.).
7. Potential for new triggers (leadership, infrastructure, third-party incidents).

Ranked importance: Control dispute > residual asymmetric capability + insurance lag > trust deficit > others.

Synthesis is not mechanical average. Shipping security + insurance + absence of Iranian administrative control are gating: without them, transit volumes and market confidence cannot fully recover even if strikes pause. Direct kinetic quiet is necessary but insufficient.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Repeated cycles of talks → partial reopening under contested terms → incidents over control/tolls → limited strikes → renewed mediation. Transit recovers partially (40–70%) with elevated insurance for many months. Full definition met only if a technical free-navigation protocol is implemented and holds for weeks without harassment, allowing insurance and commercial confidence to follow. Base case: functional normality delayed into 2027 for most practical purposes.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Rapid, verified comprehensive deal within weeks (route split or pure free navigation, no fees, monitoring acceptable to both), zero incidents for 3–4 weeks, insurer consortium statements cutting rates sharply, Gulf states and major buyers (Asia) immediately resume full loadings. Probability of this path low (~10–15% conditional on near-term deal). Would raise 1–3 month forecasts materially.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Iran successfully monetizes or administers a permission regime for extended periods; residual drone/missile capability allows sporadic attacks; insurance remains prohibitive for Western-linked fleets; shadow/Chinese trade dominates residual flows; proxies stay active. Normality delayed beyond 18 months. Supported by current Iranian statements and historical asymmetric persistence.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

- Overestimating normalization: May underweight Iranian determination to retain Hormuz as core deterrent/leverage after conventional losses; insurance markets are slower than kinetic pauses historically; commercial buyers (especially LNG) are highly risk-averse.
- Underestimating normalization: May underweight US ability to impose escalating costs and Iranian economic desperation; markets have already adapted via pipelines/rerouting, reducing Iranian leverage over time; Trump administration political incentive for visible “win” on energy prices.
- Load-bearing assumptions: That the control dispute is not easily papered over; that insurance and transit volumes are true gating rather than lagging indicators; that no sudden leadership or external shock (China pressure, major domestic Iranian unrest) alters trajectory.
- Evidence that would falsify current view: Sustained 30+ day attack-free period with volumes >80% and premiums <1% under an explicit free-navigation text, or conversely a formal Iranian declaration of permanent administrative control accepted by major Asian buyers.
