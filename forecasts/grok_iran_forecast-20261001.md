I will ground the forecast in current reporting on the 2026 Iran conflict and Hormuz disruption, then build the probability estimates from reference classes and an evidence ledger.The Persian Gulf is not functionally normal on 1 October 2026, and the gap between partial energy flows and commercial normalization is the central fact in this forecast. Oil is moving. The operating system that made Hormuz routine is not.

## 1. Forecast Question

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality after the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months from 1 October 2026.

## 2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”

Normality requires all of the following to hold together for at least 14 consecutive days, with no credible near-term trigger for reversal. A ceasefire, a lower oil price, or a convoy workaround does not qualify.

- Direct military activity. No sustained US or Israeli strikes on Iranian territory, and no Iranian ballistic, cruise-missile, or large drone salvos against US, Israeli, or Gulf-state targets. Isolated harassment below a fatal, campaign threshold can be tolerated. A pause that either side can end at will does not count.
- Proxy activity. No campaign-level Houthi, Iraqi-militia, or Hezbollah attacks on shipping or regional energy infrastructure linked to this war.
- Shipping security. Joint Maritime Information Center or UKMTO threat in the Strait of Hormuz and adjacent waters no longer Severe or Critical. No systematic mine threat in the traffic separation scheme. No enforced Iranian permit, toll, or routing regime. No recurring projectile attacks on commercial ships.
- Commercial transit. AIS-visible commercial transits sustained at or above about 70 percent of the pre-war baseline. That baseline was roughly 85 to 140 vessels a day depending on the counter. The operational bar used here is about 70 vessels a day, not a handful of escorted or dark transits.
- War-risk insurance. Additional war-risk premiums back near pre-crisis levels, under about 0.5 percent of hull value, versus the crisis range of roughly 1 to 5 percent and higher quotes. Standard P&I war cover available without crisis voyage-by-voyage pricing. Joint War Committee treatment narrowed.
- Oil and LNG continuity. Hormuz crude, products, and Qatari LNG at or above about 85 percent of pre-war flows on ordinary commercial terms. Dark-fleet passages, US Navy shuttle convoys, and Gulf of Oman ship-to-ship transfers do not count as normal export continuity.
- Energy-market stress. The structural war premium largely gone. Brent is about $100, roughly 39 percent above the February 2026 baseline near $72. Freight is no longer a dominant share of the free-on-board crude price because of war risk.
- Force posture. US naval blockade of Iranian ports ended. Routine transit no longer depends on a carrier and escort surge. Iranian coastal forces not actively interdicting non-compliant traffic.
- Escalation triggers. No unresolved major trigger — nuclear brinkmanship, a new Gulf-infrastructure campaign, or a regime-survival fight — that markets and insurers treat as imminent.

## 3. Current Situation Snapshot

The war began with US and Israeli strikes on 28 February 2026. This is roughly day 216 of the Hormuz crisis. An interim understanding around 17–18 June collapsed. The 60-day Islamabad memorandum window lapsed on 17 August without a settlement. Fighting flared again in early September. By late September, announced US strikes on Iranian territory had paused for a stretch of days, but the maritime fight had not.

Confirmed or well-corroborated as of 1 October:

- AIS-visible commercial traffic is about 5 percent of normal: roughly 4.6 vessels a day over the past week against about 85 before the crisis. hormuz.now rated the strait Restricted, threat Severe, with ships crossing under US naval coordination while Iran claims control and requires permits.
- JMIC held the threat at Severe on 27 September, citing IRGC UAV overflights, surveillance, and VHF hailing, and continued to warn of mining risk. UKMTO had described traffic about 90 percent below pre-conflict levels earlier in September. CENTCOM has said designated lanes were cleared; authorities have not declared the wider waterway mine-free.
- Energy volumes and ship counts diverge. Kpler and CNN put oil and products through Hormuz near 13.1 million barrels a day in late September, just under 80 percent of a 17.1 million barrel pre-war figure. JPMorgan has described regional crude shipments near 17.5 million barrels a day, about 98 percent of pre-war, while products lagged near 58 percent. Soufan Center notes some shipping observers put mitigation closer to 50 percent. Gulf of Oman ship-to-ship transfers have run several million barrels a day. These are workarounds.
- Brent is about $98 to $100, up roughly 39 percent from the February baseline near $72.
- Diplomacy at the UN General Assembly failed to change the structure. Trump rejected Iran’s seven-day reopening proposal, which was conditioned on lifting the US naval blockade and sanctions relief. Araghchi left New York without a deal. Both sides say talks may continue. Iranian officials say they are ready to resume war. The US blockade of Iranian ports remains in place. Iran’s parliament has moved a navigation article allowing seizure of non-compliant vessels.
- Attacks on commercial ships continued into late September, including a fatal strike on the bulk carrier Cape Dao off Musandam on 23 September, attribution disputed. A reported 14 September cruise-missile strike injuring US Marines was not in the original Defense Department count.

Disputed: exact Hormuz throughput, whether Mojtaba Khamenei is alive and in effective control, and responsibility for some recent ship attacks. Belligerent claims of “control” of the strait are treated as claims, not facts.

## 4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior

The relevant class is not “wars end.” It is “chokepoint commerce returns to unescorted, insurable routine after a state with local anti-ship capability has used that chokepoint as leverage.”

- Tanker War, 1984–1988. Relevant because Iran and Iraq attacked merchant ships for years while oil still moved at a premium. It breaks down because that war was attrition between two regional states, not a US blockade plus nuclear diplomacy. It implies multi-year disruption if the political incentive to attack ships remains, with partial adaptation in months and real normalization only after the August 1988 ceasefire plus a further lag.
- 2019 Gulf attacks and Abqaiq. Relevant as an insurance and routing shock. It breaks down because there was no open war, no blockade, and no mine campaign. Premiums and behavior normalized in weeks to a few months once attacks stopped. That is an upper bound on speed, not a base case.
- Red Sea and Houthi campaign, late 2023 into 2025. Most relevant commercial analogue. Traffic diversion and elevated war-risk pricing persisted for more than a year despite pauses, because the attacker kept capability and a political reason to use it. Full route normality lagged the reduction in threat by many months. Suggests 12 to 24 months if Iranian leverage over Hormuz is unresolved.
- Kuwait liberation and northern Gulf mines, 1991. Hormuz itself stayed open. Mine clearance and insurer comfort in mined waters took months after the February ceasefire. Useful for the clearance lag, not for the politics.
- Black Sea grain corridor, 2022–2023. Naval coordination and special insurance restored partial flows within months. Full commercial normality did not return while the war continued. Direct warning against equating escorted barrels with normality.
- June–August 2026 Islamabad window. Inside case. Oil moved faster than politics. Commitments on waivers, blockade lift, and mine clearance failed before day 60. Traffic never approached baseline. This lowers the weight on the next paper deal.

Base-rate prior before case-specific updating, for this strict definition: 1 week 2 percent; 1 month 6 percent; 3 months 14 percent; 6 months 28 percent; 1 year 42 percent; 18 months 55 percent. The prior already assumes partial flows return long before functional normality.

## 6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization

1. Partial physical recovery already exists. Escorted and dark flows near a large share of pre-war crude reduce the economic cost of waiting and show the route is not physically sealed.
2. Both sides are still talking, with Qatar in the channel. A transactional reopening-for-blockade deal remains available in principle.
3. Iranian economic stress is real. Currency collapse and blocked crude exports create a regime incentive to trade shipping pressure for relief.
4. US political calendar. Midterms in November 2026 raise the cost of a fresh price spike, which can favor a limited deal even without a nuclear settlement.
5. Mine-lane clearance, if it holds, removes one technical barrier. Insurers reprice faster once attacks stop than governments settle underlying disputes.
6. Gulf producers have adapted with bypass pipeline use, ship-to-ship transfers, and naval coordination, which lowers the chance of an indefinite total closure.

## 7. Key Drivers Against Normalization

1. Bargaining deadlock. Iran wants the blockade lifted before nuclear concessions. Washington, believing flows are restored, is raising the nuclear price. Each side thinks it is winning.
2. Iran still has a working coercive theory. Late-September attacks show it can still impose cost in the strait. That incentive lasts as long as the blockade lasts.
3. Insurance and flag behavior lag politics. Even after a deal, premiums, crew refusal, and schedule repair take weeks to months. The June window already demonstrated this.
4. Residual mine and projectile risk. Lane clearance is not a mine-free strait. JMIC still rates the threat Severe.
5. Proxy and horizontal risk. Iranian officials have already pointed at a Red Sea front if strikes resume.
6. Workaround success can delay a settlement. If Washington believes 80 to 95 percent of energy is moving, the urgency to pay Iran’s price falls.
7. Leadership and succession opacity in Tehran raises the chance of hardline vetoes over any compromise.

Ranked: political sequencing and Iranian retention of anti-ship capability dominate. Mines, insurance, and schedules are lags, not the root cause.

## 9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon

Synthesis rule: functional normality is gated by the slowest of transit security, insurance, and blockade and force posture, after a political arrangement that removes Iran’s reason to hit ships. Oil-volume recovery is necessary but not sufficient. A strike pause is not sufficient. Probabilities are cumulative: “has returned by this date.”

### 1 week
- Probability: 1%
- Base-rate anchor: 2 percent. Even short shocks do not clear insurance and routing in seven days.
- Current-evidence adjustment: Down slightly. Severe threat, permit regime, blockade, and failed UNGA round.
- Core rationale: No plausible sequence clears mines perception, premiums, schedules, and the blockade together inside a week.
- Main gating factor: Political terms plus insurer behavior.
- What would raise this forecast materially: An implemented deal with an immediate verified attack halt and blockade lift, which is not in sight.
- What would lower this forecast materially: A new salvo against ships or Gulf infrastructure.
- Confidence in this estimate: High

### 1 month
- Probability: 4%
- Base-rate anchor: 6 percent.
- Current-evidence adjustment: Down. October is more likely to see further merchant attacks than a settlement, on current diplomatic sequencing.
- Core rationale: Talks can resume. Implementation, mine assurance, and war-risk repricing cannot finish inside October even if a text appears late in the month.
- Main gating factor: Blockade-for-access bargain, then the insurance lag.
- What would raise this forecast materially: A signed, front-loaded deal in the first half of October with attacks stopped and the first unrestricted convoy week completed.
- What would lower this forecast materially: A renewed ashore strike cycle or a Gulf-infrastructure attack.
- Confidence in this estimate: High

### 3 months
- Probability: 12%
- Base-rate anchor: 14 percent.
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slightly down. One memorandum has already failed. Nuclear demands have moved back to the center.
- Core rationale: A limited transactional deal is possible by year-end under midterm and Iranian revenue pressure. The historical insurance and scheduling lag means many deals signed inside this window still miss the definition on 1 January.
- Main gating factor: Whether Washington will lift the blockade without a nuclear concession Iran will not give first.
- What would raise this forecast materially: Blockade lifted and JMIC threat cut for several uninterrupted weeks.
- What would lower this forecast materially: Another collapse after a short reopening, or sustained ship attacks through December.
- Confidence in this estimate: Medium

### 6 months
- Probability: 22%
- Base-rate anchor: 28 percent.
- Current-evidence adjustment: Down. Dual victory narratives and the failed summer window justify a lower path than the generic chokepoint prior.
- Core rationale: By April 2027 exhaustion, revenue need, and US political cost make a messy modus vivendi plausible. Mine-survey residue and crew and insurer memory still keep a large share of deals from meeting the full definition.
- Main gating factor: Durable attack halt long enough for premiums to collapse, not the announcement itself.
- What would raise this forecast materially: Sixty attack-free days, premiums under 0.5 percent, AIS traffic above 70 a day, blockade ended.
- What would lower this forecast materially: Iranian decision to ride out the blockade by intermittent ship attacks into spring.
- Confidence in this estimate: Medium

### 1 year
- Probability: 38%
- Base-rate anchor: 42 percent.
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slightly down. Nuclear issue and regime distrust are stickier than a pure maritime dispute.
- Core rationale: Inside a year, either a limited settlement or Iranian degradation of maritime strike capacity is more likely than not to have been attempted. The joint commercial test still fails often because proxies, mines, or a reversible ceasefire keep insurers and liners from treating the route as routine.
- Main gating factor: Settlement quality. A ceasefire that leaves the blockade or the permit regime in place fails the definition.
- What would raise this forecast materially: Nuclear side-understandings that let both sides claim a win, plus verified mine clearance.
- What would lower this forecast materially: Regime-hardening after succession stress, or a second full closure.
- Confidence in this estimate: Low-medium

### 18 months
- Probability: 52%
- Base-rate anchor: 55 percent.
- Current-evidence adjustment: Roughly in line, small downward tick. Long wars with chokepoint leverage often leave a residual risk premium past 18 months, as the Tanker War and Red Sea cases show.
- Core rationale: By April 2028 the modal path is an imperfect political arrangement with commercial routines mostly restored. Just under half the probability mass remains in frozen conflict, episodic interdiction, or a new escalation. This does not exceed 80 percent because the definition still requires unescorted, normally insured transit, not merely barrels moving.
- Main gating factor: Whether Iran retains both capability and a political incentive to tax Hormuz.
- What would raise this forecast materially: A monitored settlement that survives one subsequent crisis without ship attacks.
- What would lower this forecast materially: Collapse of talks into a second sustained closure, or a regional infrastructure war.
- Confidence in this estimate: Low

## 10. Most Likely Path

Through October and November, controlled attrition continues. US escorts and dark or coordinated tankers move a large but unstable share of crude. Products and liner shipping stay impaired. AIS traffic stays a small fraction of baseline. Premiums stay in crisis territory. Diplomacy produces more contacts than terms.

The modal path over two to three quarters is a transactional arrangement: blockade eased, Iranian interdiction reduced, nuclear issues deferred or narrowed. Oil normalizes before containers and before insurance. Functional normality, as defined here, arrives only after a multi-week attack-free period and a premium collapse, more likely in the second half of the one-year window than before it. A clean return to the 2025 operating system inside 18 months is a coin toss, not a base case.

## 11. Faster Normalization Case

Washington accepts blockade relief and limited sanctions relief without a full nuclear settlement. Iran stops ship attacks to lock that gain before its floating barrels run out. JMIC cuts the threat rating. Hull war-risk falls under 1 percent within weeks and toward pre-crisis within a quarter. Gulf states press both sides because their own infrastructure was hit earlier in the war. AIS traffic recovers toward 70 a day by late winter. This path is real but not likely. It requires one side to move first, which both currently refuse to do.

## 12. Prolonged Instability Case

Iran treats intermittent ship attacks as the price of forcing blockade relief. Washington treats restored crude flows as proof the strait can be held without paying that price. A winter strike exchange hits a Gulf facility or a US escort. Insurers pull cover again. Houthis open a Red Sea front. Mine uncertainty keeps the threat Severe into 2027. Crude still moves by convoy and ship-to-ship transfer, so prices oscillate rather than explode, and the political system tolerates abnormality. Under that path, functional normality is still absent at 18 months.

## 13. Red-Team Critique

The forecast may understate normalization speed if official and bank flow estimates are right and insurers follow barrels faster than the Red Sea precedent. A quiet October plus a narrow deal could reprice war risk within a quarter. The 12 percent at three months would then be low.

It may overstate later normalization. The nuclear issue, succession opacity, and Iran’s demonstrated ability to hit ships at low cost look more like the Tanker War than like 2019. If the US blockade remains the regime’s central grievance, 38 percent at one year and 52 percent at 18 months are too high. Workaround success specifically reduces urgency.

Load-bearing assumptions: Iran can still impose meaningful ship risk; Washington will not lift the blockade for free; insurers will not treat escorted dark flows as normal; a paper ceasefire does not equal the definition.

## 14. Critical Signals to Monitor

- JMIC or UKMTO threat dropping below Severe, and the mine warning withdrawn.
- AIS-visible transits sustained above 40 a day, then 70.
- Additional war-risk premiums quoted under 1 percent, then under 0.5 percent, with P&I clubs restoring standard cover.
- Formal end or pause of the US port blockade, and whether Iranian crude actually loads afterward.
- Attack-free days in the strait. Thirty matters. Sixty matters more.
- Any Gulf-state infrastructure strike or Houthi campaign restart.
- Text of any deal: sequencing of blockade relief versus nuclear steps.
- Brent holding below $80 without a demand shock, which would corroborate physical and political easing.
- Iranian parliamentary enforcement of seizure and toll rules.
