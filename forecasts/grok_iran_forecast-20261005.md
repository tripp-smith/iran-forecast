I will ground the forecast in current reporting on the 2026 Iran War and Hormuz conditions, then build the probabilities from reference classes outward.The Gulf is not near functional normality on 5 October 2026. Oil is moving again under extraordinary risk; the maritime system is not.

## 1. Forecast Question

What is the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality, as defined below, by each horizon from 5 October 2026: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months?

## 2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”

Normality requires all of the following, sustained for at least 14 consecutive days before the horizon date. A ceasefire, a lower oil price, or a pause in high-intensity bombardment is not sufficient.

- Direct military activity: no state-on-state strikes among Iran, the United States, Israel, and Gulf states in that window, and no active declared strike campaign.
- Proxy activity: no multi-day campaign by Houthis, Iraqi militias, or other Iran-linked groups against Gulf shipping, ports, pipelines, or energy facilities. Isolated incidents at or below the 2023–early 2025 baseline do not by themselves fail the test.
- Shipping security: Strait of Hormuz and nearby Gulf of Oman lanes open to commercial traffic without Iranian transit permits, tolls, or selective denial, and without mines as an active operational hazard. US or coalition convoy escort is not the default mode.
- Commercial transit: AIS-visible or otherwise confirmed commercial crossings at or above 70 percent of the pre-crisis baseline of roughly 85–130 ships a day, including a broad return of container lines and product tankers, not only risk-tolerant crude tankers.
- War-risk insurance: additional war-risk premium at or below about 1 percent of hull value (roughly four times the pre-war 0.25 percent), with cover widely available from the commercial market without a government backstop as the main mechanism.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf crude, products, and LNG moving at or above 85 percent of pre-war volumes on ordinary seaborne routes. Pipeline bypass and ship-to-ship transfers may supplement flows; they cannot be the system’s backbone.
- Energy-market stress: the wartime risk premium and extreme freight or insurance pass-through have faded. A price level alone does not qualify.
- Force posture: US and partner naval and air posture reduced from wartime surge; Iranian forces not exercising de facto closure authority.
- Escalation triggers: no fresh mine campaign, declared re-closure, major attack on Gulf-state territory, or new front that re-prices the route.

## 3. Current Situation Snapshot

The war began with US-Israeli strikes on 28 February 2026. This is about day 220 of the Hormuz crisis. A June memorandum of understanding produced a short traffic and insurance improvement, then collapsed. Early October reporting describes talks as stalled, US strikes paused but threatened, and an economic blockade of Iranian oil still in force.

Confirmed or well-corroborated:

- hormuz.now, using AIS and IMF PortWatch, rates the strait Restricted on 5 October: about 7.9 AIS-visible commercial ships a day over the past week versus 84.8 before the crisis, roughly 9 percent. Iran claims permit authority; ships that move do so under US naval coordination.
- Oil volumes have recovered more than ship counts. Kpler and related reporting put September Middle East crude exports near 16 million barrels a day, with roughly 10 million through Hormuz and several million via bypass, against about 17–20 million barrels a day pre-war through the strait. Dark AIS means barrel counts and ship counts diverge. The New York Times reported on 4 October that Iran is still attacking ships in the strait practically every day, while more tankers accept the risk.
- War-risk pricing remains crisis-level. Pre-war additional premiums were about 0.125–0.25 percent of hull. Peak quotes reached 3–10 percent. A June ceasefire cut some quotes toward 2 percent; that did not hold. Lloyd’s List and UKMTO reporting in late September and early October still show tanker strikes, including a Kuwait Oil Tanker Company vessel and an unidentified projectile strike reported on 4 October. Marine insurers have reported on the order of $2 billion in war-risk losses.
- Major container lines have not returned. Product tankers are more deterred than crude tankers because freight does not cover premiums.
- A second front is active. From July, and sharply in September, the Houthis resumed attacks on Saudi-linked shipping and energy sites, seized ground on the Red Sea coast including Mokha, and put pressure on Bab el-Mandeb. Iraqi militia attacks took the Saudi East-West pipeline offline in September. That bypass had been a main shock absorber.

Disputed: exact daily oil flow (US official figures have run hotter than tracker estimates), whether mines remain an active rather than residual hazard, and each side’s account of who fires first. Belligerent claims of control or openness are treated as positions, not facts.

## 4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior

1. Tanker War, 1984–88. Relevant because it is the last sustained Iranian mining and small-craft campaign against Gulf shipping under US convoy. It breaks down because traffic mostly continued, oil volumes stayed broadly intact, and the political end came with the August 1988 ceasefire. Insurance normalized over the following months into 1989–90, then was reset by the 1990 Kuwait crisis. Partial function can coexist with attacks; full commercial normality lagged the ceasefire by months.

2. Red Sea and Bab el-Mandeb, late 2023–2025. Relevant because a capable non-state actor diverted major container and some energy traffic for more than a year despite intermittent pauses and Western strikes. It breaks down because Hormuz is a harder geographic and energy chokepoint and a state actor is the principal threat. Suggested timeline: 12–24 months after the threat is credibly reduced, not after the first pause.

3. 2019 Gulf tanker and Abqaiq attacks. Relevant as an insurance and routing shock without war. Traffic largely continued; premia eased within weeks to a couple of months once the acute threat receded. Too mild for this case. Suggests the floor for a contained incident, not a war.

4. Black Sea grain corridor, 2022–23. Relevant as a negotiated, reversible maritime corridor inside an ongoing war. Flows resumed in weeks under a deal and collapsed when the deal died. Insurance and routing never returned to peacetime while the war continued. Suggests fragile partial function within 1–3 months of a deal, and reversal risk for as long as the war lasts.

5. 1991 Gulf War shipping shock. Relevant because Hormuz itself stayed open and the oil-price spike was short. It breaks down because Iraq could not interdict the strait and Kuwaiti production, not the lane, was the multi-year loss. Useful as an upper bound on how fast prices can normalize if the lane is not the contested object.

6. Suez closure, 1967–75. Relevant only as the extreme case of a closed chokepoint without a political settlement. Eight years is the wrong central estimate here, but it warns against assuming geography reopens on a military timetable.

Outside-in prior, before this week’s evidence, for full functional normality after a still-unsettled chokepoint war: 1 week 3 percent; 1 month 6 percent; 3 months 14 percent; 6 months 25 percent; 1 year 42 percent; 18 months 55 percent. The prior assumes settlement odds rise with time and that commercial normalization lags a deal by one to three months.

## 6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization

1. Oil is already moving at a large fraction of pre-war volume, so the physical system is not at zero and can scale if risk falls.
2. Both sides have accepted, in practice, that a pure military decision is unlikely. That raises the chance of another corridor deal.
3. Gulf producers and Asian buyers have a strong incentive to pay for risk reduction. Freight has been high enough to pull some crude tonnage back in.
4. US escort has shown that Iran cannot cheaply keep the strait at zero.
5. Insurers reprice within days of a credible quiet period, as June showed.

## 7. Key Drivers Against Normalization

1. No durable political settlement. The June framework already failed once.
2. Iran still treats Hormuz access as leverage and is still striking ships.
3. Insurance, not naval permission, is the binding commercial constraint, and losses have been large.
4. The Houthi and Iraqi-militia front threatens the bypass system that made partial oil recovery possible.
5. Container and product markets have rerouted via the Cape. Reversing that requires months of quiet, not a headline.
6. Mines, permit claims, and dark-fleet practices slow any return to ordinary lane use.
7. US blockade of Iranian exports and Iranian demands on sanctions and Yemen are linked. Solving one without the other is unstable.

Ranked: settlement failure and continued ship attacks are the primary blockers; insurance and the Yemen front are the main propagators into commercial non-normality.

Transit, insurance, and proxy restraint are the gating set. Oil recovery can lead them. Strike reduction can lead them further. Normality does not hold unless the gating set holds together.

## 9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon

Synthesis rule: the forecast is close to the joint probability of the gating set, with a small upward adjustment because success on transit, insurance, and proxies is correlated through a political deal. It is not an average of the sub-forecasts. Oil recovery is treated as necessary but not sufficient.

### 1 week
- Probability: 2%
- Base-rate anchor: 3 percent, from the outside prior for an unsettled chokepoint war.
- Current-evidence adjustment: down 1 point. AIS traffic near 9 percent, strikes this week, and talks at an impasse make a 14-day clean window almost unavailable inside seven days.
- Core rationale: even a sudden deal cannot clear mines, reopen insurance at scale, and bring liner operators back inside a week.
- Main gating factor: insurance and operator behavior.
- What would raise this forecast materially: an immediate, verified mutual halt plus a published demining and open-transit protocol already being executed.
- What would lower this forecast materially: a further declared closure or a strike on a Gulf-state port or energy site.
- Confidence in this estimate: High

### 1 month
- Probability: 5%
- Base-rate anchor: 6 percent.
- Current-evidence adjustment: down 1 point. June showed a deal can move traffic within days, but also that it can break. Yemen is an active second constraint.
- Core rationale: a corridor arrangement could lift crude flows further. It would not meet the 70 percent broad-transit and insurance tests for 14 straight days by early November unless attacks stop immediately and stay stopped.
- Main gating factor: continued ship attacks and absence of a deal.
- What would raise this forecast materially: a signed transit protocol, seven or more quiet days, and quoted premiums falling through 2 percent toward 1 percent.
- What would lower this forecast materially: another failed round plus a Houthi or Iraqi strike that keeps the East-West pipeline down.
- Confidence in this estimate: High

### 3 months
- Probability: 12%
- Base-rate anchor: 14 percent.
- Current-evidence adjustment: down 2 points. Seven months of failed or fragile pauses, and a widening Yemen front, outweigh the partial oil recovery.
- Core rationale: this is the first horizon at which a serious deal could be followed by demining, a premium cut, and a partial liner return. The base case is still a managed, attacked, or selectively permitted lane.
- Main gating factor: political settlement durable enough for underwriters.
- What would raise this forecast materially: 30 continuous days without a transit attack and premiums sustained under 1.5 percent.
- What would lower this forecast materially: renewed high-intensity strikes or Bab el-Mandeb effectively risk-priced like Hormuz.
- Confidence in this estimate: Medium

### 6 months
- Probability: 21%
- Base-rate anchor: 25 percent.
- Current-evidence adjustment: down 4 points. The second front and the sanctions-for-strait linkage make a clean six-month path less likely than the generic unsettled-war prior.
- Core rationale: by April 2027, fatigue, Gulf mediation, and the cost of the Iranian oil blockade could produce a wider arrangement. Insurance and container networks would still lag. A little over one in five paths clears the full definition.
- Main gating factor: Yemen and militia restraint tied to any US-Iran deal.
- What would raise this forecast materially: a deal that explicitly covers Hormuz, Bab el-Mandeb, and pipeline security, followed by six quiet weeks.
- What would lower this forecast materially: collapse of Saudi Red Sea exports or a new mine campaign.
- Confidence in this estimate: Medium

### 1 year
- Probability: 35%
- Base-rate anchor: 42 percent.
- Current-evidence adjustment: down 7 points. The war has already lasted seven months without a stable end state, and Iran has retained denial capability. That is closer to the Red Sea reference class than to 1991.
- Core rationale: a year is long enough for a settlement, demining, and commercial re-entry if the political war ends. It is not long enough to treat that end as likely. Residual force posture and episodic proxy fire can still fail the definition even if oil looks normal.
- Main gating factor: whether Iranian denial capability is politically traded away or only paused.
- What would raise this forecast materially: verified lane clearance, liner return, and premiums near pre-war multiples for a full quarter.
- What would lower this forecast materially: regime or succession crisis in Tehran that increases, rather than reduces, IRGC control of the strait.
- Confidence in this estimate: Low-medium

### 18 months
- Probability: 48%
- Base-rate anchor: 55 percent.
- Current-evidence adjustment: down 7 points. Long-horizon settlement odds are real, but the operational bar stays high, and the Red Sea case shows diversion can outlast several diplomatic cycles.
- Core rationale: just under half. The central path is a messy political arrangement sometime in 2027, with commercial normality arriving later and incompletely. Paths that never settle, or that settle and relapse, still account for about half the distribution. The figure stays below 80 percent because mines, proxies, insurer memory, and force posture can fail the definition even after a ceasefire.
- Main gating factor: durability of whatever arrangement ends the maritime campaign.
- What would raise this forecast materially: a year of attack-free transit and insurance inside the defined band by spring 2027, which would pull the 18-month number up sharply.
- What would lower this forecast materially: entrenchment of a permit-or-toll regime, or a second sustained closure episode.
- Confidence in this estimate: Low

## 10. Most Likely Path

Through early 2027 the lane stays restricted and contested. Crude continues to move on risk-tolerant tonnage, dark AIS, escorts, and partial bypass, with volumes fluctuating between roughly half and four-fifths of normal. Container and much product trade stay on the Cape route. Insurance remains several times peacetime. Talks recur and break. Yemen remains a parallel risk. A more stable arrangement is more plausible in the second half of 2027 than in the next quarter, and commercial normality, if it comes, arrives one to three months after attacks actually stop, not when a communiqué is issued.

## 11. Faster Normalization Case

A US-Iran arrangement in the next one to two months trades sanctions relief and a Yemen de-escalation for verified open transit and joint or third-party demining. June showed premiums can halve within days. If that quiet lasts 45 days, crude and products can normalize inside a quarter and liners can begin to return inside six months. This case puts six-month normality near 40 percent and 18-month normality near 70 percent. It requires Iranian leadership to spend Hormuz leverage and the Houthis to accept a pause. That combination has not held once already.

## 12. Prolonged Instability Case

Talks stay deadlocked, ship attacks continue at a low but priced level, and the Red Sea bypass remains impaired. Insurers keep the region in a high-risk area. Operators treat the Cape as semi-permanent for containers. Oil flows persist but never meet the ordinary-route test. Under that case, 18-month normality is closer to 20–25 percent. The 1987–88 and 2023–25 reference classes both support this if the actor with denial capability is not politically constrained.

## 13. Red-Team Critique

The forecast may understate normalization if dark-fleet and escorted crude flows are a leading indicator rather than a substitute, and if Asian buyers force a commercial return faster than underwriters want. It may overstate it if the definition is treated as too close to “oil is moving.” The 48 percent at 18 months is the soft number: settlement odds are not directly observed, and a permit regime with high volumes could look like recovery in market data while failing the definition. The 2 and 5 percent near-term numbers are the hard numbers.

Load-bearing assumptions: Iran keeps denial capability; insurers will not normalize while attacks continue; a Hormuz deal does not automatically restrain the Houthis; 14 days of broad commercial function is the right bar.

What would prove this view wrong: either a verified open lane, sub-1 percent premiums, and liner return inside 90 days, or a fresh full closure that drives even the 18-month figure below 30 percent.

## 14. Critical Signals to Monitor

- AIS and PortWatch daily crossings, split by crude, products, and containers, not barrel headlines alone.
- UKMTO and Lloyd’s List incident log, and whether attribution stabilizes.
- Quoted additional war-risk premium and whether the US or Saudi backstop is still required.
- Status of the East-West pipeline and Yanbu loadings.
- Houthi actions at Bab el-Mandeb and any Iraqi militia fire on Gulf energy assets.
- A written transit protocol that drops Iranian permit authority.
- Announced demining and independent confirmation that lanes are clear.
- Return of a top-five container line to a published Hormuz rotation.
