**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each of these horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality requires simultaneous satisfaction of all the following observable conditions (a temporary ceasefire, lower oil prices, or battlefield pause alone is insufficient):

- Direct military activity: No sustained US/Iran or Israel/Iran strikes on military or dual-use targets in/around the Gulf or Strait of Hormuz; residual isolated incidents do not exceed pre-war baseline rates.
- Proxy activity: No significant activation or sustained attacks by Iranian-aligned groups (Houthis, Iraqi militias, Hezbollah) targeting Gulf energy infrastructure, shipping, or US/partner forces beyond pre-war levels.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Free, unhindered commercial transit without Iranian “permits,” exclusion zones, minelaying, or systematic interdiction; no US naval blockade of Iranian ports; mines cleared from primary Traffic Separation Scheme lanes.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits through Hormuz sustained at ≥70–80% of pre-war average (~100–130+ vessels/day, depending on source baseline), including meaningful tanker and LNG volumes, for at least 2–4 consecutive weeks.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums for Hormuz/Gulf transit sustained at ≤2–3× pre-war levels (pre-war ~0.1–0.25% of hull value); Joint War Committee or equivalent listed-area status materially relaxed or removed; capacity readily available without voyage-by-voyage extreme scrutiny.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf oil exports (Saudi, UAE, Kuwait, Iraq, residual Iranian under sanctions) and Qatar LNG exports restored to ≥80–90% of pre-war throughput levels on a sustained basis, with force-majeure declarations largely lifted and backlog cleared.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent crude volatility and risk premium normalized such that prices and spreads no longer embed a large, persistent Hormuz-disruption premium relative to fundamentals; no repeated multi-day spikes driven by shipping attacks.
- Force posture of major actors: Material drawdown or de-escalation of US force posture from peak wartime levels in the Gulf; Iranian coastal anti-shipping and minelaying posture reduced from wartime alert; no active “mowing the lawn” campaign of repeated strikes.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No credible new casus belli (major loss of life on US/partner vessels or bases, successful mass minelaying, Iranian nuclear reconstitution trigger, or equivalent) that would restart sustained kinetic operations.

All conditions must hold concurrently for the horizon in question. Partial recovery on one or two dimensions does not count.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant reference classes (3–6):

1. **1980s Iran-Iraq Tanker War (1984–1988 peak)**: Sustained attacks on shipping in Gulf/Hormuz; hundreds of vessels hit; US reflagging/escort operations (Earnest Will/Praying Mantis). Traffic never fully stopped but was heavily disrupted and risky for years. Full commercial confidence and insurance normalization lagged the 1988 ceasefire by months to a year+. Relevant for prolonged asymmetric maritime interdiction. Breaks down because neither side had modern precision strike, nuclear overlay, or globalized insurance/supply chains of 2026; US was less directly a co-belligerent early on. Suggests multi-year elevated risk even after formal ceasefire.

2. **2019 Gulf tanker attacks / limpet-mine incidents + 2021–2025 Houthi Red Sea campaign**: Discrete attacks and seizures raised insurance and deterred some traffic; recovery after de-escalation took weeks to months for traffic, longer for premiums. Relevant for insurance and commercial behavior. Breaks down because scale was far smaller and no full-scale state war.

3. **1990–91 Gulf War oil disruption + post-ceasefire shipping recovery**: Rapid military resolution; Kuwaiti production recovery took 1–2 years for full capacity, but Hormuz itself was not the primary choke for extended periods. Suggests faster physical recovery when decisive military outcome and political settlement align. Breaks down because 2026 has no clear decisive victory and Hormuz is the explicit leverage point.

4. **Post-2022 Black Sea / Ukraine grain corridor and Red Sea (Houthi) disruptions**: Commercial traffic and insurance can partially adapt around elevated risk for many months; full normalization requires credible deterrence or settlement and often lags by 6–18 months. Relevant for second-order commercial behavior.

5. **Broader post-major Middle East wars (2003 Iraq, etc.)**: Force posture and residual proxy risk remain elevated for years; commercial energy recovery is faster when infrastructure is intact and political settlement exists.

Base-rate prior (before case-specific evidence), approximate cumulative probability of reaching the operational definition:

- 1 week: ~1–3%
- 1 month: ~5–10%
- 3 months: ~15–25%
- 6 months: ~30–45%
- 1 year: ~50–65%
- 18 months: ~65–80%

These reflect historical pattern that major Gulf maritime disruptions normalize slowly; formal ceasefires do not instantly restore commercial confidence or insurance.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

Ranked by importance: (1) Durable US-Iran political settlement that explicitly addresses Hormuz administration and ends reciprocal targeting; (2) Credible, sustained safe transit (mine-free, no interdiction) producing insurance and owner confidence; (3) Exhaustion of Iranian kinetic capacity or political will to sustain maritime leverage; (4) US force posture shift that reduces Iranian incentive for escalation while providing security guarantees to shippers; (5) Gulf states’ political pressure for economic recovery.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

Ranked by importance: (1) Iranian strategic reliance on Hormuz control as residual leverage after military losses; (2) Repeated failure of interim deals and “mowing the lawn” dynamic; (3) Insurance and commercial risk aversion that lags kinetic pauses by months; (4) Residual mines, exclusion-zone claims, and proxy residual capacity; (5) Domestic Iranian politics and succession dynamics post-Khamenei that reward defiance; (6) US domestic/political constraints on sustained high-intensity operations or full settlement.

**9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon**

**1 Week**  
- Probability: 2%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~1–3%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight downward (active strikes and exclusion-zone announcement)  
- Core rationale: Active kinetic exchanges, depressed traffic, elevated premiums, and exclusion-zone threat make simultaneous multi-domain recovery impossible in days.  
- Main gating factor: Immediate cessation of all strikes and credible safe-passage signal.  
- What would raise this forecast materially: Verified comprehensive ceasefire + immediate traffic surge + insurance rate collapse.  
- What would lower this forecast materially: Successful large attack on major tanker or US vessel.  
- Confidence in this estimate: High  

**1 Month**  
- Probability: 8%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~5–10%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight downward  
- Core rationale: Even a new MOU would face the same credibility deficit as prior ones; commercial and insurance confidence cannot reset in weeks while residual mines, posture, and Iranian leverage incentives persist.  
- Main gating factor: Sustained safe transit producing measurable traffic and premium response.  
- What would raise: Durable settlement text + 2–3 weeks clean transits + visible mine clearance + insurance market reaction.  
- What would lower: Renewed tanker strikes or expanded exclusion zone enforcement.  
- Confidence: Medium-High  

**3 Months**  
- Probability: 22%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~15–25%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Neutral to slight downward  
- Core rationale: Reference classes show partial kinetic pauses possible, but commercial normalization (traffic + insurance + exports) historically lags and requires durable political settlement that has so far proven elusive.  
- Main gating factor: Insurance and owner confidence after demonstrated safe passage.  
- What would raise: Verified corridor agreement with Oman/Gulf states + sustained ≥50% traffic for weeks + premium compression.  
- What would lower: Continued “mowing the lawn” or successful Iranian interdiction campaign.  
- Confidence: Medium  

**6 Months**  
- Probability: 38%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~30–45%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight downward  
- Core rationale: Time allows for exhaustion dynamics or negotiated corridor, but Iranian strategic incentive to retain leverage and insurance lag remain load-bearing. Partial recovery more likely than full operational definition.  
- Main gating factor: Political settlement durability + commercial risk appetite.  
- What would raise: Clear US-Iran framework that freezes Hormuz contest + visible force posture shift.  
- What would lower: New escalation trigger or prolonged low-intensity attrition.  
- Confidence: Medium  

**1 Year**  
- Probability: 55%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~50–65%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Neutral  
- Core rationale: Longer horizon increases probability of settlement or effective deterrence that restores commercial flows; still constrained by residual distrust and possible proxy residual risk. Full multi-domain normality remains demanding.  
- Main gating factor: Sustained commercial confidence after political deal or decisive military shift.  
- What would raise: Comprehensive settlement including nuclear and regional issues + insurance delisting.  
- What would lower: Frozen conflict with periodic flare-ups.  
- Confidence: Medium  

**18 Months**  
- Probability: 68%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~65–80%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight downward  
- Core rationale: Most historical Gulf disruptions eventually see commercial recovery within this window once kinetic intensity falls, but the operational definition is strict and residual Iranian leverage incentives + insurance memory create a longer tail. Overconfidence avoided because settlement is not automatic.  
- Main gating factor: Whether a new equilibrium (settlement or effective US/Gulf deterrence) hardens.  
- What would raise: Clear post-war regional security architecture.  
- What would lower: Protracted frozen conflict or major new trigger (e.g., nuclear reconstitution).  
- Confidence: Medium-Low (long-horizon uncertainty)  

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued low-to-medium intensity contest over Hormuz for several more months, with intermittent talks, partial corridor arrangements that fail or remain contested, depressed but non-zero traffic, elevated insurance, and gradual (not sudden) recovery of oil/LNG volumes as shippers adapt around residual risk. Full operational normality most probable only after a more durable political settlement or effective exhaustion of Iranian maritime coercion capacity, likely in the 9–18 month range.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

A durable US-Iran framework (perhaps mediated) that freezes reciprocal targeting, establishes a jointly monitored or Oman-facilitated corridor, produces weeks of incident-free high-volume transit, triggers rapid insurance compression, and allows force posture de-escalation. Probability of this path producing normality by 3–6 months is low but non-zero if Iranian economic pain and US domestic incentives align.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Iran treats Hormuz control as non-negotiable residual leverage; periodic strikes and exclusion claims continue; insurance and owners treat risk as structural; Gulf states hedge; US maintains blockade and episodic strikes. Full operational normality delayed beyond 18 months or achieved only in degraded form.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Strongest case for higher probabilities: Markets and shippers have adapted faster in prior disruptions than pure historical base rates suggest; Iranian capacity is degraded; economic pain on both sides creates stronger settlement incentives than in the 1980s. Strongest case for lower probabilities: The operational definition is deliberately strict; prior MOUs failed quickly; commercial confidence is sticky downward; Iranian domestic politics post-leadership change may reward defiance. Load-bearing assumptions: that commercial/insurance recovery requires more than kinetic pauses, and that Iranian Hormuz leverage remains strategically valuable. Forecast may under-estimate speed of adaptation if a credible corridor is enforced; may over-estimate long-horizon recovery if a frozen conflict becomes the equilibrium.
