**1. Forecast Question**  
Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each of these horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
All of the following must hold concurrently for a continuous period of at least 14 days (to exclude brief pauses):

- Direct military activity: No kinetic strikes by Iran, the United States, or Israel against each other’s forces, bases, or critical energy infrastructure inside the Gulf region or on Iranian territory that materially affect Gulf operations.
- Proxy activity: No sustained or successful attacks by Iranian-backed groups (Iraqi militias, Houthis, etc.) on commercial shipping, Saudi/UAE energy infrastructure, or US forces that disrupt operations.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: No successful attacks on commercial vessels; no active Iranian “toll” or unilateral route controls that force diversion; southern (Omani) corridor and Traffic Separation Scheme usable without IRGC interdiction.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily two-way transits through Hormuz averaging ≥70% of the pre-war baseline (~90–100+ vessels/day, versus current ~10–15).
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums for a typical VLCC transit ≤1.5× pre-war baseline (pre-war ~0.1–0.25% of hull value; current 3–10% / 8×+ multiples) and cover widely available without widespread club withdrawals.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Aggregate Gulf oil + LNG exports (Saudi, UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Iraq, residual Iranian) restored to ≥85% of pre-war volumes via Hormuz or alternative routes without force-majeure declarations.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent 30-day realized volatility and risk premium consistent with pre-war norms (no sustained war premium >$10–15/bbl attributable to Hormuz risk); no repeated multi-day spikes >15% from shipping disruptions.
- Force posture of major actors: No major new US force surges or Iranian mobilization indicators beyond residual levels; no active naval blockades of commercial traffic.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new nuclear threshold crossings, leadership strikes, or third-party interventions that restart major combat.

Temporary ceasefires, lower oil prices alone, or battlefield pauses do **not** satisfy the definition.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot (as of 29–30 July 2026)**  
War began 28 February 2026 with US-Israeli strikes. An Islamabad MOU was signed 17 June aiming at a 60-day negotiation window and Hormuz reopening. Partial recovery occurred (transits rose toward 40–50+/day briefly; premiums fell from peaks of 5–10% to ~2%).  

MOU effectively collapsed after 7–8 July Iranian attacks on commercial vessels. US resumed intensive strikes (dozens to hundreds of targets over multiple nights), reimposed blockade elements on Iranian ports, and conducted joint US-Saudi strikes on Iraqi proxies. Iran rejected Omani-backed joint management proposals for Hormuz, asserted unilateral control/tolls, and continued intermittent missile/drone attacks (including on US forces in Jordan 28 July). Proxies (Iraqi militias, Houthis) remain active against Saudi infrastructure and Red Sea shipping.  

Hormuz traffic remains severely depressed: recent daily AIS/IMF PortWatch counts ~9–16 vessels (mostly Iranian/Chinese-linked northern corridor), ~10–15% of pre-war ~130–140 or the ~88 baseline used by some trackers; 7-day averages ~11. War-risk premiums elevated (reports of 3–10% of hull value, multiples of 8× peacetime, VLCC costs up to $2–2.5M). Brent ~$88–92 with high volatility. Iranian oil continues via shadow fleet to China at reduced rates; Gulf state exports constrained. Talks continue via Oman/Qatar/Pakistan mediators but with hardline Iranian insistence on Hormuz leverage. US posture remains strike-capable with periodic pauses.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant historical cases:

1. **Iran-Iraq Tanker War (1984–1988)**: Hundreds of attacks on shipping; traffic reduced but never fully stopped; US reflagging/Operation Earnest Will and Praying Mantis (1988) restored safer transit within months of peak intensity, but residual risk and elevated insurance persisted until the 1988 ceasefire. Functional commercial normalization lagged major combat reduction by 3–12 months. Breaks down: lower precision weapons, no full blockade attempt, different global energy market.
2. **1990–91 Gulf War (Kuwait invasion + Desert Storm)**: Rapid coalition victory; oil infrastructure recovery and Hormuz transit normalization within 1–3 months of ceasefire for most commercial flows, though Kuwait production took longer. Breaks down: clear conventional defeat of one side, no ongoing Iranian-style asymmetric control claims.
3. **Houthi Red Sea campaign (2023–2025/26)**: Sustained proxy attacks sharply reduced traffic; insurance spiked; partial recovery only after intensive US/UK strikes + diplomatic pressure, still incomplete years later with elevated premiums and diversions. Highly relevant for proxy persistence and insurance stickiness.
4. **Brief 2019 Gulf tanker attacks / Iranian seizures**: Temporary spikes; traffic and insurance normalized in weeks to a few months once kinetic activity stopped. Breaks down: far smaller scale.
5. **Post-2011 Libya / other chokepoint disruptions**: Commercial recovery often 1–6 months after kinetic phase ends, longer for insurance and full volumes if political residual risk remains.
6. **Suez Canal closures (1956, 1967–75)**: Full closures produced multi-month to multi-year diversions; reopening required political settlement.

**Base-rate prior (before case-specific evidence)**:  
- 1 week: ~2–5%  
- 1 month: ~10–15%  
- 3 months: ~25–35%  
- 6 months: ~40–55%  
- 1 year: ~55–70%  
- 18 months: ~65–80%  

These reflect that military pauses occur faster than commercial/insurance normalization, and asymmetric chokepoint contests (especially with regime survival stakes) tend to produce prolonged residual risk.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Mutual interest in avoiding prolonged global energy shock and domestic economic costs (US political calendar, Iranian regime survival).  
2. Degraded Iranian strike and maritime capabilities from sustained US campaigns.  
3. Gulf state pressure for de-escalation and alternative export routes (pipelines).  
4. Open mediation channels and prior partial MOU success.  
5. Market adaptation (shadow fleet, Red Sea/East-West pipelines, SPR releases).

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Iranian hardline view of Hormuz control as core residual leverage after leadership and nuclear losses.  
2. Proxy autonomy and utility for plausible deniability.  
3. Insurance market stickiness and shipowner risk aversion (require sustained clean record).  
4. Cycle of tit-for-tat that has already broken one MOU.  
5. US domestic/political incentives for continued pressure; Israeli residual concerns.  
6. Difficulty verifying and enforcing any “safe passage” deal given IRGC operational culture.

**9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon**

**1 Week**  
- Probability: 3%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~3%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight downward (active kinetic exchanges and depressed traffic as of 29–30 Jul)  
- Core rationale: No plausible path to simultaneous multi-domain normalization in days. Even a sudden deal would leave insurance and traffic lagging.  
- Main gating factor: Ongoing direct and proxy attacks + current traffic/insurance levels.  
- What would raise: Immediate verified bilateral cessation + clear Iranian public abandonment of unilateral controls.  
- What would lower: Further successful ship attacks or major US response.  
- Confidence: High  

**1 Month**  
- Probability: 12%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~12%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Neutral to slight down (MOU fragility demonstrated)  
- Core rationale: Possible kinetic pause, but commercial transit, insurance, and proxy restraint rarely normalize this fast after repeated breakdowns.  
- Main gating factor: Insurance market and shipowner confidence requiring clean record.  
- What would raise: Durable, verifiable safe-passage agreement with third-party monitoring and rapid traffic rebound.  
- What would lower: Another collapse of talks or major proxy escalation.  
- Confidence: Medium-High  

**3 Months**  
- Probability: 28%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~30%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight down (Iranian insistence on control; recent rejection of joint management)  
- Core rationale: Time for degraded capabilities + economic pressure to produce a more durable arrangement, but residual proxy risk and insurance lag remain material.  
- Main gating factor: Iranian willingness to trade Hormuz leverage for broader relief.  
- What would raise: Clear Iranian operational restraint + traffic sustained above 60–70% for weeks.  
- What would lower: New leadership hardline consolidation or major successful attack.  
- Confidence: Medium  

**6 Months**  
- Probability: 48%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~45–50%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Neutral  
- Core rationale: Historical chokepoint and Tanker War patterns suggest meaningful stabilization becomes more likely once kinetic intensity falls and markets adapt; still not a majority given regime incentives.  
- Main gating factor: Combination of transit security + insurance normalization.  
- What would raise: Sustained low incident rate + formal multilateral navigation regime.  
- What would lower: Prolonged low-level proxy campaign or renewed US-Iran direct exchanges.  
- Confidence: Medium  

**1 Year**  
- Probability: 65%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~60–70%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Neutral  
- Core rationale: Cumulative pressure, capability attrition, and adaptation make functional commercial normality the more likely state, though not guaranteed if Iran treats Hormuz as permanent bargaining chip.  
- Main gating factor: Absence of new major triggers and insurance market reset.  
- What would raise: Comprehensive political settlement covering nuclear and regional issues.  
- What would lower: Regime radicalization or major external shock (e.g., Israeli independent action).  
- Confidence: Medium  

**18 Months**  
- Probability: 75%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~70–80%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Neutral to slight down (long residual risk in asymmetric contests)  
- Core rationale: Most historical reference classes show functional commercial recovery within this window once major combat ends; full pre-war risk pricing may still lag. Definition remains demanding, so probability is not higher.  
- Main gating factor: Structural resolution of Iranian leverage claims vs. Gulf state/US tolerance.  
- What would raise: Verified long-term demilitarization of the strait approaches.  
- What would lower: Entrenchment of Iranian “authority” claims accepted de facto by some traffic.  
- Confidence: Medium-Low (long-horizon uncertainty)

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Intermittent kinetic pauses and limited mediation produce partial traffic recovery (30–50% of baseline) by 3–6 months with elevated but declining insurance. Full multi-domain normality (per definition) more probable only after further capability attrition and economic costs force Iranian pragmatists to accept reduced Hormuz leverage, likely in the 6–18 month window. Residual proxy risk and insurance stickiness keep the path below 50% until later horizons.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
A durable, monitored safe-passage deal within weeks, rapid US force posture reduction, and Iranian hardliners sidelined could push 3-month probability toward 45–50% and 6-month above 65%. Requires Iranian calculation that survival depends on economic relief more than chokepoint control.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Iran treats Hormuz as non-negotiable residual power; proxies maintain low-level campaign; insurance market remains structurally elevated. Probabilities stay below 40% even at 1 year. Supported by recent rejection of joint management and continued attacks during pauses.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Strongest case that the forecast overestimates normalization: Iranian regime may view partial survival after heavy losses as requiring permanent asymmetric tools; historical Tanker War residual risk was long; insurance markets after Red Sea and Hormuz may reprice permanently higher. Strongest case that it underestimates: severe Iranian military degradation + global economic pain + US political incentives could force faster pragmatic deal than base rates suggest. Load-bearing assumptions: (1) Iranian hardliners retain veto power over Hormuz concessions; (2) insurance and shipowners require multi-week clean records before normalizing. Evidence that would falsify: sustained ≥70% traffic + premiums ≤1.5× for 30+ days without kinetic events.
