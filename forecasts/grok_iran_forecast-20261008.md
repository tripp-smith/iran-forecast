I will ground the forecast in current reporting on the 2026 Iran conflict, Hormuz shipping, war-risk insurance, and energy flows, then build the probabilities from reference classes rather than narrative.

The Persian Gulf is not near functional normality. Day 223 of the Hormuz crisis still shows restricted transit, live attacks on shipping, elevated war-risk pricing, damaged LNG capacity, and active preparations for renewed major combat. A military pause would not meet the definition used here.

## 1. Forecast Question

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each horizon: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months. Horizons are measured from 8 October 2026. Each figure is the probability that the operational definition has been met by that date and has held, not the probability of a temporary lull.

## 2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”

All of the following must be true together, and must have held for at least 14 consecutive days:

- Direct military activity: no state-on-state kinetic strikes between the United States or Israel and Iran, and no IRGC or Iranian state strikes on commercial vessels or Gulf energy infrastructure.
- Proxy activity: Houthi and Iraqi-militia attacks on Gulf shipping and energy targets at or below the pre-28 February 2026 baseline, not merely paused for days.
- Shipping security: the Strait of Hormuz and adjacent Gulf of Oman waters open to commercial traffic without Iranian transit permits, without a prohibited zone, and without convoy or naval facilitation as a condition of routine passage.
- Commercial transit: AIS-visible commercial crossings averaging at least 70 percent of the pre-crisis baseline of about 85 ships a day (about 60 or more a day), including a normal mix of tankers, not only dark or high-risk hulls.
- War-risk insurance: hull war-risk widely available near peacetime terms, at or below roughly 0.4 percent of hull value per voyage (about 2 times the pre-crisis 0.1–0.25 percent range), without a government backstop as the main market.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: non-Iranian Gulf crude exports, via Hormuz plus already-operating bypasses, at or above 90 percent of February 2026 levels, and Qatar able to load LNG from remaining operable capacity without war interruption. Full restoration of destroyed Ras Laffan trains is not required; three-year physical repairs are treated as infrastructure damage, not as a failure of the security regime.
- Energy-market stress: no acute Hormuz disruption premium dominating freight and prices. Brent returning to a pre-war level is not itself proof. Emergency stock releases specifically tied to the war should have ended.
- Force posture: US naval presence back to routine patrol rather than blockade or strait-control operations; Iranian forces not enforcing closure or permit rules.
- Escalation triggers: no new major trigger in the prior 14 days, including a mine campaign, an enforced closure, a strike on a regional energy hub, or a decision to resume major combat.

A ceasefire communiqué, a lower oil price, or a quiet week of AIS data does not qualify.

## 4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior

1. Tanker War, 1984–1988. Relevant because a Gulf state used limited attacks on shipping to impose insurance and routing costs without sinking the trade. It breaks down because that war was Iran–Iraq, not a US–Israel campaign against Iran, and because today’s drone and missile accuracy is higher. Traffic and insurance normalized only after the August 1988 ceasefire, with commercial confidence lagging by months. Suggested lag after a real settlement: 3–9 months.

2. 1990–91 Gulf War. Relevant as a US war touching Gulf energy. It breaks down because Iraq never successfully interdicted Hormuz and the campaign was short. The risk premium faded within weeks of the ceasefire. Suggested lag if the threat is removed decisively: 2–8 weeks. This is the fast case, and it is a weak match.

3. 2019 Gulf attacks and Abqaiq. Relevant for insurance and price spikes from limited strikes. It breaks down because there was no general war and traffic mostly continued. Elevated premia lasted weeks to a few months. Useful only as a floor on how fast markets reprice after incidents stop.

4. Red Sea and Bab al-Mandab, late 2023–2025. Most relevant commercial analogue. A sub-state actor with Iranian links depressed transit for more than a year, rerouting became semi-structural, and insurance stayed listed while the threat continued. Partial military suppression did not restore baseline traffic. Suggested timeline while the threat actor retains capability: no functional normality.

5. Black Sea grain corridor, 2022–2025. Relevant for deals that reopen a waterway without ending the war. Traffic returned in bursts, insurance stayed political, and collapses were repeated. Suggested pattern: oscillation, not normality, until the political conflict is settled.

6. Post-ceasefire insurance lags in the Tanker War and the 1980s. Even after shots stopped, owners and underwriters waited through a clean period. A working rule is 4–12 weeks of no incidents before premia approach peacetime.

Base-rate prior before case-specific adjustment, for a chokepoint war with no durable settlement yet:

- 1 week: 3 percent
- 1 month: 8 percent
- 3 months: 15 percent
- 6 months: 28 percent
- 1 year: 42 percent
- 18 months: 55 percent

The prior already assumes most such wars do not normalize while fighting and interdiction continue, and that settlement, when it comes, is followed by a commercial lag.

## 6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization

1. Fatigue and mediation. Eight months of cost, ongoing Oman-linked talks, and repeated US statements that a deal is the preferred end state keep a political off-ramp open.
2. Oil adaptation. Pipelines, Fujairah, and dark or facilitated transits have already restored a large share of crude. That reduces the incentive for Gulf producers to demand total closure and shows physical flows can rebound quickly once risk falls.
3. Iranian constraint. A US blockade has cut Iranian loadings sharply relative to neighbors. Tehran has a reason to trade strait harassment for sanctions or blockade relief, if it can sell that at home.
4. Insurance elasticity. The June quote drop showed underwriters reprice within days of a credible pause. The lag is weeks, not years, if incidents actually stop.
5. US political calendar. A pre-midterm deal that lowers oil is a plausible incentive. It is not the base case.

## 7. Key Drivers Against Normalization

1. No settlement of the political object. Nuclear limits, sanctions, blockade, and regime aims are unresolved. The last MoU addressed transit and not the war.
2. Iran’s remaining leverage is the strait. As neighbors’ exports recover and Iran’s do not, harassment is the cheap way to reimpose costs. That logic points to more attacks, not fewer, while the blockade holds.
3. Live escalation machinery. Strike options are being prepared. Recent attacks already broke the last pause. A new round before or after the midterms resets the insurance clock.
4. Proxies and bypasses. Houthi pressure on the Red Sea reduces the Saudi bypass and spreads the war geographically.
5. Infrastructure and trust. Ras Laffan damage caps LNG for years. Owners who returned and were struck will not treat the next communiqué as normality.
6. Force posture. Passage still depends on US facilitation and a southern corridor. That is a wartime operating mode.

Ranked gating order: political durability of a ceasefire, then a clean incident window, then insurance, then traffic. Oil volumes are not the gate.

## 9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon

### 1 week

- Probability: 1%
- Base-rate anchor: 3 percent, cut for an active attack surge.
- Current-evidence adjustment: Down. Eleven ships struck in eight days, waivers revoked, strike options in preparation, traffic near 12 percent of baseline.
- Core rationale: The definition cannot be met inside 14 clean days when the last week failed the incident test.
- Main gating factor: Ongoing strikes and militarized passage.
- What would raise this forecast materially: Nothing plausible inside seven days.
- What would lower this forecast materially: Already near the floor.
- Confidence in this estimate: High

### 1 month

- Probability: 4%
- Base-rate anchor: 8 percent.
- Current-evidence adjustment: Down. A new MoU would still need a clean incident window that has not started, and insurance repricing lags.
- Core rationale: Even a sudden deal around the midterms would leave traffic, premia, and posture outside the definition by early November. A decision to resume major combat would zero the path.
- Main gating factor: No credible, incident-free settlement window.
- What would raise this forecast materially: An immediate, verified stop to ship attacks plus a published transit regime both sides honor.
- What would lower this forecast materially: US strikes before 3 November or a formal Iranian prohibited zone.
- Confidence in this estimate: High

### 3 months

- Probability: 12%
- Base-rate anchor: 15 percent.
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slightly down. Mediation exists, but the last pause failed in under a month and proxies are active.
- Core rationale: This is the first horizon on which a deal, a 4–8 week clean window, and partial insurance normalization could theoretically stack. The base case is another cycle of pause and strikes. January 2027 still sits inside the wartime reference class, not the post-Tanker-War class.
- Main gating factor: Durability of any ceasefire past the first incident.
- What would raise this forecast materially: A written transit and sanctions arrangement that survives 30 days without ship strikes.
- What would lower this forecast materially: Renewed major combat or Houthi closure pressure on Bab al-Mandab.
- Confidence in this estimate: Medium

### 6 months

- Probability: 22%
- Base-rate anchor: 28 percent.
- Current-evidence adjustment: Down. Adaptation in crude is real, but Tehran’s incentive to harass rises as neighbors export and Iran cannot. LNG and Red Sea risk keep the system abnormal even if Hormuz volumes rise.
- Core rationale: By April 2027 a settlement is plausible, not likely. If it arrives early in the window, insurance and traffic can catch up. If it arrives late, the 14-day test is met but the commercial regime may still be short of 70 percent transit and peacetime premia.
- Main gating factor: Political settlement early enough to leave a commercial lag inside the horizon.
- What would raise this forecast materially: End of the blockade and ship attacks together, with underwriters cutting quotes toward 0.5 percent.
- What would lower this forecast materially: A mine incident or a second Ras Laffan-scale strike.
- Confidence in this estimate: Medium

### 1 year

- Probability: 38%
- Base-rate anchor: 42 percent.
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slightly down. Wars of this type often last longer than governments predict. Partial oil recovery reduces urgency. Residual Iranian capability keeps a harassment option alive after any paper deal.
- Core rationale: A year is long enough for war exhaustion, a post-midterm policy reset, and one or two failed pauses to produce a more durable arrangement. It is not long enough to treat normalization as the default. The Red Sea case is the warning: threat persistence blocks commercial return even when headline fighting dips.
- Main gating factor: Whether Iran still uses Hormuz as leverage in October 2027.
- What would raise this forecast materially: A monitored ceasefire with six clean months and AIS traffic above 50 percent and rising.
- What would lower this forecast materially: Frozen conflict with episodic strikes, the Tanker War pattern.
- Confidence in this estimate: Low

### 18 months

- Probability: 52%
- Base-rate anchor: 55 percent.
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slightly down. Physical LNG damage does not by itself block the definition, but a frozen conflict with retained Iranian sea-denial capability can.
- Core rationale: By April 2028 the modal outcome is a messy political arrangement or a de facto stalemate that has stopped routine ship attacks. Just over half is the judgment that exhaustion wins more often than not by then, with a wide band. It is not a claim that the pre-2026 Gulf returns.
- Main gating factor: Conversion of a stalemate into behavior underwriters will price as peacetime.
- What would raise this forecast materially: Verified drawdown of coastal anti-ship systems and a year of baseline proxy activity.
- What would lower this forecast materially: Institutionalized wartime routing, as in the Red Sea, still in place in 2028.
- Confidence in this estimate: Low

Long-range figures stay near 50 percent because the definition still requires insurance, uncoerced transit, and posture change. Oil-flow recovery alone would have produced higher numbers and would have been the wrong target.

## 10. Most Likely Path

The next one to three months stay in the current regime: militarized partial transit, episodic IRGC attacks, US retaliation, crude moving at a discount to normal via a mix of facilitated Hormuz passages and bypasses, LNG largely offline, and premia elevated. A negotiating spike around the US midterms is likely. A durable deal is not. The modal path through mid-2027 is oscillation, with traffic sometimes reaching 20–40 percent of baseline and then falling after the next incident. Functional normality, if it arrives inside 18 months, arrives after a settlement that markets believe, not after the next quiet week. Oil volumes normalize before insurance and before LNG.

## 11. Faster Normalization Case

A pre- or post-midterm deal trades nuclear limits and a US blockade pause for an Iranian stop to ship attacks and proxy restraint. Incidents cease for six weeks. Underwriters cut quotes. Facilitated transit becomes ordinary transit. By three to six months the definition is met even though Ras Laffan is still damaged and Brent has not fully mean-reverted. Probability mass on this path is what supports the 12 percent at three months and the upper half of the one-year figure. It requires Iranian decision-makers to prefer revenue over leverage, and Washington to accept less than “wiped out.”

## 12. Prolonged Instability Case

Major combat resumes, or the current harassment equilibrium hardens. Iran keeps a permit and strike regime. The United States keeps a blockade and escort regime. Houthis tax the Red Sea bypass. Owners treat every reopening as temporary, which is already the Kpler description of the last MoU. Insurance never approaches peacetime. By 18 months the Gulf looks like a managed wartime chokepoint, with crude adapted through pipelines and dark fleets and LNG structurally impaired. This path is why the 18-month probability is only slightly above half.

## 13. Red-Team Critique

The forecast may be too low if adaptation is smuggled into the definition. September crude already near 70–80 percent of pre-war, plus a US-run corridor, could be called functional by traders who only need barrels. That reading is rejected here because insurance, uncoerced transit, and posture fail. If the user only cares about barrels, add roughly 15–25 points at the 6- to 18-month horizons.

The forecast may be too high at one year and 18 months. The Tanker War lasted four years. Iran’s incentive to harass strengthens as neighbors recover. US domestic politics can sustain a low-intensity conflict. A frozen conflict with retained coastal missiles is the strongest reason these figures should not be above 60 percent. The 52 percent at 18 months is the softest number in the set.

Load-bearing assumptions: Iran retains sea-denial capability; underwriters need about a month of clean transits; proxies do not become the main barrier once state strikes stop; dark-fleet crude does not count as commercial normality.

## 14. Critical Signals to Monitor

- UKMTO and CENTCOM incident counts in Hormuz, weekly. Two clean weeks would raise the one-month figure. Another double-digit strike week would cut the three-month figure.
- AIS commercial crossings versus the 85-a-day baseline, and the share that is US-facilitated or dark.
- Hull war-risk quotes from Lloyd’s and major brokers, not freight anecdotes.
- Whether the US blockade of Iranian ports is on or off, and whether Iranian loadings at Kharg resume.
- Qatar LNG cargo count. Security normality can return before volume normality.
- Houthi incidents at Bab al-Mandab and status of the East–West pipeline.
- A dated, mutual transit protocol versus another unsigned pause.
- Any Pentagon execution of prepared strike options.
