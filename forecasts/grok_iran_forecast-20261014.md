**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each of these horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality requires simultaneous, sustained satisfaction of the following observable criteria for a continuous period of at least 30 days (longer for insurance and market confidence metrics). A temporary ceasefire, price dip, or partial shuttle recovery does not qualify.

- **Direct military activity**: No sustained campaign of US, Israeli, or Iranian strikes on each other’s forces, infrastructure, or territory. Isolated incidents do not disqualify, but ongoing exchanges of fire do.
- **Proxy activity**: Houthi and other Iran-aligned proxy attacks on Saudi, Emirati, or other Gulf infrastructure, airports, energy facilities, and shipping reduced to pre-February 2026 baseline levels (sporadic or none). No major territorial advances in Yemen that expand pressure on the Bab el-Mandeb.
- **Shipping security**: JMIC/UKMTO threat assessment for Hormuz and adjacent waters no higher than moderate. Fewer than one confirmed significant attack on commercial vessels per week, sustained for four weeks. No enforced Iranian permit/fee/“illegal route” regime that systematically deters traffic.
- **Commercial transit levels**: Combined AIS-visible and estimated dark traffic averaging at least 70–80% of the 2025 baseline (~85–100+ commercial vessels/day depending on counting method; PortWatch-style ~60+). Free or lightly coordinated transit without routine naval escort as the primary enabler.
- **War-risk insurance**: Additional war-risk premiums ≤2–3× peacetime baseline (roughly ≤0.3–0.5% of hull value per voyage vs. current multiples of 15× or higher in some quotes), with broad commercial availability and no dependence on ad-hoc US government backstops as the main capacity source.
- **Oil and LNG export continuity**: Middle East crude and LNG exports via normal Hormuz routes at ≥90% of pre-war levels on a sustained basis, without reliance on high-risk dark/shuttle/bypass arrangements as the dominant mode. Refinery and terminal operations largely restored.
- **Energy market stress**: Brent risk premium attributable to Gulf disruption ≤10–15%; volatility normalized relative to other fundamentals. No persistent physical shortages or extreme freight spikes driven by Hormuz.
- **Force posture**: US/coalition forces able to sustain or reduce elevated presence without crisis-driven surge; Iranian forces not actively enforcing a de facto closure or high-intensity interdiction.
- **Absence of major new escalation triggers**: No new nuclear breakout signals, major attacks on third-country infrastructure, or opening of additional fronts that reset the risk environment.

This is a high bar by design. Military pauses and partial oil recovery via protected shuttles fall short.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot** (as of 14 October 2026)

The war began with US-Israeli strikes on 28 February 2026. A June Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) for a 60-day negotiating window and partial Hormuz reopening collapsed by early July; the US naval blockade of Iranian ports resumed and remains in force. President Trump has publicly paused US strikes on Iran until after the 3 November midterm elections while describing discussions as productive; Iranian officials have called US responses to their Hormuz conditions inadequate and insist Hormuz issues precede nuclear talks. Indirect talks continue via mediators (Qatar, Pakistan, Oman).

Maritime conditions remain severe. AIS-visible commercial traffic is roughly 10% of the 2025 baseline (recent 7-day averages ~8–14 crossings/day vs. ~85). Hundreds of vessels are loitering. Iran continues high-tempo attacks on tankers (record weekly totals in early October, including off Qatar and near the UAE), using mines, projectiles, drones, and new spotter tactics, while claiming control and targeting “illegal” southern-corridor/shuttle traffic. **4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant classes (adjusted for differences in intensity, technology, and stakes):

- **1980s Tanker War (Iran-Iraq)**: ~4 years of systematic tanker/mine/missile attacks; shipping continued at reduced levels under risk; US escorts (Earnest Will) raised costs but did not end attacks. Full commercial normalization followed the 1988 ceasefire. Suggests multi-year disruption while conflict persists; faster recovery once hostilities end. Breaks down: lower missile/drone density, no modern dark-shipping/AIS opacity, different political stakes.
- **2019 Gulf tanker attacks and seizures**: Short, limited campaign; insurance spiked briefly then eased; traffic largely recovered within weeks to months once attacks stopped. Suggests rapid commercial recovery if interdiction ceases. Breaks down: far lower intensity and no full war.
- **Red Sea Houthi campaign (2023–2025)**: Months of diversion, elevated insurance, and reduced traffic despite escorts; partial normalization only after pressure reduced. Suggests commercial recovery lags military de-escalation by 3–12 months. Relevant for proxy and insurance dynamics.
- **1990–91 Gulf War**: Rapid military resolution; shipping recovered quickly post-liberation. Suggests short timelines after decisive end. Breaks down: conventional land war with clear endpoint vs. current hybrid attrition.
- **Ukraine Black Sea grain/shipping disruptions**: Prolonged risk, insurance issues, and reduced volumes lasting years despite partial corridors. Suggests sticky commercial effects. Breaks down: different geography and actors.
- **Prior Hormuz threats (2011–12, intermittent seizures)**: Traffic rarely collapsed fully; recovery within weeks to months once threats receded. Suggests resilience when no sustained interdiction.

**Base-rate prior** (before case-specific adjustment; rough midpoint of comparable disruption timelines):

- 1 week: ~2–5%
- 1 month: ~5–10%
- 3 months: ~15–25%
- 6 months: ~30–45%
- 1 year: ~50–65%
- 18 months: ~60–75%

These reflect that functional commercial/insurance normality typically trails military pauses and requires demonstrated security for months.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

Ranked: (1) US political calendar and stated preference to avoid pre-midterm escalation plus mutual interest in lower oil prices; (2) demonstrated partial recovery of oil flows via workarounds, showing commercial adaptability; (3) ongoing mediated talks and mutual exhaustion after ~7.5 months; (4) US air/unmanned protection enabling some dark transit.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

Ranked: (1) Persistent Iranian interdiction capability and demonstrated willingness to attack even as flows partially recover (core leverage); (2) high war-risk insurance costs and insurer caution that lag any military pause; (3) Houthi/proxy escalation and second-front risk in Yemen/Red Sea; (4) unresolved political terms (Iranian conditions on Hormuz control, sanctions, frozen assets, nuclear sequencing); (5) US blockade and elevated force posture that sustain Iranian incentives to disrupt.

**8. Decomposed Sub-Forecasts** (probability that each sub-condition is largely met by the horizon)

These are not averaged; gating factors (especially sustained shipping security + insurance normalization + proxy restraint) dominate the joint probability.

- Major reduction in direct strikes: 1w ~40%; 1m ~55%; 3m ~65%; 6m ~75%; 1y ~80%; 18m ~85%. (US pause helps near-term; longer depends on deal.)
- Sustained safe commercial transit (low attacks + high volume): 1w ~5%; 1m ~10%; 3m ~20%; 6m ~35%; 1y ~55%; 18m ~65%.
- Meaningful insurance normalization: 1w ~3%; 1m ~8%; 3m ~18%; 6m ~35%; 1y ~55%; 18m ~70%.
- Oil/LNG export recovery to high sustained levels via normal routes: 1w ~15%; 1m ~25%; 3m ~40%; 6m ~55%; 1y ~70%; 18m ~75%.
- Proxy restraint: 1w ~10%; 1m ~15%; 3m ~25%; 6m ~40%; 1y ~55%; 18m ~65%.
- Force drawdown/posture stabilization: 1w ~20%; 1m ~30%; 3m ~45%; 6m ~60%; 1y ~70%; 18m ~75%.
- No new major escalation trigger: 1w ~70%; 1m ~60%; 3m ~55%; 6m ~50%; 1y ~45%; 18m ~40%. (Risk accumulates.)

Gating: Transit security and insurance are the binding constraints. Oil can partially recover under protection without full normality.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued intermittent talks, US restraint around the midterms, partial oil recovery via protected/dark/shuttle means, and persistent Iranian maritime pressure plus Houthi activity. Full functional normality is unlikely before late 2027 without a political breakthrough. Oil markets price elevated risk; commercial shipping remains cautious.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Post-midterm US priority on lower energy prices + Iranian economic pressure produces a sequenced deal (Hormuz first, limited nuclear steps). Attacks drop sharply, traffic rebounds within weeks, insurers reprice within 1–2 months. Probability mass shifts earlier (e.g., 3-month ~35–40%). Requires both sides to accept partial wins.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Talks fail; post-election US strikes resume; Iran maintains high-tempo interdiction and expands proxies. Traffic stays suppressed, insurance remains punitive, bypasses face attacks. Frozen conflict with chronic disruption through 2027–28. Probabilities fall (1-year ~25–30%).

**13. Red-Team Critique**

I may be underestimating near-term normalization if a narrow shipping deal is struck quickly and insurers follow traffic data aggressively, or if Iranian capability degrades faster than reported. I may be overestimating longer-term probabilities if the conflict settles into a durable low-intensity interdiction equilibrium (as in parts of the Tanker War or Red Sea) that keeps premiums and volumes permanently lower, or if Houthi pressure on Saudi creates cascading Gulf instability. Load-bearing assumptions: Iranian interdiction remains effective and politically valuable; insurance markets are slower to normalize than military facts; no decisive military outcome ends the war abruptly.
