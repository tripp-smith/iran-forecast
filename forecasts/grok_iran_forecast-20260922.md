**1. Forecast Question**  
Probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War, by horizons of 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months (as of 22 September 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
Functional normality requires concurrent, observable conditions across domains for a sustained period (not a single-day snapshot or temporary pause):

- Direct military activity: No sustained reciprocal kinetic strikes by major state actors (US/coalition and Iran) on each other’s forces, bases, energy infrastructure, or territory in the Gulf theater; residual low-level incidents do not reverse trend.
- Proxy activity: Meaningful restraint by Iranian-aligned proxies (notably Houthis) such that they do not systematically close or heavily attrit alternative routes (Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb) or strike Gulf energy nodes at scale.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: AIS-visible commercial transits recover to ≥70–80% of pre-war baseline (~100–125 large commercial vessels/day pre-February 2026); mines cleared and not routinely re-laid; attacks on commercial vessels rare and not systemic.
- Commercial transit levels: Regular, unescorted or lightly escorted traffic by major Western, Asian, and regional operators without widespread dark running or avoidance.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums for Hormuz transits fall to ≤0.5–1% of hull value (from current elevated multiples of 15–40× peacetime ~0.1–0.25%) and capacity is broadly available without special government backstops dominating.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Combined Gulf (ex-Iran where sanctioned) crude + products + LNG flows recover to ≥80–85% of pre-war levels through Hormuz or fully equivalent secure alternatives; Iranian exports (if any) move under predictable rules rather than shadow-fleet improvisation.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent risk premium attributable to Gulf disruption compresses materially (current ~$100–101 vs ~$72 pre-crisis baseline implies substantial residual premium); volatility normalizes toward pre-war ranges.
- Force posture of major actors: US/coalition naval posture shifts from high-tempo blockade/escort/interdiction to routine presence; Iranian forces cease systematic interdiction or toll regimes.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No significant new mining campaigns, massed anti-ship strikes, base attacks, or nuclear-related escalations that re-freeze traffic/insurance.

A temporary ceasefire, partial escorted traffic, lower oil prices alone, or battlefield pause does not qualify. Commercial and insurance normalization lag military de-escalation and are gating.

War began ~28 February 2026 with US-Israeli strikes. A June Islamabad MOU produced a partial, fragile reopening and evacuation efforts; it collapsed amid tit-for-tat strikes in late June/July. Subsequent cycles of escalation and pauses have occurred. As of mid-to-late September: a multi-day “fire pause” on ballistic/drone attacks against host states exists (roughly 10+ days by 19 September reporting), but maritime incidents, claims of attacks on vessels, Iranian assertions of control/restricted zones/tolls, and US blockade/redirection activity continue.<grok:render card_id="513e04" card_type="citation_card" type="render_inline_citation"><argument name="citation_id">40</argument></grok:render>

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant classes (Gulf/chokepoint/maritime disruption + limited regional wars):

- 1980s Tanker War (Iran-Iraq): Prolonged low-to-medium intensity attacks on shipping; traffic adapted with escorts/convoys but never fully “normal” until war ended; functional commercial recovery lagged ceasefire by months to years. Suggests multi-month to multi-year horizon for full insurance/commercial normalization even after major kinetic decline.
- 2019–2021 Gulf tanker attacks / mine incidents / Saudi infrastructure strikes: Discrete escalations produced temporary traffic dips and premium spikes; recovery to near-baseline occurred in weeks to a few months once kinetic intensity fell and no sustained interdiction regime locked in. Breaks down because intensity and duration were far lower than 2026.
- Red Sea/Houthi campaign (2023–ongoing into 2026 context): Sustained proxy disruption forced major rerouting; insurance and traffic normalization incomplete years later despite intermittent pauses. Highly relevant for proxy and insurance lag; less so for direct state-on-state intensity.
- Suez crises / other chokepoint closures (historical): Physical or political closures produce rapid traffic collapse; reopening often requires political settlement + demining/clearance; commercial confidence lags clearance by weeks–months.
- Limited US–Iran confrontations (e.g., 1988, 2019–20): De-escalation possible without full regime change, but residual risk premia and force postures persist.
- Post-ceasefire Gulf/Lebanon patterns: Military pauses frequently precede commercial normalization by 1–6+ months; full insurance reset rarer without durable political framework.

Base-rate prior (pre-current-evidence adjustment, timeline for functional normality under the demanding multi-domain definition):  
1 week ≈ 1–3%; 1 month ≈ 5–10%; 3 months ≈ 15–25%; 6 months ≈ 30–40%; 1 year ≈ 45–55%; 18 months ≈ 55–65%.  
These reflect that maritime/insurance recovery historically lags kinetic pauses, especially when control of a chokepoint remains contested and proxies remain active.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Mutual exhaustion + economic costs (Iranian internal stress; Gulf state/US interest in energy flows).  
2. Possibility of mediated interim navigation arrangement (Oman/Gulf channels).  
3. Demonstrated ability to move partial volumes under escort/STS.  
4. Fire pauses creating political space.  
5. Market adaptation (rerouting, alternative supply).

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Contested control of Hormuz itself (Iran seeks recognized leverage; US/GCC reject).  
2. Absence of durable political settlement; repeated MOU/ceasefire failures.  
3. Insurance and commercial confidence lag (high premiums, crew/vessel risk aversion).  
4. Residual kinetic capacity + proxy activation risk.  
5. Force posture inertia and escalation triggers (nuclear, bases, mining).  
6. Second-order effects (Asian buyer caution, Gulf state hedging).

**8. Decomposed Sub-Forecasts** (approximate probabilities of material progress on each component by horizon; these are not independent)

- Major reduction in direct strikes: 1w 15–25%; 1m 30–40%; 3m 45–55%; 6m 55–65%; 1y 65–75%; 18m 70–80%.  
- Sustained safe commercial transit (≥70–80% baseline, largely unescorted): 1w <5%; 1m 5–10%; 3m 15–25%; 6m 30–40%; 1y 45–55%; 18m 55–65%.  
- Meaningful insurance normalization (≤0.5–1% range, broad capacity): 1w <5%; 1m 5–10%; 3m 10–20%; 6m 25–35%; 1y 40–50%; 18m 50–60%.  
- Oil/LNG export recovery to high fraction of pre-war: 1w 10–15%; 1m 20–30%; 3m 35–45%; 6m 50–60%; 1y 60–70%; 18m 65–75%.  
- Proxy restraint: 1w 20–30%; 1m 30–40%; 3m 40–50%; 6m 50–60%; 1y 55–65%; 18m 60–70%.  
- Force drawdown / posture normalization: lags the above by 1–3+ months.  
- No major new escalation trigger: declining probability of clean path as horizon lengthens due to more opportunity for shocks.

Gating factors are commercial transit + insurance; military pause alone is insufficient.

**9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon**

**1 Week**  
• Probability: 2%  
• Base-rate anchor: Near-zero for full multi-domain normality in acute chokepoint wars.  
• Current-evidence adjustment: Slight upward from pure base rate due to existing fire pause, but traffic/insurance data show no rapid shift.  
• Core rationale: Structural barriers (contested control, high premiums, low visible traffic) cannot resolve in days.  
• Main gating factor: Commercial transit and insurance.  
• What would raise this forecast materially: Sudden durable navigation accord + immediate traffic surge + premium collapse.  
• What would lower this forecast materially: New massed vessel attacks or base strikes.  
• Confidence in this estimate: High  

**1 Month**  
• Probability: 8%  
• Base-rate anchor: Low teens at best in comparable cases for partial recovery; full normality rarer.  
• Current-evidence adjustment: Modest upward for pause and adaptation, downward for failed prior MOUs and residual incidents.  
• Core rationale: Possible interim deal or sustained pause, but insurance lag and Iranian leverage incentives make full normality unlikely.  
• Main gating factor: Insurance + sustained unescorted transit.  
• What would raise: Verified demining + premium drop + GCC-Iran practical arrangement.  
• What would lower: Renewed systematic interdiction or major proxy escalation.  
• Confidence: Medium-High  

**3 Months**  
• Probability: 22%  
• Base-rate anchor: 15–25% range from reference classes for meaningful stabilization after intensity peaks.  
• Current-evidence adjustment: Slight upward for economic pressures; downward for contested Hormuz status and proxy risk.  
• Core rationale: Highest-probability window for a workable interim navigation regime if mutual costs bite, but commercial confidence rebuilds slowly.  
• Main gating factor: Combination of military restraint + insurance capacity return.  
• What would raise: Durable political framework or effective third-party (Oman/China) guarantee of free navigation.  
• What would lower: Collapse of pause into sustained tanker war or Red Sea closure.  
• Confidence: Medium  

**6 Months**  
• Probability: 38%  
• Base-rate anchor: ~30–40%.  
• Current-evidence adjustment: Balanced; adaptation visible but structural contest over the strait persists.  
• Core rationale: Time allows more negotiation cycles and market workarounds, yet full multi-domain normality still requires resolution of control/insurance issues.  
• Main gating factor: Political settlement quality and insurance reset.  
• What would raise: Verifiable force posture shifts + sustained high transit volumes at moderate premiums.  
• What would lower: Entrenchment of dual-control or high-toll regime + continued high premiums.  
• Confidence: Medium  

**1 Year**  
• Probability: 52%  
• Base-rate anchor: ~45–55%.  
• Current-evidence adjustment: Mild upward for cumulative economic pressure on Iran and Gulf adaptation capacity.  
• Core rationale: Longer horizon raises chance of either settlement or stable (if suboptimal) coexistence with higher baseline risk; full pre-war normality remains demanding.  
• Main gating factor: Whether contested control hardens into a new normal or is resolved.  
• What would raise: Comprehensive political deal including navigation guarantees.  
• What would lower: Protracted low-intensity maritime campaign + proxy expansion.  
• Confidence: Medium  

**18 Months**  
• Probability: 62%  
• Base-rate anchor: ~55–65%.  
• Current-evidence adjustment: Modest upward.  
• Core rationale: Most reference classes show eventual functional recovery or adaptation within 1–2 years after peak intensity, though residual risk premia often linger. Demanding definition keeps probability from higher.  
• Main gating factor: Cumulative political and market processes.  
• What would raise: Clear end to systematic interdiction + insurance market reset.  
• What would lower: Regime-level decisions to treat Hormuz control as permanent strategic asset.  
• Confidence: Medium (long-horizon uncertainty higher)  

Synthesis: Sub-component probabilities are combined non-mechanically. Commercial transit and insurance are strict gates; military pause and export recovery are necessary but insufficient. The joint probability is therefore lower than the highest individual sub-component. Longer horizons allow more independent resolution paths, raising the joint figure.

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Continued low-to-medium intensity maritime contestation with intermittent pauses and partial escorted/STS flows. Gradual volume recovery via adaptation, but insurance and commercial confidence lag. Periodic diplomatic efforts produce limited interim arrangements that fray. Functional normality arrives (if at all) via slow accretion of restraint + market workarounds rather than a clean settlement, most plausibly in the 6–18 month window.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Mutual recognition that prolonged disruption is mutually costly; Oman/Gulf-mediated practical navigation protocol; rapid demining verification; insurance capacity returns aggressively; proxies stand down. Could push 3–6 month probabilities substantially higher.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Iran prioritizes Hormuz leverage as core residual power; US maintains hard blockade posture; proxies expand; repeated escalation cycles; insurance market permanently reprices the Gulf higher. Normalization delayed beyond 18 months or redefined downward.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Possible over-estimate of normalization speed: reference classes under-weight the unique combination of contested chokepoint sovereignty claims + nuclear overhang + proxy networks; insurance markets may structurally reprice rather than mean-revert. Possible under-estimate: economic pain on Iran and energy-importing Asia could force faster practical accommodation than pure military logic suggests; US political calendar or Gulf state hedging could produce surprising deals. Load-bearing assumptions include continued Iranian regime cohesion and absence of catastrophic escalation (nuclear use or major base loss). Evidence base has conflicting throughput claims; dark fleet activity reduces visibility.
