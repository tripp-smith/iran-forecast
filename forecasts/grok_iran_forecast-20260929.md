**1. Forecast Question**

Probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by horizons of 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months (as of ~29 September 2026 / Day ~213 of the conflict).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality requires simultaneous, observable satisfaction of all of the following for a sustained period (not a single day or temporary pause):

- Direct military activity: No US/coalition or Iranian airstrikes, ballistic/missile/drone attacks on military or commercial targets in the Gulf/Hormuz/Gulf of Oman, or reciprocal tanker-for-tanker actions, for at least 30 consecutive days.
- Proxy activity: No significant Houthi, Hezbollah, or other Iranian-aligned attacks on Gulf shipping, energy infrastructure, or partner states that materially affect Gulf transit or exports.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Sustained commercial transit without systematic mining, boarding, disabling fire, or exclusion-zone enforcement; UKMTO/JMIC risk assessments at or near pre-war levels; no mass AIS dark sailing required for safety.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily commercial vessel transits (all types) averaging ≥80% of pre-war baseline (~100–130/day depending on source), with commodity/tanker flows supporting ≥15–18 mb/d oil/LNG equivalent through or effectively via the strait system (including verified STS only as residual, not primary).
- War-risk insurance conditions: Additional war-risk premiums ≤2–3× peacetime baseline (pre-war ~0.1–0.25% of hull value), with broad commercial capacity available without special government facilities or extreme voyage-by-voyage pricing.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Persian Gulf crude/product/LNG exports sustained near pre-war volumes (≥90% of ~16–20+ mb/d crude liquids baseline via normal routes), without chronic reliance on East-West pipeline, Fujairah, or massive STS workarounds as primary mechanism.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent crude and related benchmarks free of war-premium spikes driven by Hormuz risk; implied volatility and crack spreads reflecting normal supply security rather than chokepoint fear.
- Force posture of major actors: Material drawdown or de-alert of US/coalition naval/air assets specifically dedicated to Hormuz escort/blockade/enforcement; Iranian forces not asserting exclusive administrative control or toll regimes incompatible with freedom of navigation.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No unresolved nuclear enrichment reconstitution, leadership targeting cycles, or territorial control shifts (e.g., Houthi consolidation at Bab el-Mandeb) that re-trigger the above.

A temporary ceasefire, lower oil prices alone, or battlefield pause without commercial/insurance recovery does not qualify. All domains must be operationally normalized.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot**

Conflict ongoing since ~28 February 2026 (US-Israeli strikes under Operation Epic Fury; Iranian retaliation including Hormuz disruption). As of late September 2026 (Day ~213):

- Direct exchanges intermittent; multi-day/week pauses in announced ashore strikes occur, but claims of drone/mine/projectile incidents on shipping continue; US “tanker-for-tanker” and coastal target strikes have occurred in September waves.
- Shipping: Independent trackers (Kpler, Windward, IMF PortWatch, GEF AIS) show commercial transits far below pre-war (~3–25/day range recently vs ~100–130; many dark). Oil flows partially recovered via escorts, STS in Gulf of Oman, Fujairah/Yanbu pipelines, and dark sailing—estimates of Hormuz-related crude ~3.7–9+ mb/d or higher in optimistic US claims, with total Gulf exports sometimes cited near 70–80% of pre-war via workarounds. Not normal commercial transit.
- Insurance: War-risk still heavily elevated (reports of ~15× or several percent of hull value; special facilities/reinsurance in play). Commercial capacity constrained.
- Energy markets: Brent recently in high-$90s to ~$108 range with war-driven volatility; not normalized.
- Diplomacy: Mediated talks (Qatar et al.) ongoing; Iran offered 7-day Hormuz reopen tied to blockade lift/sanctions relief/assets; Trump publicly rejected. Midterms (early November) and possible post-election US posture shift noted in reporting. Iranian regime intact; nuclear issues unresolved.
- Proxies: Houthi advances in Yemen (Mokha, Perim/Hanish, coastal control) creating second-front risk at Bab el-Mandeb; intermittent strikes on Saudi targets/pipeline. Hezbollah activity lower but present in Lebanon context.
- Force posture: US CENTCOM active with escorts, blockade elements on Iranian ports/traffic, periodic “mow the lawn” strikes. Iran asserts control/claims seizures.

Sources conflict on exact transit volumes (US optimistic claims vs. tracker floors); independent AIS/satellite data weighted more heavily for commercial normality. Evidence base is solid on disruption persistence, thinner on exact daily flows due to dark sailing.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant historical classes (adjusted for modern precision weapons, insurance markets, AIS, and energy interdependence):

1. **Iran-Iraq Tanker War (1980–88)**: Prolonged attacks on shipping; insurance spiked; traffic reduced but never fully stopped; normalization only after ceasefire + political settlement (years). Partial stabilization lagged major pauses by months–years. Breaks down: no modern global LNG/oil market tightness or US direct large-scale intervention.
2. **2019 Gulf tanker attacks / Iranian seizures + US response**: Short sharp disruptions; insurance rose; traffic recovered within weeks–months after de-escalation and escorts. Breaks down: limited scope, no full war or nuclear overlay.
3. **Houthi Red Sea campaign (2023–25+)**: Persistent low-intensity attacks; major rerouting around Africa; insurance elevated for extended periods; partial recovery only with sustained pressure/ceasefire elements, incomplete even years later. Highly relevant for proxy persistence and insurance stickiness.
4. **1980s–90s Gulf War aftermath / Kuwait liberation shipping recovery**: Rapid military victory allowed faster commercial return once mines cleared and security guaranteed (weeks–months for traffic, longer for full insurance/market confidence). Breaks down: clear winner, occupation/enforcement possible.
5. **Suez closures (1956, 1967)**: Multi-year disruptions until political resolution; traffic and insurance lagged political deals. Relevant for chokepoint psychology.
6. **Post-ceasefire Lebanon/Gaza maritime or energy corridor recoveries**: Pauses do not equal commercial normalization; residual risk keeps premiums and behavior elevated for months–years.

Base-rate prior (pre-current-evidence adjustment, probability of full multi-domain functional normality):  
1 week: <1–2%  
1 month: 5–10%  
3 months: 15–25%  
6 months: 30–40%  
1 year: 45–55%  
18 months: 55–65%  

These reflect that chokepoint wars and proxy campaigns rarely normalize commercially on short horizons; full recovery typically requires durable political settlement + demonstrated security, not just pauses. Modern insurance and AIS make residual risk more visible and sticky.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

Ranked: (1) Durable mediated political settlement covering Hormuz administration, sanctions/blockade, and nuclear file; (2) Sustained multi-week absence of attacks enabling insurance repricing and owner confidence; (3) Demonstrated safe transit under escorts leading to organic commercial return; (4) Houthi restraint or effective containment; (5) Post-midterm US policy clarity favoring deal over further kinetic pressure; (6) Iranian economic exhaustion forcing compromise.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

Ranked: (1) Absence of durable settlement + residual Iranian capacity/will to disrupt; (2) Sticky insurance and owner risk aversion (even after pauses); (3) Houthi second-front leverage at Bab el-Mandeb linking Gulf and Red Sea risk; (4) Nuclear reconstitution or leadership targeting cycles as escalation triggers; (5) US domestic politics (midterms) incentivizing continued pressure; (6) Path dependence of dark shipping/STS/pipeline workarounds becoming semi-permanent; (7) Proxy activation risks.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued intermittent kinetic activity and diplomatic contact through midterms; possible limited post-election deal or enforced pause that improves volumes via escorts/workarounds but leaves insurance elevated and pure commercial transit incomplete for many months. Partial recovery of export volumes occurs earlier than full normality. Houthi front remains a drag. Functional multi-domain normality most probable in the 12–18 month window if a durable arrangement holds; otherwise a prolonged elevated-risk equilibrium.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Strongest case: Rapid post-rejection or post-midterm mediated deal that includes verified demining, blockade lift, transit protocol with Oman, sanctions relief, and effective Houthi restraint. Sustained 4–6 week attack-free period triggers insurance capacity return and owner confidence; US posture shifts to monitoring. Transit metrics recover to 80%+ within 2–3 months; premiums fall sharply. Probability mass shifts upward if early verified implementation data appears.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Strongest case: No durable deal; residual Iranian disruption capacity + Houthi Bab el-Mandeb leverage create linked dual-chokepoint risk; insurance remains elevated; dark/STS methods become semi-permanent; periodic “mow the lawn” cycles continue; nuclear issues provide escalation triggers. Commercial normality delayed beyond 18 months into a “new normal” of higher baseline risk and costs.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Possible overestimation of normalization: Underweighting insurance/owner stickiness and proxy autonomy (Houthis may not fully follow Iranian restraint); underweighting domestic US political incentives for continued pressure; optimistic on Iranian willingness to relinquish Hormuz leverage.  

Possible underestimation: Overweighting current low transit numbers while underweighting adaptive workarounds and US escort capacity; underestimating Iranian economic pressure forcing earlier compromise; underestimating speed of insurance repricing once a credible multi-week pause is demonstrated.  

Load-bearing assumptions: That commercial normality requires political settlement more than pure military suppression; that Houthi activity remains linked; that insurance markets are slow to reprice residual risk.
