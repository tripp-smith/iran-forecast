**1. Forecast Question**  
Probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War (onset ~28 February 2026) by horizons of 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months from 25 September 2026.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
All of the following must hold concurrently and sustainably (not a temporary pause or partial recovery):

- Direct military activity: No sustained direct strikes by major belligerents (US/coalition/Israel vs. Iran) on each other’s territory, forces, or critical infrastructure in/around the Gulf for a sustained period; no active large-scale maritime interdiction campaign by either side targeting the other’s commercial or military assets as primary policy.
- Proxy activity: Material reduction in proxy attacks (Houthis and others) on commercial shipping, energy infrastructure, or Gulf state assets linked to the main conflict; no sustained proxy campaign that keeps insurance or routing elevated.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: AIS-visible commercial transit volumes recovering to ≥70–80% of pre-war baseline (~85–125 large commercial vessels/day depending on source); absence of repeated vessel strikes, mining threats, or enforced alternate corridors that force dark sailing or major diversions; JMIC or equivalent threat rating not at “Severe.”
- Commercial transit levels: Sustained recovery in energy and non-energy cargo movements through Hormuz without systemic backlog of hundreds of vessels or widespread carrier suspensions/reroutes via Cape.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums for Hormuz transits returning to ≤0.5–1% of hull value (or clear multi-week trend toward pre-war ~0.1–0.25% levels) with broad market capacity and removal or narrowing of Joint War Committee listed-area designations; cargo war-risk not remaining prohibitively elevated.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf (including Saudi, UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Iraq, and residual Iranian) seaborne oil/LNG exports recovering toward pre-war volumes without reliance on high-risk escorted or shadow fleets as the dominant mode; no sustained pipeline or terminal outages that keep effective export capacity materially impaired.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent (and related benchmarks) no longer carrying a large persistent Gulf/Hormuz risk premium relative to fundamentals; reduced day-to-day volatility driven by daily transit counts or attack reports; physical differentials and freight rates normalizing.
- Force posture of major actors: No major new forward deployments or escalatory force movements that signal imminent large-scale resumption; observable drawdown or stabilization of US/coalition maritime presence from peak wartime levels and Iranian coastal/anti-access posture from peak disruption mode.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new nuclear threshold events, major third-party interventions, or irreversible territorial/control claims over the strait that would reset commercial risk pricing.

A temporary ceasefire, partial reopening under escort, lower oil prices from demand destruction or non-Gulf supply, or a battlefield pause without commercial normalization does **not** meet the definition.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant classes (with caveats):

- **Tanker War (Iran-Iraq, 1980s, especially 1984–88 peak)**: Prolonged attacks on shipping in the Gulf/Hormuz approaches. Commercial traffic adapted (escorts, flags of convenience, higher premiums) but never fully normalized until the broader war ended. Partial adaptation occurred within months; functional commercial normality required years and the 1988 ceasefire. Breaks down because it was a bilateral conventional war without today’s precision weapons, globalized insurance markets, or LNG dependence.
- **2019 Gulf tanker attacks + Abqaiq/Khurais (Iran-linked)**: Short, sharp disruptions. Shipping and insurance normalized within weeks to a few months once attacks stopped and deterrence held. Suggests rapid partial recovery possible if kinetic activity ceases cleanly. Breaks down: limited scale, no sustained blockade or multi-month war.
- **Red Sea/Houthi campaign (2023–ongoing, intensified 2024–25)**: Sustained proxy attacks on shipping. Major carriers rerouted for many months; insurance elevated; partial traffic recovery only with sustained naval presence and reduced attack success. Full commercial normality still incomplete years later in some metrics. Highly relevant for proxy/insurance lag; differs in scale (Hormuz is higher-value energy artery) and state involvement.
- **Suez closures (1956, 1967–75)**: Full closures lasted months to years; traffic recovered post-reopening but with permanent route diversification and political residual risk. Suggests multi-month lag even after political resolution.
- **Post-Gulf War I (1991) and limited Iran-Israel exchanges (e.g., 2024)**: Relatively rapid shipping/energy market recovery once major combat ended, but residual insurance and posture effects lingered. Limited by lower duration/intensity than current case.
- **Broader chokepoint disruptions (e.g., Black Sea grain after 2022)**: Partial corridors and insurance workarounds within months; full pre-disruption normality often took 1–2+ years or never fully returned under continued risk.

Base-rate prior (before case-specific updating; approximate probabilities of meeting a demanding multi-domain normality standard):  
- 1 week: <5%  
- 1 month: 5–15%  
- 3 months: 15–30%  
- 6 months: 25–45%  
- 1 year: 40–60%  
- 18 months: 50–70%  

These reflect historical lags between kinetic pauses and commercial/insurance normalization, plus the frequency of incomplete or reversed stabilizations in Gulf/proxy cases.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**  
Ranked roughly by importance: (1) Durable political/military settlement or sustained de facto pause that removes the blockade and Iranian interdiction threat; (2) Sustained absence of vessel attacks allowing insurance repricing and carrier return; (3) Demonstrated safe transit volume recovery that rebuilds commercial confidence; (4) Gulf state mediation and hedging that reduces secondary fronts; (5) Market adaptation (alternative routes, non-Gulf supply) that lowers the cost of residual risk.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**  
Ranked: (1) Core unresolved issues (blockade sequencing, nuclear file, Iranian control claims over the strait); (2) Insurance and commercial lag even after kinetic pauses (historical pattern); (3) Proxy autonomy and secondary fronts (Houthis, others); (4) Mutual incentives to maintain pressure (Iran leverage via strait; US/coalition sanctions/blockade leverage); (5) Residual force posture and risk of miscalculation or spoilers; (6) Physical damage, backlog, and crew/owner risk aversion.

**8. Decomposed Sub-Forecasts** (approximate probabilities of meaningful progress on each component by horizon; these are building blocks, not mechanical averages)

- Major reduction in direct strikes: 1w ~40–50%; 1m ~55–65%; 3m ~65–75%; 6m+ higher but with relapse risk.
- Sustained safe commercial transit (volume + security): 1w <10%; 1m ~15–25%; 3m ~30–45%; 6m ~45–60%; 1y ~55–70%; 18m ~60–75%.
- Meaningful insurance normalization: lags transit security; 1w/1m very low; 3m ~20–35%; 6m ~40–55%; longer higher but sticky.
- Oil/LNG export recovery (volume + continuity): partially decoupled via alternatives but still constrained; similar lag to transit, somewhat faster for non-Iranian Gulf volumes.
- Proxy restraint: lower and more variable; meaningful sustained restraint lower probability than direct-strike pause.
- Force drawdown / no new major escalation trigger: moderate near-term (pause exists), declining certainty at longer horizons without political settlement.

Gating factors are primarily shipping security + insurance + absence of new triggers; military pause alone is insufficient.

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Continued diplomatic engagement with intermittent progress and setbacks. Military activity remains lower than peak but with residual vessel incidents and proxy actions. Gradual, uneven recovery in non-Iranian Gulf exports and some escorted or alternative-route traffic. Insurance and full commercial confidence lag. Partial normalization of volumes by 3–6 months is plausible under continued pause; full multi-domain functional normality more likely only if a durable political arrangement solidifies in the 6–18 month window. Residual elevated risk persists longer.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Rapid acceptance of a sequenced deal (ceasefire + phased reopening + talks), sustained incident-free transit for weeks, insurance capacity floods back, carriers resume, and proxies stand down. Could push 3-month probability toward 45–50% and 6-month toward 60%+. Requires both sides prioritizing economic relief over leverage.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Talks stall or produce only temporary pauses; intermittent vessel attacks and proxy campaigns continue; insurance stays elevated; carriers maintain heavy diversions; secondary fronts expand. Normalization probabilities compress across horizons (e.g., 1-year closer to 35–40%). Supported by mutual leverage incentives and historical proxy stickiness.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Possible overestimation of normalization: The definition is deliberately strict; commercial and insurance markets have shown multi-month lags even after kinetic pauses (Red Sea precedent); Iranian incentives to retain strait leverage and proxy autonomy may prevent clean resolution; US domestic/political timelines could produce pressure for incomplete deals that fail.  
Possible underestimation: Economic pain (oil, insurance, Gulf state costs, Iranian isolation) is high and rising; adaptation (pipelines, non-Gulf supply, dark fleets) already occurring; a single durable deal could unlock faster commercial recovery than base rates imply because global energy demand and Asian buyers create strong pull.  
Load-bearing assumptions: That commercial normality requires more than a military pause; that proxy restraint is harder than direct-strike restraint; that insurance responds with lag to observed security rather than announcements.
