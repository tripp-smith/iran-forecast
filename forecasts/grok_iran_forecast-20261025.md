**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each of these horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality requires simultaneous, sustained (minimum 30 consecutive days of observation for the shorter horizons; longer for the extended ones) multi-domain conditions. It is not a ceasefire, not lower oil prices alone, and not a battlefield pause while commercial risk remains elevated.

- **Direct military activity**: No sustained large-scale US/Israeli strikes on Iranian territory or major Iranian counterstrikes on US/Gulf state assets or shipping. Minor isolated incidents do not disqualify if they do not restart the prior tempo.
- **Proxy activity**: Houthi attacks on Saudi energy, infrastructure, airports, or shipping reduced to sporadic/low levels (well below current multi-incident weekly rates). No major new or expanded activation of Iraqi, Lebanese, or other proxies that materially affects Gulf shipping or energy.
- **Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters**: AIS-visible plus confirmed total commercial transits return to ≥70% of pre-crisis levels (pre-crisis roughly 80–90 commercial vessels per day). Attack frequency falls below roughly two significant incidents per week with no sinkings. Mine threats are cleared or demonstrably negligible.
- **Commercial transit levels**: Sustained high volumes of ordinary commercial traffic without primary reliance on military escorts or widespread “dark” (AIS-off) operations for the majority of vessels.
- **War-risk insurance conditions**: Additional war-risk premiums fall below 1% of hull value for routine tankers/LNG carriers (versus current 6–10%), with broad market availability and no requirement for large-scale government backstops for ordinary voyages.
- **Oil and LNG export continuity**: Non-Iranian Gulf crude exports (via Hormuz plus bypass pipelines) at or above pre-war levels on a sustained basis. Qatar/UAE LNG flows approach pre-war volumes. Iranian seaborne exports are not required for this definition.
- **Energy market stress and volatility**: Brent not persistently elevated solely by Hormuz-specific risk premia (sustained movement toward a range consistent with non-war fundamentals). Volatility metrics normalize.
- **Force posture of major actors**: US naval blockade of Iranian ports lifted or converted to routine presence. Major US carrier/strike assets reduced from crisis levels. Iranian naval forces not postured for active interdiction of non-Iranian traffic.
- **Absence of major new escalation triggers**: No new leadership targeting, nuclear-related provocations that restart large-scale strikes, or territorial seizures that reopen high-intensity fighting.

Temporary workarounds (US-facilitated corridors, pipeline bypasses, elevated insurance that still allows some traffic) do not constitute normality.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot** (as of ~25 October 2026)

The war began on 28 February 2026 with joint US-Israeli strikes (Operation Epic Fury / Roaring Lion) that killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei. Iran retaliated with missiles and drones and restricted the Strait of Hormuz. An April pause and the June Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding produced a temporary lift of the US blockade and a partial traffic rebound; the arrangement collapsed in July, the blockade was reimposed on 14 July, and the MOU expired in mid-August.

As of late October (roughly Day 240 of the Hormuz crisis):
- US blockade of Iranian ports remains active (CENTCOM reports prevention of ~$25 billion in Iranian oil exports; no meaningful Iranian seaborne crude loadings for months).
- US forces facilitate “dark” and escorted transits for non-Iranian traffic via a southern corridor near Oman. AIS-visible commercial traffic is ~7% of pre-crisis (~5–6 vessels/day versus ~85). Actual facilitated volumes are higher (reports of dozens per day or hundreds in some weekly windows).
- Iranian/IRGC attacks on commercial shipping reached their highest monthly total of the war in October (UKMTO/IMO and maritime trackers report 20–28+ incidents; some ships sunk). Claims of mines versus projectiles are disputed; US statements treat many IRGC claims as exaggerated or false.
- Non-Iranian Gulf crude flows have recovered to or above pre-war levels excluding Iran via a combination of Hormuz (often 70–80+% of pre-war in recent weeks) plus increased pipeline bypasses (Saudi East-West and UAE routes now carrying a much larger share). LNG remains more constrained. Brent has traded in the $100–115 range recently (pre-war ~$70).
- War-risk premiums for Gulf tanker voyages stand at 6–10% of hull value (versus ~0.25% pre-crisis), equating to multi-million-dollar costs per VLCC transit. Coverage is available but commercially prohibitive for ordinary traffic.
- Houthi attacks on Saudi airports, energy facilities, and related targets continue at elevated levels, with reports of interceptor shortages and ground fighting in Yemen.
- Indirect talks continue via mediators; Iranian conditions for reopening Hormuz remain extensive (end blockade, sanctions relief, force withdrawals, compensation). Trump has publicly pledged no strikes on Iran before the 3 November US midterms. US naval presence remains large.

Confirmed facts are distinguished from claims: attack counts and traffic data come from UKMTO, Lloyd’s List, Kpler/Vortexa-type trackers, IMO, and CENTCOM statements; Iranian claims of control or specific mine hits are frequently contested by US reporting.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

- **Tanker War (Iran–Iraq, ~1984–1988)**: Relevant as the closest sustained maritime campaign against Gulf shipping with US escort operations (Earnest Will from 1987). Traffic never fully stopped; insurance rates rose sharply (peaks in the several-percent range) and declined after the August 1988 ceasefire, with more complete normalization over the following 1–2 years. Breakdown: current conflict involves direct US–Iran state-on-state fighting, a formal blockade, drones/missiles at higher volume, and proxy dimensions absent or weaker then. Suggests commercial stabilization lags military intensity reduction by months, with full insurance and force normalization often taking 6–18 months after intensity drops.
- **Red Sea / Houthi disruptions (2023–2025)**: Relevant for asymmetric attacks raising insurance and diverting traffic without full physical closure. Traffic remained depressed for over a year in many segments; insurance stayed elevated until attack frequency fell materially. Partial returns occurred only after sustained quiet periods. Breakdown: Hormuz has far less viable bypass capacity than the Cape route. Suggests multi-month to multi-quarter lags even after attacks decline.
- **Brief 2019 Gulf tanker incidents and 2019–2020 US–Iran tensions**: Relevant for short, limited disruptions resolved by diplomacy or restraint within weeks to a few months. Breakdown: scale and duration of the 2026 war are larger.
- **1991 Gulf War oil recovery**: Relevant for rapid export rebound once military conditions stabilized, aided by alternative routes. Breakdown: different political and military end-state.
- **Historical chokepoint physical closures (e.g., Suez)**: Physical clearance can enable recovery in weeks once the obstacle is removed, but risk perception and insurance lag.

**Base-rate prior** (before current-case updates, reflecting typical lags in comparable chokepoint/maritime war cases):  
1 week ~5%; 1 month ~10–12%; 3 months ~25%; 6 months ~40%; 1 year ~55–60%; 18 months ~70–75%.  
These embed the observed pattern that commercial and insurance normalization trail military de-escalation by 3–12+ months and that low-level residual risk can persist.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

1. Physical oil-flow resilience via US-protected corridor + pipeline bypasses (reduces immediate economic pressure for a deal).  
2. High cumulative cost of the blockade to Iran and demonstrated US capacity to sustain it.  
3. Mutual interest in avoiding full resumption of large-scale strikes (US domestic politics; Iranian reconstruction needs).  
4. Historical pattern that sustained quiet produces insurance and traffic recovery within quarters.  
5. Ongoing (if stalled) mediation channels.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

1. Iranian use of shipping attacks as leverage while the blockade continues (current tempo highest of the war).  
2. Insurance pricing stickiness and commercial aversion even after partial quiet (Red Sea and Tanker War precedents).  
3. Active Houthi campaign against Saudi targets and ground fighting in Yemen.  
4. Trust deficit, expired MOU, and expansive Iranian conditions.  
5. Elevated US force posture and post-midterm strike risk.  
6. Regime incentives under Mojtaba Khamenei to demonstrate resistance capability.

Ranked importance: shipping-attack tempo and insurance pricing are the tightest short-term gates; proxy and force-posture issues dominate medium-to-long-term risk.

**9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon**

**1 Week**  
• Probability: 3%  
• Base-rate anchor: ~5%  
• Current-evidence adjustment: Down (peak October attacks, elevated insurance, active blockade and Houthi activity).  
• Core rationale: Current tempo of shipping attacks and insurance pricing make multi-domain normality impossible in one week.  
• Main gating factor: Shipping security and insurance.  
• What would raise this forecast materially: Immediate, verified halt to all shipping attacks plus public de-escalation commitments.  
• What would lower this forecast materially: Additional sinkings or resumption of large-scale strikes.  
• Confidence in this estimate: High

**1 Month**  
• Probability: 8%  
• Base-rate anchor: ~10–12%  
• Current-evidence adjustment: Down (attacks still elevated; midterm politics constrain but do not resolve).  
• Core rationale: One month is insufficient for insurance markets and commercial operators to treat the corridor as reliable even if attacks drop sharply.  
• Main gating factor: Insurance normalization and sustained safe transit.  
• What would raise this forecast materially: Documented multi-week drop in attacks plus visible insurance quotes falling toward 2%.  
• What would lower this forecast materially: Post-midterm strike resumption or Houthi intensification.  
• Confidence in this estimate: High

• Base-rate anchor: ~25%  
• Current-evidence adjustment: Down (insurance stickiness and proxy activity).  
• Core rationale: Possible if a durable quiet period begins immediately after midterms and holds, but insurance and commercial return typically lag by additional weeks to months.  
• Main gating factor: Transition from escorted/dark traffic to ordinary commercial volumes with falling premiums.  
• What would raise this forecast materially: Formal or de facto ceasefire holding through January with attack rates near zero.  
• What would lower this forecast materially: Continued or renewed high-frequency attacks.

• Main gating factor: Insurance falling below ~2% and force posture beginning to ease.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Attacks remain elevated through the US midterms. A period of relative military quiet follows, possibly with continued low-level Iranian shipping harassment and Houthi activity. US-facilitated corridors and pipeline bypasses keep non-Iranian oil volumes near pre-war levels. Insurance declines only gradually. Full multi-domain normality (ordinary traffic, sub-1% insurance, reduced force posture, proxy restraint) is more likely in the 9–18 month window than earlier, contingent on no major post-election escalation.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Post-midterm negotiations produce a limited, holding arrangement that includes attack halt, partial blockade relief, and proxy de-escalation. Insurance markets reprice within 6–8 weeks of verified quiet. Ordinary commercial traffic returns above 70% by month 4–5. Probability mass shifts upward by 15–25 points at the 6-month and 1-year horizons.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Iran maintains intermittent shipping attacks as leverage; Houthis continue pressure on Saudi targets; post-election US strikes resume or the blockade hardens. Insurance remains structurally elevated; commercial operators treat Hormuz as permanently higher-risk. Normality probabilities fall 15–20 points at the 1-year and 18-month horizons, with functional recovery extending beyond 18 months.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Strongest case for faster normalization: Oil physical flows have already recovered substantially, reducing the economic cost of delay and increasing the chance that both sides accept a messy, incomplete status quo that markets eventually treat as normal enough. Insurance can reprice faster than historical averages if attack data improve sharply.  
Strongest case for prolonged instability: The current attack tempo demonstrates Iranian capability and willingness eight months in; insurance losses already exceed $2 billion range; proxies have independent incentives; political trust is lower than in the Tanker War.  
Where the forecast may overestimate normalization: Residual “acceptable” risk pricing and partial dark-traffic norms may become semi-permanent, so the operational definition is never fully met even if volumes are high.  
Where it may underestimate: A sharp post-election political breakthrough or Iranian internal constraints could produce faster quiet than base rates imply.  
Load-bearing assumptions: Attack tempo is responsive to US restraint/blockade status; insurance lags but does not freeze; proxies can be influenced by Iran.
