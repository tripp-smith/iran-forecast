**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each of these horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

“Return to functional normality” requires simultaneous satisfaction of these observable, multi-domain conditions (all must hold concurrently; temporary pauses or partial recoveries do not count):

- Direct military activity: No reciprocal kinetic strikes between the United States/coalition and Iran (or Iranian forces) for a sustained period of at least 14 consecutive days, with no active major combat operations in the Gulf theater.
- Proxy activity: No significant proxy attacks (Houthis, Iraqi militias, or others) on commercial shipping, energy infrastructure, or Gulf state/partner assets that materially disrupt operations.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Sustained commercial transit without attacks, boardings for control purposes, mine incidents, or forced diversion to Iranian-designated corridors under threat. Safe passage available via both northern and southern routes without Iranian veto or tolls.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits through Hormuz averaging ≥70–80% of the pre-war baseline (~100–110+ of the historical ~130 vessels/day), sustained over at least two weeks, including meaningful Western/allied tanker and LNG participation (not dominated by shadow-fleet/Iranian/Chinese traffic).
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums for Hormuz transit ≤1.5–2% of vessel value (down from current elevated levels of ~7.5–10%), with broad market capacity restored and no widespread coverage withdrawals or voyage-by-voyage refusals.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Aggregate crude + products + LNG flows from the Gulf (Saudi, UAE, Kuwait, Qatar, Iraq, and residual Iranian) at ≥80–85% of pre-war volumes (~16–18+ mb/d equivalent), without major production curtailments due to security or logistics.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent crude trading in a range consistent with non-crisis fundamentals (no persistent $20–40/bbl geopolitical risk premium embedded relative to pre-war levels), with reduced day-to-day volatility driven by Hormuz headlines.
- Force posture of major actors: Material drawdown or repositioning of surge forces (US carrier/strike groups, additional air assets, Iranian coastal/anti-ship batteries and small boats) away from wartime alert levels; no ongoing naval blockade of Iranian ports.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new closure declarations, large-scale mining, attacks on desalination/power plants, or expansion to Bab el-Mandeb that reintroduce systemic risk.

A temporary ceasefire, oil-price dip from a pause, or battlefield lull does not meet the definition. Commercial confidence, insurance pricing, and transit volume are gating factors that lag kinetic pauses.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot (as of 28 July 2026)**

War began ~28 February 2026 with US-Israeli strikes. A 17 June MOU created a 60-day window to reopen Hormuz and negotiate; it collapsed ~7–8 July after Iranian attacks on commercial vessels using non-Iranian routes. US conducted ~13 consecutive nights of strikes (coastal radars, anti-ship missiles, small boats, deeper infrastructure). Iran retaliated against US facilities and Gulf states (Bahrain, Kuwait, etc.) and activated proxies (Houthi attacks on Saudi facilities/Red Sea shipping).

As of 26–28 July: Mutual kinetic pause (US halted strikes; Iran mirrored). Mediators (Oman, Pakistan, Qatar) active; talks on Hormuz transit reported as “useful.” Iran continues to claim control via its “Persian Gulf Strait Authority,” turns back vessels on non-approved routes, and maintains mine threats. Shipping remains severely depressed: daily transits often 3–10 vessels (vs. pre-war ~130), tanker traffic near two-month lows, dominated by certain flags; overall flows ~15% of pre-war in recent windows. US naval blockade of Iranian ports remains in effect. War-risk premiums elevated (7.5–10% of hull value or multiples of 8× peacetime). Brent recently traded ~$87–90 after falling from >$100 on the pause (still elevated vs. pre-war). Proxies active; force postures high; no commercial normalization. Evidence base is solid on kinetic events and traffic data from multiple trackers (Kpler, Windward, MarineTraffic, JMIC, UKMTO) but thinner on exact private insurance quotes and internal Iranian decision-making.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant historical cases (3–6):

1. **Tanker War (Iran-Iraq, 1984–88)**: Sustained attacks on shipping in Gulf/Hormuz. Partial commercial adaptation occurred within months via escorts and route changes, but full normalization required war end + years. Timeline suggestion: 6–18+ months post-major de-escalation for functional commercial recovery. Breaks down: bilateral state war, less proxy complexity, no modern insurance markets or global just-in-time energy system.
2. **2019 Gulf tanker attacks / Hormuz tensions (Iranian seizures, limpet mines)**: Short-duration spikes. Shipping and insurance recovered in weeks to 1–3 months once kinetic activity ceased and deterrence restored. Breaks down: limited scale, no full war, rapid US/European naval response.
3. **2023–25 Red Sea / Houthi crisis (Bab el-Mandeb)**: Prolonged proxy disruption of a chokepoint. Traffic and insurance only partially recovered after months of escorts + strikes; full normalization incomplete even after reduced attacks. Timeline: 6–12+ months for meaningful recovery, longer for full. Relevant for proxy persistence and insurance lag; breaks down: alternative routes more available than for Hormuz.
4. **1990–91 Gulf War / Kuwait liberation aftermath**: Rapid military victory followed by slower commercial and insurance normalization (months). Breaks down: clear victor, coalition occupation phase.
5. **Post-ceasefire phases in prior Iran-related maritime crises (e.g., 1988 ceasefire effects)**: Kinetic pause precedes commercial recovery by weeks to months; insurance and volume lag further.
6. **Broader chokepoint disruptions (Suez 2021 blockage, various piracy eras)**: Physical clearance or threat reduction yields volume recovery in days–weeks, but insurance/market confidence in 1–3 months when geopolitical.

Base-rate prior (before current-evidence update; probability of meeting the full operational definition):
- 1 week: ~2–5%
- 1 month: ~8–15%
- 3 months: ~20–30%
- 6 months: ~35–45%
- 1 year: ~50–60%
- 18 months: ~60–70%

These reflect that kinetic pauses are common and relatively fast, but multi-domain commercial + insurance + proxy + posture normalization is rarer and slower in Gulf/chokepoint reference classes.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

Ranked by importance:
1. Sustained mutual kinetic restraint + successful mediated deal on free transit (no Iranian veto/tolls).
2. Demonstrated safe multi-route transit restoring insurer and charterer confidence.
3. De-escalation of proxy activity and force-posture adjustments.
4. Market adaptation (rerouting, SPR releases, demand response) reducing urgency of full Hormuz recovery.
5. Domestic political incentives in US and Iran for a durable off-ramp.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

Ranked by importance:
1. Iranian strategic priority of asserting long-term Hormuz control as war “achievement.”
2. Fragility of pauses (prior MOU collapsed quickly on shipping disputes).
3. Insurance and commercial lag: premiums and volume recover slowly even after kinetic calm.
4. Proxy autonomy and horizontal escalation options (Houthis, Iraqi groups).
5. High remaining force postures and munitions/stockpile dynamics that favor re-escalation if talks stall.
6. Second-order effects: Asian buyer caution, Gulf state political sensitivity, secondary sanctions.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Fragile pause holds for days to a few weeks. Mediated technical talks produce limited transit arrangements (possibly dual corridors with monitoring). Traffic recovers partially (30–50% of baseline) but remains security-constrained and insurance-expensive. Proxies continue intermittent activity. Oil prices ease but retain a risk premium. Full multi-domain normality is delayed into the 6–18 month window, with intermittent setbacks. Force postures remain elevated longer than kinetic activity.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Strongest path: Rapid, verified free-transit protocol (no Iranian veto/tolls), demining confirmation, 3–4 weeks of high-volume safe passages including Western fleets, insurer re-entry driving premiums <2%, simultaneous proxy de-escalation, and visible US/Iran force posture reductions. Probability of this path materializing within 3 months is low (~15–20% conditional).

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Strongest path: Talks stall on control issues; Iran resumes selective interdiction or mining; proxies intensify on Red Sea/Saudi targets; insurance remains prohibitive; traffic stays structurally depressed; periodic kinetic exchanges resume. This produces chronic partial disruption rather than full closure, keeping the operational definition unmet for 12–18+ months. Conditional probability elevated given current Iranian behavior and prior MOU failure.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Overestimation risk: The forecast may under-weight rapid market adaptation and the economic pressure on both sides for volume recovery; base rates from shorter 2019-style episodes could apply more than prolonged Tanker War analogies if US degradation of Iranian coastal forces is greater than assessed. Underestimation risk: Iranian leadership may treat Hormuz control as non-negotiable post-war “victory,” making even long-horizon probabilities too high; proxy residual capacity and insurance structural repricing (seen in Red Sea) could keep the definition unmet beyond 18 months. Load-bearing assumptions: that commercial volume + insurance are true gating factors (not just kinetic pause) and that Iranian incentives can be shifted by cost. Evidence that would falsify: sustained free multi-route traffic at >80% volume with premiums <2% within 1–2 months, or conversely, formal Iranian Strait Authority recognition by major shippers/insurers.
