**1. Forecast Question**  
Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by horizons of 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months (from 29 July 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
“Return to functional normality” requires simultaneous satisfaction of all of the following observable conditions for a sustained period (minimum 14 consecutive days, except where noted):

- Direct military activity: No kinetic exchanges (airstrikes, missile/drone attacks, naval engagements) between the US/coalition and Iran inside or immediately adjacent to the Persian Gulf/Strait of Hormuz/Gulf of Oman, and no major Iranian attacks on Gulf Arab energy or military infrastructure.
- Proxy activity: No significant attacks by Iranian-aligned groups (Houthis, Iraqi militias, etc.) on commercial shipping in the Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb or on Gulf energy facilities that materially disrupt flows or raise risk premiums.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: No successful or attempted attacks on commercial vessels; no active mining threats requiring ongoing clearance operations; free navigation without mandatory Iranian coordination, tolls, or route restrictions enforced by force.
- Commercial transit levels: Average daily vessel transits through the Strait of Hormuz recover to ≥80% of the pre-war baseline (~110–130 vessels/day), sustained for at least 14 days, with both inbound and outbound traffic and a mix of tankers, LNG carriers, and dry cargo.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums for Hormuz transits fall to ≤1% of hull value (or ≤4× pre-war baseline of ~0.25%) and remain available from major underwriters without widespread exclusions or cancellations.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf crude + condensate + products + LNG export loadings recover to ≥85% of pre-war volumes, with Iranian exports no longer under active naval blockade and non-Iranian Gulf producers able to load and sail without systematic delays.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent crude trades with a geopolitical risk premium of ≤$5–7/bbl relative to a no-Gulf-risk counterfactual, and 30-day realized volatility is not materially elevated solely due to Hormuz risk.
- Force posture of major actors: No sustained elevated US/coalition combat operations tempo focused on Iranian coastal/anti-ship capabilities; Iranian IRGC Navy posture does not include routine interdiction or escort demands that deter commercial traffic.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new major attacks on critical infrastructure, no expansion to new theaters that re-close the strait, and no collapse of any interim diplomatic arrangement that immediately restarts high-intensity kinetic activity.

A temporary ceasefire, partial traffic rebound, or oil-price decline alone does not satisfy the definition. Commercial and insurance normalization lag military pauses.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot (as of 29 July 2026)**  
The war began late February 2026. A 17 June Memorandum of Understanding produced a partial, fragile reopening. Traffic recovered toward roughly half of pre-war levels in late June before collapsing again after Iranian attacks on commercial vessels in early July and subsequent US retaliatory strikes (including large packages of 80+ targets). President Trump declared the ceasefire “over”; the US reinstated a naval blockade of Iranian ports and revoked oil-sale licenses.  

As of late July, a short kinetic pause (several days) is in effect amid Omani mediation. Iran continues to claim control of the strait, rejects proposals that dilute that control, and has used force and mine threats to deter non-Iranian-approved routes. Daily transits remain extremely low (single digits to low teens in recent reporting from Kpler/Windward vs. ~130 pre-war). Oil prices have been volatile ($85–100+ range recently; around mid-$80s to high-$80s in the most recent sessions). War-risk premiums remain multiples of peacetime levels. Proxy activity (Houthis) continues. Iranian internal divisions exist between harder-line IRGC elements and more negotiation-oriented factions, but both prioritize strait leverage. **4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant historical cases (3–6):

1. Iran–Iraq Tanker War (1980–88, especially 1984–88 phase): Prolonged low-to-medium intensity attacks on shipping in the Gulf. Traffic adapted via escorts, insurance spikes, and rerouting but never fully normalized until the 1988 ceasefire and subsequent years of demining/stabilization. Functional commercial recovery took 1–3+ years after major fighting ended. Breaks down because the current war involves direct US–Iran high-intensity strikes and modern precision munitions/drones.
2. 2019–2020 Gulf tanker attacks / IRGC seizures and the 2019 Abqaiq attack: Short, sharp disruptions. Traffic and insurance normalized within weeks to a few months once kinetic activity stopped and deterrence was reasserted. Suggests faster recovery when the initiator retains residual deterrent capacity but faces strong external pressure. Breaks down due to far lower intensity and no prolonged blockade or multi-month closure.
3. Red Sea / Houthi campaign (late 2023–2025+): Sustained proxy disruption of a major chokepoint. Traffic collapsed, insurance spiked, and partial recovery occurred only after sustained naval response and (limited) Houthi restraint; full normalization remained incomplete for many operators even after intensity dropped. Timeline for partial functional recovery: 6–18 months of elevated risk. Highly relevant for insurance, shipping behavior, and proxy persistence; breaks down because Hormuz is more critical and Iran is a state actor with greater capabilities.
4. 1990–91 Gulf War and post-liberation Kuwait reconstruction / demining: Rapid military victory produced relatively fast commercial recovery (months), but residual mine threats and force posture issues lingered. Suggests faster path if one side achieves decisive military dominance. Breaks down because no decisive victory is evident here.
5. Suez Canal closures (1956, 1967–75): Extended closures produced multi-year commercial adaptation and slow return. Less relevant for intensity but useful for insurance and long-horizon commercial lag.

Base-rate prior (before case-specific evidence): Probability of meeting the full operational definition ≈ 1–3% at 1 week, 5–10% at 1 month, 15–25% at 3 months, 30–40% at 6 months, 45–55% at 1 year, 55–65% at 18 months. These reflect the historical lag between kinetic pauses and multi-domain commercial/insurance normalization in chokepoint conflicts involving state actors with residual capacity.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**  
Ranked: (1) Sustained kinetic pause that allows insurance markets and shipowners to regain confidence; (2) Diplomatic arrangement that provides Iran face-saving residual influence short of coercive control while guaranteeing free navigation; (3) Clear US/coalition commitment to protect traffic without indefinite high-tempo strikes; (4) Gulf Arab states’ willingness to facilitate routes and absorb residual risk; (5) Economic pressure on Iran from continued blockade and damage.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**  
Ranked: (1) Iranian regime (especially IRGC) view of strait control as core residual deterrent and revenue/leverage tool; (2) Mutual distrust and repeated collapse of interim arrangements; (3) Lag in insurance and commercial behavior even after kinetic activity drops; (4) Proxy activity and secondary fronts (Red Sea) that keep risk elevated; (5) Domestic political incentives on both sides that punish perceived concessions; (6) Residual mines, damaged infrastructure, and elevated force posture.

**8. Decomposed Sub-Forecasts**  
Approximate probabilities that each major component reaches a level consistent with the operational definition (by horizon):

- Major reduction in direct strikes: 1w 25%, 1m 40%, 3m 55%, 6m 65%, 1y 75%, 18m 80%

- No new major escalation trigger: 1w 40%, 1m 45%, 3m 50%, 6m 55%, 1y 55%, 18m 50% (longer horizons accumulate risk)

Transit, insurance, and absence of coercive Iranian control are the tightest gating factors. Military pause alone is insufficient.

**9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon**

**1 Week**  
- Probability: 2%  
- Base-rate anchor: 1–3%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight downward (active low traffic, unresolved control dispute, very recent pause only)  
- Core rationale: Impossible to achieve multi-domain commercial and insurance recovery in days. Even a perfect diplomatic breakthrough would leave traffic and premiums lagging.  
- Main gating factor: Commercial transit + insurance  
- What would raise this forecast materially: Immediate, verified free transit surge + major underwriter announcements of sharp premium cuts  
- What would lower this forecast materially: Any kinetic incident  
- Confidence in this estimate: High  

**1 Month**  
- Probability: 8%  
- Base-rate anchor: 5–10%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight downward  
- Core rationale: A durable pause and interim arrangement are possible, but historical chokepoint recoveries and current Iranian insistence on control make full multi-domain normality unlikely. Partial traffic rebound is more plausible than the full definition.  
- Main gating factor: Sustained safe transit without Iranian coercive conditions  
- What would raise: Verified multi-week traffic ≥80% of baseline + insurance market response  
- What would lower: Collapse of current pause or new ship attacks  
- Confidence: Medium-High  

**3 Months**

- Core rationale: Time allows for a more durable diplomatic package and some commercial adaptation, but the structural Iranian interest in strait leverage and insurance lag keep the probability modest.  
- Main gating factor: Combination of transit volumes and insurance normalization  
- What would raise: Binding arrangement with monitoring + sustained low kinetic activity  
- What would lower: Renewed high-tempo strikes or expansion of proxy campaign

- Main gating factor: Iranian willingness to accept non-coercive transit regime  
- What would raise: Clear degradation of Iranian anti-access capabilities or major internal political shift  
- What would lower: Entrenchment of dual-route or toll regime enforced by threat  
- Confidence: Medium  

**1 Year**

- Base-rate anchor: 55–65%

- What would raise: Formal multilateral transit regime with enforcement  
- What would lower: Protracted low-level conflict or successful Iranian institutionalization of control  
- Confidence: Medium-Low  

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Continued stop-start diplomacy and intermittent low-to-medium kinetic activity focused on the strait. Partial, uneven traffic recovery under contested conditions, with insurance remaining elevated for many months. Full multi-domain normality is delayed beyond the near term; a messy, higher-risk equilibrium emerges over 6–18 months rather than a clean return to 2025 conditions.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Decisive US military degradation of Iranian coastal and anti-ship systems combined with a face-saving diplomatic package (Omani or multilateral monitoring, limited Iranian “services” role without coercive enforcement) produces sustained free transit within weeks. Insurance markets respond within 1–2 months. Probability of this path is low but non-zero given current mediation and US strike capacity.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Iran successfully institutionalizes a degree of control or dual-route system enforced by residual threat; proxies remain active; repeated MOU collapses keep risk premiums high and traffic suppressed. Normalization fails to meet the full definition even at 18 months. Supported by Iranian public statements and the strategic value they assign to the strait.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
The forecast may overestimate near-term diplomatic prospects by under-weighting Iranian regime cohesion around the strait as a core interest. It may underestimate the speed of commercial adaptation if a credible US security guarantee is offered (shipowners and insurers can move faster than pure historical analogs suggest). Longer-horizon probabilities risk false precision; structural change (regime dynamics, capability shifts) could move outcomes more than the linear path implies. The operational definition is intentionally demanding; a looser definition focused only on “no major attacks + partial traffic” would produce higher probabilities.
