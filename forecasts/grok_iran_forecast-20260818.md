**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each of these horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

“Return to functional normality” is defined as the simultaneous satisfaction of all of the following observable conditions for a sustained period of at least 14 consecutive days (to exclude transient pauses):

- Direct military activity: No confirmed Iranian or U.S./Israeli kinetic strikes on commercial vessels, energy infrastructure, or opposing military assets in the Persian Gulf, Strait of Hormuz, or immediately adjacent waters (Gulf of Oman approaches). Residual air-defense or surveillance activity does not count as violation if it produces no kinetic effects.
- Proxy activity: No sustained Iranian-directed or coordinated proxy attacks (Houthis, Iraqi militias, Hezbollah maritime elements) that meaningfully degrade Gulf shipping or energy exports.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: UKMTO/JMIC risk assessments at “moderate” or lower; no active minefields in primary traffic separation schemes; no routine IRGC turn-back orders or boardings of non-Iranian commercial vessels.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits through Hormuz averaging ≥70% of pre-war baseline (~130 vessels/day, i.e., ≥90 vessels/day) for liquid and dry cargo combined, sustained, with non-Iranian and non-sanctioned traffic comprising a majority share.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums for standard VLCC/Suezmax Hormuz transits ≤1.0% of hull value (pre-war baseline ~0.25%; peak crisis 5–10%+; recent multiples still elevated at ~30× in some reporting) and widely available without special consortia or state guarantees; Joint War Committee listing status no longer driving prohibitive pricing.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Combined Gulf oil + LNG exports through Hormuz and alternative pipelines (Petraline, ADCOP, Goreh-Jask) ≥85% of pre-war volumes (~20+ mb/d oil equivalent + Qatar LNG), with no force-majeure declarations remaining in force by major producers (Saudi, UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Iraq).
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent crude 30-day realized volatility ≤ pre-war average levels (or ≤25% annualized); no sustained risk premium >$10–15/bbl attributable to Hormuz risk alone; Asian and European buyers restoring normal contract volumes without large-scale force majeure or rerouting premiums.
- Force posture of major actors: U.S. naval blockade of Iranian ports lifted or reduced to routine presence; no large-scale additional carrier strike group deployments or Iranian “offensive doctrine” shifts producing kinetic effects; Gulf states not under sustained missile/drone attack.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new high-casualty strikes on population centers, nuclear facilities, or third-party states that reset the conflict trajectory.

A temporary ceasefire, partial traffic recovery, or oil-price decline alone does **not** satisfy the definition. Commercial and insurance normalization must lag and confirm military de-escalation.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant reference classes (3–6):

1. **Iran-Iraq Tanker War (1984–1988)**: Hundreds of attacks on shipping; Hormuz never fully closed but traffic and insurance severely disrupted. Relevance: same geography, Iranian asymmetric maritime tactics. Breaks down: multi-year attrition war with different political endgame; no U.S. carrier presence or nuclear dimension. Suggests partial stabilization only after formal ceasefire + years of residual risk; functional commercial normality took 1–3+ years post-1988.

2. **2019 Gulf shipping crisis (May–Sep)**: Iranian attacks/seizures on tankers; elevated insurance; limited duration. Relevance: same actors and waterway. Breaks down: far lower intensity, no full-scale war or leadership decapitation. Suggests 3–6 months for traffic and premiums to normalize after de-escalation.

3. **Red Sea/Houthi crisis (2023–2025/26)**: Sustained proxy attacks; massive rerouting; insurance spikes. Relevance: Iranian-linked proxies, chokepoint risk pricing, commercial caution. Breaks down: alternative route (Cape) existed; no direct state-on-state war. Suggests even after attack pauses, full traffic/insurance recovery takes 3–12+ months; residual risk persists.

4. **Black Sea grain corridor (2022 onward)**: Mined waters + ongoing conflict; insurance and escort requirements. Relevance: war-risk lag after political agreements. Suggests weeks-to-months for initial traffic, longer for insurance confidence.

5. **Suez closures / major chokepoint disruptions (historical)**: Physical or risk-driven; recovery lags political reopenings by weeks–months due to backlog, insurance, and confidence.

Base-rate prior (pre-case-specific evidence), approximate cumulative probability of functional normality:

- 1 week: 1–3%
- 1 month: 5–10%
- 3 months: 15–25%
- 6 months: 30–45%
- 1 year: 50–65%
- 18 months: 60–75%

These reflect historical lag between military pauses and commercial/insurance normalization in high-stakes energy chokepoints.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

1. Mutual economic pain (oil prices, Gulf state losses, Iranian export constraints) creating incentives for deal.  
2. Existence of diplomatic channels (Oman, prior MoU framework, Qatar mediation history).  
3. Pipeline workarounds reducing absolute dependence on Hormuz for some volumes.  
4. U.S. political preference for lower energy prices (explicit statements).  
5. Historical pattern that prolonged high-intensity Gulf disruption eventually produces negotiated off-ramps.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

1. Core dispute over sovereignty/control of Hormuz and U.S. blockade remains unresolved post-MoU.  
2. Iranian internal hardline shift and “offensive doctrine” signaling.  
3. Insurance and commercial confidence lag far behind any kinetic pause (historical 1–2 quarters minimum).  
4. Proxy and secondary fronts (Lebanon, Red Sea) can re-ignite.  
5. Leadership changes and domestic Iranian politics raise commitment problems.  
6. Force posture (U.S. carriers, Iranian mine/drone capabilities) remains elevated.  
7. Backlog of stranded vessels and global supply-chain rewiring create inertia against rapid return.

Ranked importance: unresolved Hormuz sovereignty + insurance lag are the highest-weight gates; kinetic intensity is secondary once a credible sustained pause exists.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued low-level friction and intermittent vessel incidents through autumn 2026. Economic costs eventually force limited technical arrangements (Oman-mediated corridors, partial blockade relief) by early 2027, producing partial traffic recovery (40–60% of baseline) and elevated but declining insurance. Full multi-domain normality remains elusive until mid-to-late 2027 at earliest, with residual war-risk pricing and force posture elevated. Oil markets adapt via pipelines, strategic stocks, and demand destruction rather than full Hormuz restoration.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Sudden comprehensive deal within weeks (driven by U.S. energy-price priorities + Iranian economic collapse) + rapid demining + insurer rate collapse. Probability of this path is low (~10–15% unconditional) but would lift 3- and 6-month forecasts substantially.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Iranian offensive doctrine produces sustained attacks or mining; U.S. maintains or expands blockade; proxies re-ignite; no durable political floor. Traffic stays <20% of baseline for 12+ months; insurance remains prohibitive. This path is plausible (~25–35% weight) given current signaling.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Strongest case I am overestimating normalization: The definition is correctly demanding, but I may still under-weight Iranian domestic hardline consolidation and the difficulty of restoring insurer confidence after repeated MoU failures. Historical Tanker War residual risk lasted years.  

Strongest case I am underestimating: Mutual economic pain + U.S. domestic political incentives for cheap energy could produce faster political off-ramps than pure historical base rates imply; pipeline workarounds already blunt some pressure.

Load-bearing assumptions: (1) Insurance markets require sustained clean evidence before repricing; (2) Hormuz sovereignty remains a core Iranian red line; (3) No sudden regime-altering internal Iranian change. Evidence that would prove the forecast wrong: a verified comprehensive deal + 30 consecutive days of ≥90 vessels/day at ≤1% war-risk premiums.
