**1. Forecast Question**  
Probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War (initiated 28 February 2026) by horizons of 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months from 1 September 2026.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
“Return to functional normality” requires simultaneous, sustained satisfaction of all the following observable multi-domain criteria for at least 30 consecutive days (or the full remaining horizon if shorter). Temporary ceasefires, partial reopenings, lower spot prices alone, or battlefield pauses do not qualify.

- Direct military activity: No US/Israeli or Iranian strikes on each other’s forces, bases, or energy infrastructure in the Gulf/Hormuz theater; no Iranian mine-laying or rocket deployments targeting the strait.
- Proxy activity: No significant Iranian-directed attacks by remaining Axis elements on Gulf shipping, ports, or energy assets.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: No successful attacks on commercial vessels; residual mine threat reduced to levels allowing unrestricted transit without military escort for the majority of traffic; AIS-dark activity returns to pre-war norms.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily two-way commercial transits recover to ≥70% of 2025 baseline (~85–100+ vessels/day average sustained).
- War-risk insurance conditions: Additional war-risk premiums for Hormuz/Gulf transit fall to ≤2–3× pre-war levels (pre-war baseline ~0.1–0.25% of hull value per voyage) and remain available without widespread capacity withdrawal or special conditions.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf oil exports via Hormuz (or equivalent) recover to ≥80% of pre-war levels without reliance on large-scale shuttle/STS workarounds as the primary mode; Qatar/UAE LNG loadings recover to ≥75% of pre-war capacity without force majeure.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent and regional differentials no longer embed a persistent >$10–15/bbl Hormuz risk premium relative to non-Gulf benchmarks; LNG spot (JKM/TTF) volatility normalizes without sustained 2022-style spikes driven by Gulf supply loss.
- Force posture of major actors: Material drawdown or repositioning of elevated US CENTCOM assets dedicated to Hormuz escort/mine clearance; Iranian IRGC naval forces no longer enforcing unilateral transit schemes or blacklists as primary control mechanism.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No nuclear threshold crossing, major Gulf infrastructure hits, or third-party (e.g., Israeli) actions that re-ignite the cycle.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant classes (3–6):

1. 1980s Tanker War (Iran-Iraq): Prolonged attacks on shipping; traffic reduced but never fully stopped; full commercial/insurance normalization required years after major fighting subsided and required external naval protection + political end to hostilities. Suggests multi-year commercial lag after kinetic peak.
2. 2019–2020 Gulf tanker attacks / Saudi Aramco strike / maximum pressure period: Short spikes; insurance spiked then partially eased within months once attacks paused, but residual risk premium and posture changes persisted. Faster partial recovery possible with clear de-escalation.
3. Red Sea / Houthi disruptions (2023–ongoing into 2026 context): Sustained chokepoint risk; shipping re-routes, elevated insurance for extended periods even with intermittent pauses; full normalization requires credible deterrence or political settlement measured in many months to years.
4. Post-1991 Gulf War / post-2003 Iraq maritime/energy recovery: Rapid military victory did not produce immediate full commercial normality; residual insecurity, insurance, and force posture adjustments lasted 1–2+ years.
5. Broader post-major Middle East wars with energy infrastructure involvement (e.g., elements of 1973, 1990–91): Political settlement + visible security guarantee needed before markets fully reprice risk down; pure battlefield pauses insufficient.

Base-rate prior (before case-specific evidence; probability of meeting the full multi-domain definition):  
- 1 week: <5%  
- 1 month: 5–10%  
- 3 months: 15–25%  
- 6 months: 30–40%  
- 1 year: 45–55%  
- 18 months: 55–65%  

These reflect that durable multi-domain commercial normality after major Gulf/chokepoint wars is slow; temporary kinetic reductions are more common than full insurance + transit + export + posture reset.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Mutual economic exhaustion + mediation producing a workable Hormuz corridor + sanctions relief package.  
2. Sustained absence of ship attacks allowing insurers and owners to re-enter at scale.  
3. Visible mine clearance + reduced IRGC enforcement of unilateral rules.  
4. US/Israeli decision that further kinetic pressure yields diminishing returns.  
5. Gulf states’ accommodation pressure on both sides.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Fundamental dispute over Hormuz sovereignty/control as Iranian leverage and regime legitimacy tool.  
2. Repeated collapse of interim deals creating credibility deficit for any new pause.  
3. Residual mine, small-boat, and missile threat sustaining high insurance and owner risk aversion.  
4. Domestic political incentives on both sides (US midterms context; Iranian succession/consolidation).  
5. Proxy or third-party (Israel) actions that re-escalate.  
6. Structural damage and force-majeure effects on LNG/export infrastructure requiring time to reverse even after security improves.

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Continued low-to-medium intensity exchanges and failed or partial interim arrangements for several more months. Gradual, uneven recovery in oil flows via workarounds and limited corridors. Insurance and full transit lag. Functional multi-domain normality becomes plausible only in the 6–18 month window if economic costs force a pragmatic (not maximalist) settlement. Residual elevated risk and posture persist beyond pure transit recovery.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
A credible, monitored Hormuz corridor deal within weeks, paired with mutual stand-down and initial sanctions relief, produces rapid transit rebound and insurance softening by 3–6 months. Requires both sides to prioritize economic recovery over maximalist control claims. Probability of this path materializing fully is low given recent collapse history (~15–20% conditional).

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Sovereignty dispute hardens; intermittent ship attacks and strikes continue; insurance remains punitive; LNG recovery stalls; Gulf states hedge further. Functional normality delayed beyond 18 months. Supported by Iranian need for leverage post-leadership change and US domestic constraints. Non-trivial probability (~25–30%).

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Possible overestimation of normalization speed: reference classes may underweight the unique combination of great-power involvement, nuclear residue, and Hormuz as core Iranian deterrent. Insurance and owner behavior can stay risk-averse longer than kinetic pauses suggest. Possible underestimation: economic pain (especially for Iran and energy-importing Asia) plus US political calendar could force faster pragmatic deals than historical Gulf cases. Load-bearing assumptions include continued mutual interest in avoiding full-scale renewed war and the effectiveness of mediation channels.
