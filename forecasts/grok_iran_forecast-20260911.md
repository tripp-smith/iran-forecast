**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each of these horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

“Return to functional normality” requires simultaneous satisfaction of all of the following observable conditions for a sustained period (not a single-day snapshot or temporary pause):

- Direct military activity: No significant US/Iran or Israel/Iran kinetic exchanges (missile, drone, or airstrikes on military or energy infrastructure) in the Gulf region for ≥14 consecutive days; no new major mine-laying or anti-ship missile deployments by Iran that materially threaten commercial routes.
- Proxy activity: No sustained Houthi, Iraqi PMF, or other Iranian-aligned attacks on commercial shipping, energy facilities, or GCC territory at a rate exceeding pre-war baseline (near-zero for Hormuz-linked incidents).
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Free commercial transit without military escort requirements for the majority of voyages; no active Iranian “restricted zones,” turn-back orders, or systematic attacks on merchant vessels; UKMTO/JMIC incident reports at or near pre-war levels.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily AIS-visible commodity vessel transits ≥80% of pre-war baseline (~100–130 vessels/day, or equivalent adjusted for dark shipping); sustained crude + product + LNG flows through Hormuz approaching 15–20 mb/d range (pre-war ~20 mb/d oil-equivalent).
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums ≤0.5% of hull value per voyage (pre-crisis ~0.1–0.25%); cargo war-risk rates near pre-war levels; Joint War Committee high-risk area designation meaningfully narrowed or removed for core Hormuz lanes.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf (ex-Iran under sanctions if still applied) crude/condensate/LNG exports restored to ≥80–85% of pre-war volumes via Hormuz + bypass pipelines without chronic backlog or forced rerouting; Iranian shadow-fleet flows not the dominant residual channel.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent front-month not persistently >$90–95 in the absence of non-Gulf shocks; implied volatility and risk premia consistent with a chokepoint that is open and low-risk rather than contested.
- Force posture of major actors: US naval/air presence reduced from peak wartime levels toward pre-war CENTCOM posture (or a stable, lower-intensity deterrence baseline); no continuous large-scale carrier strike group operations solely for Hormuz escort; Iranian coastal anti-ship and small-boat forces not in high-alert interdiction mode.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No successful large-scale Iranian minefields, massed anti-ship missile barrages, or attacks that close the strait for >48 hours; no new major strikes on Kharg, Ras Tanura, or equivalent critical nodes that reset risk pricing.

A temporary ceasefire, partial corridor escort regime, or oil-price dip driven by demand destruction or non-Hormuz supply does not satisfy the definition. Battlefield pause without commercial and insurance normalization does not count.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot (as of ~11 September 2026, Day ~195–196)**

The war began ~28 February 2026 with US/Israeli strikes. A June 2026 MOU produced a fragile partial reopening that collapsed by early July and again after late-August/early-September exchanges. As of the latest reporting:

- Active tit-for-tat: US strikes on Iranian coastal radar, anti-ship systems, and Iranian tankers; Iranian attacks on merchant shipping (largest declared wave ~9–10 September, with UKMTO confirming multiple disabling strikes); Iranian missile/drone activity against regional bases.
- Hormuz traffic: Single-digit to low-teen daily commodity transits (recent reports of 7–11/day vs pre-war ~125); many vessels dark; effective commercial throughput a small fraction of normal. Combined Gulf crude flows (Hormuz + bypass) recovered intermittently to ~50% of pre-war in some windows but remain heavily constrained.
- Insurance: War-risk hull rates still elevated (recent indications ~1.5% or multiples of 15× peacetime baseline; earlier peaks 3–10%); claims already substantial (> $2 bn range cited).
- Oil: Brent recently traded above $100 (spikes to ~$106–110 range); persistent risk premium; Iranian exports heavily constrained by US measures and shadow fleet.
- Diplomacy: No active durable negotiation track; Iranian conditions for reopening remain maximalist; US posture continues kinetic pressure. Proxy fronts (Houthis in Red Sea/Bab al-Mandeb) also active.
- Overall: Contested, high-risk environment with episodic kinetic activity and structural commercial deterrence. Partial recoveries under the MOU proved reversible.

Evidence base is solid on traffic, price, and kinetic reporting from multiple maritime trackers, UKMTO, Kpler/TankerTrackers-style data, and major outlets; Iranian claims require discounting.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant reference classes (3–6):

1. **1980s Tanker War (Iran-Iraq, 1984–1988 peak)**: Hundreds of attacks on shipping; oil flows through Gulf reduced but never fully stopped; commercial traffic adapted with escorts (Earnest Will); insurance spiked but market functioned; full normalization only after 1988 ceasefire. Timeline: multi-year disruption; functional (escorted) normality during conflict; commercial/insurance recovery measured in months after ceasefire. Breaks down: lower absolute volumes then; no modern precision missiles/drones/mines at current scale; no US direct war with Iran.

2. **Post-1991 Gulf War and post-2003 Iraq War maritime normalization**: Rapid military victory → relatively fast commercial recovery (weeks–months for core routes once kinetic threat removed). Breaks down: decisive conventional defeat of one side; no ongoing asymmetric interdiction capability left in place.

3. **Red Sea/Houthi disruption (2023–2025+)**: Sustained low-intensity attacks; major shipping diversion around Africa; insurance spikes; partial recovery only with sustained naval presence and reduced attack tempo. Normalization incomplete years later for some operators. Breaks down: single chokepoint (Bab al-Mandeb) vs Hormuz criticality; lower energy dependence.

4. **2019–2020 Gulf tanker incidents / Iranian mining threats**: Short, sharp spikes in risk; insurance rose; traffic adapted; returned toward baseline in months once kinetic intensity fell. Breaks down: far lower intensity and duration than 2026 war.

5. **Broader chokepoint/war recovery patterns (Suez 1956/1967/1973 closures; various African/Asian conflicts)**: Full commercial/insurance normalization typically lags kinetic ceasefire by 1–6+ months depending on residual risk and capacity rebuild. Base rates favor multi-month lags for high-value energy routes.

**Base-rate prior (before current evidence adjustment, approximate cumulative probability of meeting the full operational definition):**

- 1 week: ~1–3%
- 1 month: ~8–12%
- 3 months: ~20–30%
- 6 months: ~35–45%
- 1 year: ~50–60%
- 18 months: ~60–70%

These reflect historical patterns in which kinetic pauses occur faster than commercial/insurance/force-posture normalization, and high-stakes chokepoints retain residual risk premiums for extended periods.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization** (ranked)

1. Sustained US military pressure degrading Iranian coastal interdiction and small-boat/missile capacity sufficiently that residual risk becomes insurable at near-normal rates.
2. Iranian internal economic collapse or elite decision to prioritize regime survival over Hormuz leverage, leading to de facto acceptance of open transit.
3. Durable third-party mediated deal that credibly lowers kinetic risk for insurers and operators.
4. Successful US/GCC escort + demining regime that restores operator confidence even without formal Iranian consent.
5. Market adaptation (bypass capacity, non-Gulf supply, demand destruction) reducing the strategic value of continued disruption.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization** (ranked)

1. Iranian strategy of treating Hormuz as enduring leverage; willingness to accept economic pain and episodic attacks to keep risk premiums elevated.
2. Fragility of any ceasefire (demonstrated by June–July and August–September collapses).
3. Insurance market conservatism: underwriters require weeks–months of clean transit before rates collapse; one major incident resets pricing.
4. Persistent proxy capacity (Houthis, others) creating second fronts that sustain regional risk.
5. US domestic political calendar / midterm considerations potentially constraining sustained high-tempo operations or deal-making.
6. Residual Iranian anti-ship systems, mines, and swarm capability even after degradation.

Shipping security + insurance are the tightest gating factors; kinetic pause alone is insufficient.

- Main gating factor: Immediate cessation of attacks + surge in traffic (impossible at current tempo).  
- What would raise: Sudden comprehensive bilateral ceasefire + immediate free transit announcement with visible compliance.  
- What would lower: Already near floor.  
- Confidence: High

- Main gating factor: Credible multi-week clean transit window sufficient for underwriters.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued episodic kinetic exchanges and contested transit for the next 1–3 months, with traffic remaining well below normal and insurance elevated. Gradual degradation of Iranian interdiction capacity plus economic pressure produces a more durable de facto restraint by mid-to-late 2026. Traffic and flows recover toward 60–80% of pre-war over 6–12 months under escort or improved security, with insurance lagging. Full multi-domain normality (per definition) more probable than not only after 12–18 months, and still incomplete on force posture and residual risk.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Rapid US success in degrading coastal and small-boat threats + Iranian recognition of unsustainable costs → de facto open transit within weeks, followed by underwriter rate cuts within 1–2 months. Probability of this path is low (~10–15% weight) given recent reversals.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Iran prioritizes Hormuz leverage indefinitely; periodic attacks and restricted zones persist; insurance remains structurally elevated; traffic stabilizes at 20–40% of pre-war with escorts. This path has meaningful weight (~25–30%) through at least the 6–12 month horizon.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Strongest critique of the forecast: It may under-weight the speed of market adaptation and over-weight the difficulty of insurance normalization once a clean kinetic window appears (1980s and 2019–20 episodes show faster commercial recovery than the definition requires). Conversely, it may over-estimate the probability of Iranian strategic shift; maximalist conditions and regime survival logic could sustain disruption longer than base rates from less existential conflicts suggest. The operational definition is deliberately demanding; a looser “commercially usable with escorts” standard would produce higher probabilities at every horizon. Load-bearing assumptions include continued US willingness to sustain pressure and the absence of a major successful Iranian mine or mass-missile event that resets the clock.
