**1. Forecast Question**  
Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each horizon: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
Functional normality requires simultaneous, sustained satisfaction of all the following for at least 30 consecutive days (or the equivalent continuous condition at longer horizons):

- **Direct military activity**: No significant US/Israeli strikes on Iranian territory or Iranian retaliatory strikes on US/allied assets beyond isolated, non-escalatory incidents. A temporary ceasefire or pause is insufficient.
- **Proxy activity**: Houthi and other Iranian-aligned proxy attacks on Gulf shipping, ports, or energy infrastructure reduced to pre-February 2026 baseline levels (negligible weekly incidents).
- **Shipping security (Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters)**: No active mining campaigns, systematic IRGC interdiction, or requirement for routine naval escorts/permits as a condition of passage. Residual low-level harassment must be at or below 2019–2020 levels.
- **Commercial transit levels**: Daily AIS-visible commercial transits (all vessel types) and confirmed tanker flows through Hormuz at ≥80% of the January–February 2026 pre-war average, sustained. Bypasses (Saudi East-West, UAE ADCOP, etc.) can contribute to overall Gulf export recovery but do not substitute for Hormuz normalization under this definition.
- **War-risk insurance**: Additional premiums broadly available in the commercial market at ≤1–2% of hull value per relevant period (near or below post-June 2026 MoU lows and far from the 6–10% peaks), without primary reliance on special US or Gulf government reinsurance facilities.
- **Oil and LNG export continuity**: Combined Gulf crude, condensate, products, and LNG exports (Hormuz + bypasses + Iranian residual) at ≥90% of pre-war sustained levels, with no major terminal or pipeline outages from military action.
- **Energy market stress and volatility**: Brent crude in a sustained range near or below $75–85 with volatility (realized and implied) near pre-war levels; absence of large war-risk premia in freight or crack spreads.
- **Force posture**: Material US naval drawdown from blockade enforcement levels (no routine interdiction of vessels to/from Iranian ports); Iranian IRGC Navy posture without active mine-laying or night-time restrictions.
- **Absence of major new escalation triggers**: No nuclear breakout steps, large-scale proxy campaigns, or new US/Israeli kinetic campaigns.

Lower oil prices, partial flow recoveries under escort or restriction regimes, or short pauses do not meet the definition.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot (as of ~10 November 2026)**  
The war began with US-Israeli strikes on 28 February 2026. An April ceasefire and June Islamabad Memorandum produced temporary partial reopenings, but both collapsed; the US naval blockade of Iranian ports was reinstated in mid-July and remains in force (CENTCOM reporting 140+ vessels redirected or disabled since then; Task Force Iron Gate added for land/Caspian interdiction). IRGC forces have conducted repeated shipping attacks, mine strikes, and restrictions in and near Hormuz. Oil flows have been highly volatile: total Gulf exports (including bypasses) have at times exceeded pre-war levels (~24 mb/d), but Hormuz-specific tanker flows have frequently run 40–80% below the ~17 mb/d pre-war baseline, with recent AIS-visible traffic near 9% of normal in the latest week. War-risk premiums remain elevated (reports of 6–10% of hull value). Brent has fallen to the low $90s following post-midterm comments by President Trump that further major strikes are “highly unlikely,” that Iran is “militarily effectively over,” and that the status quo (blockade + limited pressure) is preferred by Gulf partners, while diplomatic contacts via Qatar continue without a breakthrough. Iran continues to condition any Hormuz reopening on lifting the blockade and sanctions relief. Low-level strikes (including recent Qeshm Island actions) and proxy activity (Houthis) persist. **4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
- **1980s Tanker War (1984–1988)**: Relevant because of systematic attacks on Gulf shipping and eventual US escort operations. Breaks down: traffic never fully stopped; war ended via broader Iran-Iraq ceasefire. Suggests multi-year disruption possible but shipping recovery within weeks–months once major hostilities and mining cease.  
- **Red Sea Houthi campaign (2023–2025)**: Relevant for prolonged insurance and routing disruption from non-state actors with Iranian links. Breaks down: no full closure of a major oil chokepoint or US blockade of a state exporter. Partial stabilization took 6–18 months with reduced attack tempo.  
- **2019–2020 Gulf tanker incidents and US-Iran tensions**: Relevant for short spikes in Hormuz risk. Breaks down: no sustained war or blockade. Normalization occurred within weeks to a few months after peak incidents.  
- **Suez Canal/Ever Given and other chokepoint mechanical disruptions**: Relevant for commercial recovery speed. Breaks down: no military or political residual risk. Recovery in days to weeks.  
- **Post-ceasefire phases of prior Middle East wars (e.g., post-1991 Gulf War maritime aspects)**: Relevant for force drawdown and insurance lags. Breaks down: clearer military victory or settlement. Shipping and insurance typically normalized in 1–3 months after hostilities ended.  

**Base-rate prior (pre-current-evidence adjustment)**: 1 week ~5%; 1 month ~15%; 3 months ~30%; 6 months ~45%; 1 year ~60%; 18 months ~70%. These reflect that durable commercial normalization lags military pauses and requires resolution of residual threat and insurance/market confidence.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**  
1. Post-midterm US political incentive to avoid new large-scale kinetic campaigns (Trump’s explicit statements).  
2. Gulf states’ preference for status quo over renewed high-intensity war (explicit in reporting).  
3. Demonstrated partial resilience of Gulf export volumes via bypasses and escorted/restricted transit.  
4. Ongoing diplomatic channel via Qatar.  

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**  
1. Active US naval blockade of Iranian ports and Iranian reciprocal restrictions/attacks on Hormuz (gating for insurance and unrestricted transit).  
2. Unresolved nuclear, sanctions, and reparations issues that Iran treats as preconditions.  
3. Persistent elevated war-risk premiums and insurer caution.  
4. Residual mine and interdiction risk plus proxy activity.  
5. Domestic political constraints in both Washington (new congressional balance) and Tehran (maximalist public positions).

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Sustained low-intensity status quo through early 2027: limited or no major US kinetic campaign, continued blockade, Iranian harassment of Hormuz at reduced but non-zero levels, partial oil flows via restricted routes and bypasses, persistently elevated (though somewhat lower) insurance and freight costs. Full functional normality arrives only if/when a political deal or mutual exhaustion produces verified demining, blockade lift, and insurer confidence—most plausibly in the 9–18 month window, with substantial probability it remains incomplete.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Trump’s post-midterm incentives + Gulf pressure produce a limited deal within 1–3 months that lifts the blockade, ends Iranian interdiction, and includes demining. Insurance premiums fall rapidly on verified calm. Probability of this path: ~15–20% by 6 months. Strongest evidence would be simultaneous announcements of blockade suspension and IRGC cessation of restrictions.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Talks fail; low-level strikes and shipping attacks continue or intensify; blockade remains; insurers keep high rates; occasional proxy or nuclear-related spikes. Oil flows stay volatile and below full capacity. This path remains plausible through 18 months (~30–40% probability of still not meeting the full definition).

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Strongest case that I overestimate normalization speed: residual mines, insurer conservatism, and Iranian domestic incentives to retain Hormuz leverage could keep commercial conditions abnormal even after a military pause (reference: multi-year Tanker War and Red Sea lags). Strongest case that I underestimate it: post-midterm US politics and Gulf state preferences may force a quicker de-escalation than historical reference classes suggest, and partial flow recovery has already shown markets can adapt. Load-bearing assumptions: that the blockade is a durable US tool and that Iran will not unilaterally cease interdiction without concessions.
