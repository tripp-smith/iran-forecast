**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each of these horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

“Return to functional normality” means the simultaneous, sustained (minimum 2–4 consecutive weeks, with no major reversion) satisfaction of all of the following observable conditions across military, maritime, commercial, insurance, energy, and political domains. A temporary ceasefire, battlefield pause, escorted or dark-fleet transit, partial oil-flow recovery via workarounds, or price declines alone do not qualify:

- Direct military activity: No significant IRGC or Iranian conventional strikes on commercial shipping, Gulf infrastructure, or regional targets; no major US/Israeli strikes on Iranian territory or forces; residual low-level incidents below pre-war background rates.
- Proxy activity: Sustained restraint by Houthis and other Iran-aligned groups in Bab el-Mandeb/Red Sea and against Gulf states; no major new activation or escalation.
- Shipping security (Strait of Hormuz and adjacent waters): No active Iranian transit-permit regime or effective enforcement; residual mine risk cleared or negligible; no routine need for US naval coordination/escorts for routine commercial traffic.
- Commercial transit levels: AIS-visible and overall commercial vessel transits approaching pre-crisis averages (historically ~80–140/day depending on source and definition); no systematic diversion of major volumes via ship-to-ship transfers or alternative pipelines solely due to threat.
- War-risk insurance: Additional war-risk premiums for Hormuz/Gulf transits returned to near pre-war levels (roughly 0.1–0.3% of hull value for typical cover, versus the multi-percent levels of 2026); capacity available without extraordinary government backstops as the primary mechanism.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Sustained flows through Hormuz near pre-war baselines (~17 mbpd oil equivalent range) without reliance on emergency workarounds, with Iranian exports also normalized under any residual sanctions framework; no major terminal/port disruptions.
- Energy market stress: Brent and related volatility returned to ranges consistent with non-crisis fundamentals; freight rates normalized; no persistent elevated war-risk component in landed costs.
- Force posture: Major actors (US, Iran, partners) have drawn down elevated wartime deployments in the Gulf/Hormuz area to more routine levels; no active blockade of Iranian ports.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No fresh incidents that reopen the above domains (e.g., new mine-laying campaigns, large-scale missile exchanges, proxy surges, or political breakdowns that re-close the strait).

**3. Current Situation Snapshot (as of 21 October 2026)**

The conflict began with US-Israeli strikes on 28 February 2026 (Operation Epic Fury). It is now roughly Day 236 of the associated Hormuz crisis. There is no active comprehensive ceasefire. A June 2026 memorandum of understanding collapsed in July; indirect talks via Pakistan and Qatar continue, but Iran conditions any full reopening of Hormuz on US acceptance of terms (including blockade lift, sanctions relief, and related sequencing). President Trump has pledged no major US strikes on Iran before the 3 November midterm elections; post-election options remain open. IRGC claims of control and permit requirements persist; CENTCOM asserts US/partner control and reports facilitated transits.

AIS-visible commercial traffic is severely restricted (reports of ~1–2% of pre-crisis averages in recent days, e.g., ~1.4/day vs. ~85). US-facilitated or total reported transits are higher but still far below normal (often cited in the 20–30/day range recently vs. pre-war ~138). Oil flows have partially recovered via US-protected corridors, dark/STS transfers in the Gulf of Oman, and Saudi/UAE pipeline bypasses: recent Kpler data cited by the Wall Street Journal show Hormuz-related volumes around 14 mbpd (~82% of pre-war) and total Middle East flows exceeding pre-war levels in one recent week. Iranian crude loadings through the strait remain near zero under the US blockade. Attacks on shipping reached wartime highs in October (dozens of reported incidents; some sinkings). War-risk insurance is extremely elevated (broker reports of 6–10% of hull value per voyage for tankers). Brent has traded in the $105–112 range recently. Residual mine risk is acknowledged; clearance operations have occurred but full confidence and residual threats remain disputed. Houthi activity continues in the Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb theater. Claims by both sides on control, mine numbers, and incident attribution frequently conflict; independent maritime reporting (UKMTO, JMIC, vessel trackers) and energy analytics (Kpler) are weighted more heavily for operational metrics than unverified IRGC assertions.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant historical cases and the timelines they suggest for functional (not merely tactical) stabilization:

- 1984–1988 Tanker War (Iran-Iraq): Systematic attacks on commercial shipping in the Gulf/Hormuz for years. Traffic continued under risk and with US escorts (Operation Earnest Will), but full normalization followed the 1988 ceasefire. Relevance: state actor with asymmetric naval/mine capabilities contesting a chokepoint. Breakdown: Iran then depended on its own oil exports through the strait and lacked the same degree of US blockade pressure. Suggests multi-year disruption possible without political settlement; commercial normalization lagged ceasefire by months.
- Houthi Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb campaign (2023–present, with escalations into 2026): Attacks forced major rerouting, elevated insurance, and partial recovery only after sustained lulls. Full pre-crisis traffic and insurance have not returned even after declared pauses. Relevance: proxy/asymmetric maritime disruption and insurance lag. Breakdown: lower intensity and different geography than a direct Hormuz contest by a major state. Suggests quarters-to-years for confidence restoration.
- Shorter historical Hormuz threat episodes (e.g., 2011–12 sanctions threats, 2019 tanker incidents): Rhetoric and limited actions produced temporary risk premiums and minor disruptions but rapid de-escalation. Relevance: Iranian leverage via Hormuz. Breakdown: no sustained kinetic campaign or wartime blockade. Suggests rapid recovery once kinetic activity ceases.
- Broader regional maritime/energy crises with external intervention: Patterns of partial recovery under protection, with insurance and commercial confidence lagging military pauses by 3–12 months.

Base-rate prior (before case-specific updates): Probability of full functional normality is very low at short horizons (weeks to 1–3 months) because commercial/insurance/force-posture recovery lags any military pause. It rises gradually with time as political settlements or decisive outcomes become more plausible, but remains well below certainty even at 12–18 months given historical duration of chokepoint contests. Approximate prior anchors: ~1–5% (1 week), ~5–10% (1 month), ~10–20% (3 months), ~20–35% (6 months), ~35–55% (1 year), ~50–70% (18 months). These are deliberately conservative on the definition’s multi-domain stringency.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**
- US naval protection and improvised logistics enabling partial oil recovery (ranked high for near-term volume but low for true normality).
- Economic pressure on Iran from blocked exports and war costs (medium; may incentivize deal but also escalation).
- Ongoing mediated talks and mutual interest in avoiding unlimited escalation (medium).
- Post-midterm US decision point that could produce either renewed pressure or a deal window (medium).

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**
- Active Iranian contestation of Hormuz via attacks, permits, and residual mines; ability to re-mine (highest).
- Extremely elevated insurance and shipowner risk aversion (high; lags any pause by months).
- Absence of political settlement; sequencing disputes and Iranian leverage claims (high).
- Proxy theaters (Houthis) and potential post-midterm US/Israeli resumption of strikes (high).
- Force postures and blockade remaining elevated (medium-high).

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Continued contested but not fully closed Hormuz through the midterms, with elevated attacks, high insurance, and partial oil flows via protected/workaround routes. Post-midterm period brings either renewed US pressure or intensified talks. Full multi-domain normality is more likely in the 6–18 month window than earlier, contingent on a political settlement; a prolonged low-intensity maritime contest remains a strong alternative.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Rapid post-midterm deal that ends the blockade, secures Iranian restraint, enables verified mine clearance, and produces an immediate insurance/transit recovery cascade. Strongest drivers would be acute Iranian economic pressure combined with US domestic incentives.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Failed talks, post-midterm strikes, Iranian re-mining or attack campaigns, Houthi escalation, and persistent insurance/shipowner aversion producing a multi-year “new normal” of elevated risk premiums and suboptimal flows.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
The forecast may overestimate normalization speed if Iranian asymmetric capacity (mines, small craft, proxies) proves more resilient and re-deployable than assumed, or if insurance markets treat any residual risk as structural. It may underestimate it if economic exhaustion forces a quicker Iranian concession than base rates suggest, or if US protection plus workarounds are judged sufficient for partial commercial recovery under a looser definition. Load-bearing assumptions include the durability of any future pause and the lag between military and commercial normalization.
