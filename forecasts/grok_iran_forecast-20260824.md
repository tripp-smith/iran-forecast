**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by the horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality is an observable, multi-domain state meeting all of the following thresholds simultaneously for a sustained period (minimum 14 consecutive days for short horizons; 30+ days for longer ones). It is not a temporary ceasefire, battlefield pause, or oil-price dip.

- **Direct military activity**: No Iranian or coalition kinetic strikes on shipping, Gulf state territory/infrastructure, or opposing military assets in the Gulf/Hormuz theater (excluding routine self-defense intercepts of single drones/missiles). No mine-laying or active interdiction.
- **Proxy activity**: No Houthi or other Iranian-aligned proxy attacks on Gulf/Red Sea commercial shipping or energy infrastructure that materially affect Hormuz-adjacent flows.
- **Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters**: Unrestricted transit under international law without mandatory Iranian permits, fees, or IRGC coordination. No active US naval blockade of Iranian ports/routes. Clearance of residual mines confirmed by IMO/CENTCOM/Oman.
- **Commercial transit levels**: Daily commodity vessel transits (Kpler/JMIC/IMF PortWatch equivalents) sustain ≥70% of pre-war baseline (~90–100+ vessels/day, of which a substantial share are non-Iranian-linked tankers and LNG carriers). Southern/Omani and TSS corridors freely usable.
- **War-risk insurance conditions**: Hull war-risk premiums ≤1.0% of hull value (vs. current 7.5–10%+ or higher multiples) with broad capacity available; JWC listed-area status substantially relaxed or removed for core Hormuz transit.
- **Oil and LNG export continuity**: Persian Gulf crude + condensate + products + LNG flows recover to ≥75–80% of Q4 2025 / pre-war levels via Hormuz and alternative pipelines (Petroline, ADCOP, Jask), without systemic STS reliance or dark-fleet dominance for non-Iranian volumes. Qatar LNG and major Gulf terminals operating without chronic security interruptions.
- **Energy market stress and volatility**: Brent futures term structure and realized volatility consistent with non-crisis baselines (no persistent $20+/bbl war premium or repeated 5%+ daily swings driven by Hormuz headlines). Asian and European buyers not systematically diverting or stockpiling at crisis levels solely due to Gulf risk.
- **Force posture of major actors**: Material reduction in US carrier/amphibious/strike-group presence dedicated to Hormuz enforcement relative to peak 2026 levels; Iranian coastal anti-ship/missile/drone forces not on continuous high-alert interdiction posture; no new major US/Israeli strike packages inside Iran tied to Hormuz.
- **Absence of major new escalation triggers**: No fresh attacks on nuclear sites, leadership, or critical energy infrastructure that restart the cycle; no formal Iranian claim of exclusive sovereignty backed by force that closes the strait again.

A temporary MOU-style pause that restores partial traffic while Iran retains de-facto veto, fees, or permit power does **not** meet the definition.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant classes (selected for maritime chokepoint disruption + regional interstate war involving Iran or major powers):

1. **Tanker War / Iran-Iraq War phase (1984–88)**: Relevant for sustained attacks on Gulf shipping, insurance spikes, and partial recovery under escort. Stabilization of commercial traffic took years after the 1988 ceasefire; full insurance/market normalization lagged further. Breaks down because it was a bilateral attrition war without modern precision strikes or nuclear overhang, and US escort eventually imposed order. Suggests multi-year partial recovery, 12–24+ months for functional commercial normality after major kinetic phase ends.

2. **1987–88 US Operation Earnest Will / reflagging**: Relevant for external power forcing transit security. Traffic recovered relatively quickly once escorts and deterrence were credible, but residual Iranian mining/threats persisted. Breaks down on scale of modern Iranian A2/AD and political will. Suggests 3–9 months for shipping security once political decision for sustained protection is locked in.

3. **Houthi Red Sea campaign (2023–ongoing into 2026)**: Relevant for asymmetric maritime disruption, insurance repricing, and rerouting. Traffic has partially adapted but not fully normalized after 2+ years; premiums remain elevated. Breaks down because Red Sea is less critical than Hormuz and lacks the same great-power direct involvement. Suggests 12–24 months minimum for insurance/commercial normalization even after attacks drop sharply.

4. **2024–25 Israel-Hezbollah / Gaza escalations and limited Iran exchanges**: Relevant for rapid kinetic spikes followed by pauses. Ceasefires often restore partial calm in weeks–months but leave force postures and proxy risk high; commercial effects were secondary. Breaks down on Hormuz centrality. Suggests 1–6 months for direct-strike reduction, longer for commercial domains.

5. **Post-1991 Gulf War / post-2003 Iraq maritime stabilization**: Relevant for coalition force posture and oil-export recovery. Shipping and exports normalized within months once major combat ended, but residual risk and insurance lagged. Breaks down on Iranian residual capacity and nuclear dimension. Suggests 3–12 months for core export/shipping recovery after decisive military outcome.

6. **Suez 1956 / 1967–75 closures and reopenings**: Relevant for chokepoint closure duration and recovery. Reopening after political settlement restored traffic in weeks–months, but insurance and market confidence took longer. Breaks down on duration and modern insurance markets. Suggests rapid shipping recovery (weeks) once political clearance is clear, slower commercial full normality (3–9 months).

**Base-rate prior (pre-case-specific adjustment)**:  
Probability of meeting the full operational definition:  
1 week ~1–3%; 1 month ~5–10%; 3 months ~15–25%; 6 months ~30–40%; 1 year ~45–55%; 18 months ~55–65%.  

These reflect historical lag between kinetic pause and multi-domain commercial/insurance/force-posture normalization in Gulf/maritime conflicts. Full “normality” is rarer and slower than partial traffic recovery.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

1. Economic pressure on Iran (oil export collapse under blockade + sanctions) and Gulf states/Asia (higher energy costs) creating mutual interest in a workable transit regime.  
2. US military superiority and ability to sustain blockade/strikes, potentially forcing Iranian concessions or degrading interdiction capacity.  
3. Omani mediation channel and residual diplomatic infrastructure from the MOU.  
4. Market adaptation via pipelines and alternative sources limiting the absolute pain of prolonged closure.  
5. Domestic political costs of prolonged high oil prices in the US and elsewhere.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

1. Iranian regime survival logic: Hormuz control is core leverage after leadership losses and military degradation; any deal that fully restores pre-war freedom of navigation is existentially costly.  
2. Mutual distrust and maximalist positions post-MOU collapse; repeated tit-for-tat has hardened positions.  
3. Residual Iranian asymmetric capabilities (drones, missiles, small boats, mines) that are hard to eliminate completely and cheap to regenerate.  
4. Insurance market conservatism: premiums and capacity require sustained incident-free evidence, not just announcements.  
5. Second-order cascade: each attack or rhetoric spike resets buyer/insurer/shipowner caution for weeks.  
6. Proxy and multi-front risk (Lebanon, Houthis, Gulf states) that can re-ignite even if core US-Iran kinetic tempo drops.  
7. Force-posture inertia: high US presence and Iranian coastal alert status are self-reinforcing.

Ranked importance: Iranian political will and residual capability (1), insurance/commercial confidence lag (2), absence of durable political settlement (3), force posture (4).

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued low-intensity contestation through late 2026: sporadic vessel incidents, active blockade, high insurance, suppressed traffic, elevated but not extreme oil prices. Periodic diplomatic probes (Oman or others) produce temporary pauses that fail to meet the full definition. By mid-to-late 2027 a more durable, imperfect arrangement emerges under economic pressure, restoring majority traffic and partial insurance relief, but residual risk and elevated force posture persist. Full multi-domain normality remains incomplete or fragile at the 18-month mark.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

A credible, verified political package in the next 1–3 months that includes Iranian abandonment of exclusive administration claims, mutual de-escalation, third-party monitoring, and rapid demining. Followed by sustained incident-free traffic for 30+ days, premium compression, and visible US force posture reduction. Probability of this path is low (~10–15% overall) given current positions, but it would raise shorter-horizon forecasts sharply.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Iran prioritizes leverage retention; residual capabilities allow intermittent attacks; US maintains or tightens blockade; insurance market stays punitive; energy markets adapt via pipelines/alternative sources but Gulf commercial normality never fully returns. Proxy fronts periodically reignite. This path keeps probabilities well below 40% even at 18 months and is the primary downside risk.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Strongest critique of the forecast: it may underweight the speed with which markets and shipowners can adapt once kinetic risk visibly falls (historical escort operations and post-ceasefire recoveries sometimes moved faster than insurance rhetoric implies). Conversely, it may overestimate the probability of any political settlement that fully restores pre-war freedom of navigation; Iranian domestic politics after leadership losses and military degradation may treat Hormuz control as non-negotiable, producing a durable semi-closed or dual-control equilibrium. Load-bearing assumptions include: (a) residual Iranian interdiction capacity cannot be fully eliminated by air/naval power alone, and (b) insurance markets require multi-week/month evidence rather than announcements. Evidence that would most damage the forecast: a sudden, verified, durable deal producing rapid traffic and premium recovery within 60 days, or conversely a decisive US campaign that collapses Iranian coastal interdiction capability without producing a political backlash that prolongs the conflict.
