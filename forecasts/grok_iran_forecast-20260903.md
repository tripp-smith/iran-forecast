**1. Forecast Question**  
Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each horizon: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
Functional normality requires simultaneous, observable satisfaction of all of the following for a sustained period (not a one-day snapshot or temporary lull):

- Direct military activity: No sustained exchanges of strikes, missile/drone attacks, or major air/naval operations between Iran/US/Israel or involving Gulf bases at levels that disrupt operations. Isolated residual incidents below a clear de-escalatory threshold are tolerable only if declining.
- Proxy activity: Meaningful restraint by Iranian-aligned proxies (Houthis, others) on shipping and energy infrastructure; no pattern of attacks that materially elevates regional risk.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Commercial vessels can transit the international lanes (or accepted managed corridors) with low probability of attack, boarding, mining, or interdiction. Mines largely cleared from primary lanes. No effective unilateral Iranian veto on routing.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits and oil/LNG volumes through Hormuz recover to ≥70–80% of pre-war baseline (pre-28 Feb 2026 levels of ~70–100+ ships/day and ~15–20 mb/d oil-equivalent flows), sustained over weeks, not spikes under escort.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums for Hormuz transit fall to ≤2–3× pre-war baseline (from current elevated multiples) and remain available without widespread cancellations or exclusion clauses that deter major operators.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf producers (Saudi, UAE, Qatar, Iraq, Kuwait, etc.) restore export continuity via Hormuz plus bypass pipelines without chronic backlog, dark-fleet dominance, or major terminal disruptions. Iranian exports, if occurring, do so under transparent commercial conditions rather than pure shadow activity under blockade.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent and related benchmarks stabilize without persistent war-risk premium dominating price formation; volatility normalizes relative to non-conflict periods; no repeated spikes driven by Hormuz incidents.
- Force posture of major actors: US/coalition naval presence shifts from high-intensity escort/blockade/strike posture toward more routine presence; Iranian coastal and small-boat forces stand down from active interdiction; no large-scale new deployments indicating imminent major escalation.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new large-scale strikes on leadership, nuclear sites, energy infrastructure, or third-country bases that restart the cycle; no formal reimposition of full naval blockades or equivalent closures.

A temporary ceasefire, lower oil prices alone, battlefield pause, or partial escort-dependent traffic does **not** meet the definition. Normalization is commercial, multi-domain, and confidence-based, not merely kinetic quiet.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot (as of ~3 September 2026)**  
War began ~28 February 2026 with US-Israeli strikes. Hormuz disruption is on day ~186–187. Commercial traffic is at roughly 4–6% of pre-war levels (~4 ships/day 7-day average vs. ~70–85+). War-risk insurance remains elevated (~15× peacetime baseline in recent monitoring, previously higher). Oil prices are elevated/volatile (Brent ~$94–96 range recently, with spikes on escalations). **4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant reference classes (3–6):

1. **Tanker War phase of Iran-Iraq War (1980s)**: Prolonged attacks on shipping in Gulf/Hormuz approaches; traffic continued at reduced/risk-adjusted levels under escort and with high insurance; full commercial confidence lagged kinetic reduction by years. Suggests multi-year tail for full normality after major disruption. Breaks down: lower intensity, different technology (mines/small boats/drones today), and Cold War context.
2. **Houthi Red Sea / Bab el-Mandeb campaign (2023–ongoing relative to pre-2026 baseline)**: Shipping volumes collapsed then partially recovered under escorts and rerouting; insurance stayed elevated; normalization incomplete despite intermittent pauses. Suggests 6–18+ months for partial recovery, longer for full confidence. Breaks down: secondary chokepoint vs. primary energy artery; different state actor.
3. **Gulf War 1990–91 / post-invasion Kuwait**: Rapid military victory followed by relatively quick restoration of shipping and exports once major combat ended, aided by clear coalition control. Suggests months for functional recovery if decisive outcome and mine clearance. Breaks down: conventional ground war with clear winner vs. prolonged air/naval/asymmetric contest without regime collapse.
4. **Post-2019 tanker attacks / Soleimani aftermath and limited Iran-related disruptions**: Short-lived spikes in insurance and caution; rapid reversion when kinetic activity stopped. Suggests weeks–months if conflict ends cleanly. Breaks down: far lower intensity and duration.
5. **Suez Canal closures or major chokepoint disruptions (historical)**: Traffic recovers faster than full commercial/insurance confidence once physical obstacle removed. Suggests physical reopening can precede insurance/commercial normalization by weeks to months.
6. **Broader post-major Middle East wars with energy disruption**: Recovery timelines for exports and shipping confidence often 3–12+ months after major combat ends, longer if political settlement incomplete or mines/proxies linger.

**Base-rate prior (pre-current-evidence adjustment)** for meeting the full multi-domain definition:  
1 week: ~1–3%  
1 month: ~5–10%  
3 months: ~15–25%  
6 months: ~30–45%  
1 year: ~50–65%  
18 months: ~60–75%  

These reflect that chokepoint recoveries after intense state-involved disruption are rarely rapid when confidence, insurance, and political settlement are required; decisive victories shorten timelines, prolonged attritional contests lengthen them.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**  
Ranked: (1) Mutual exhaustion + economic pain (Iran export collapse under blockade pressure; US/Gulf desire for lower energy prices pre-midterms/political calendars); (2) Technical possibility of mine clearance + escort regimes enabling stepwise traffic recovery; (3) Pipeline bypass capacity reducing absolute pressure for full Hormuz return; (4) Diplomatic channels (Oman, others) that have produced temporary frameworks before; (5) Market incentives for insurers and operators once kinetic risk falls sustainably.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**  
Ranked: (1) Iranian strategic view of Hormuz control as core leverage/deterrent; competing sovereignty claims hard to resolve; (2) Pattern of MOU collapse after limited reopening attempts; (3) Residual asymmetric capabilities (mines, drones, small boats, missiles) that are hard to eliminate fully and cheap to reconstitute; (4) Insurance and commercial confidence lag kinetic pauses by months; (5) Proxy activation risk and third-party (Israel, Houthis) actions as escalation triggers; (6) Domestic political constraints on both US and Iranian sides limiting durable compromise; (7) Force posture inertia (high US presence, Iranian coastal forces).

**8. Decomposed Sub-Forecasts** (approximate probabilities of material progress on each component by horizon; these are not independent)  

- Major sustained reduction in direct strikes: 1w <10%; 1m ~20%; 3m ~40%; 6m ~55%; 1y ~70%; 18m ~75%.  
- Sustained safe commercial transit (high volumes without constant high risk): 1w <5%; 1m ~10%; 3m ~25%; 6m ~40%; 1y ~55%; 18m ~65%.  
- Meaningful insurance normalization (≤2–3×): lags transit; 1w negligible; 1m <10%; 3m ~20%; 6m ~35%; 1y ~50%; 18m ~60%.  
- Oil/LNG export recovery to high fractions of pre-war via Hormuz + bypass: 1w low; 1m ~15–20%; 3m ~35%; 6m ~50%; 1y ~65%; 18m ~75%.  
- Proxy restraint: 1w low; rises slowly with main conflict de-escalation.  
- Force drawdown / posture shift: lags political settlement; material only at longer horizons.  
- No new major escalation trigger: hard; probability of clean window declines with time if underlying issues unresolved, but cumulative chance of some durable arrangement rises.

Gating factors are safe high-volume transit + insurance confidence + absence of renewed major kinetic cycles. Military pauses alone are insufficient.

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Prolonged periods of reduced but not zero kinetic activity, intermittent diplomatic frameworks that produce temporary traffic increases under managed/escorted conditions, continued heavy use of bypass pipelines, elevated-but-declining insurance, and gradual volume recovery that falls short of full pre-war commercial normality for many months. Full definition met only if a more durable political arrangement reduces the interdiction incentive. Expect stop-start progress rather than clean linear recovery.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Mutual recognition of economic costs + US political calendar pressure produces a more robust framework than prior MOUs; rapid mine clearance and credible escort/ deterrence reduce attack probability; insurers reprice quickly; traffic and exports rebound sharply within 3–6 months. Probability of this path is secondary but non-trivial if exhaustion accelerates.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Iran prioritizes long-term leverage over short-term export recovery; residual capabilities and proxy options allow recurrent low-cost disruption; insurance and commercial confidence remain impaired; US and partners maintain high posture without decisive resolution. Functional normality delayed beyond 18 months or achieved only at lower traffic/confidence levels. This is a credible base-case risk.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Strongest critique of the forecast: It may underweight the speed of commercial adaptation once kinetic risk falls (markets and insurers can move faster than political settlements in some reference classes) and the mitigating effect of pipelines. Conversely, it may still be too optimistic at longer horizons by underweighting how central Hormuz control is to Iranian strategy and how sticky insurance/commercial risk premia can be after 6+ months of disruption. Load-bearing assumptions include continued absence of regime collapse or total military defeat of Iranian maritime capabilities, and that “functional” requires high commercial volumes rather than just managed lower-volume flows. The probabilities could be biased low if a decisive external shock (leadership change, major capability destruction) occurs, or high if the conflict freezes into a new normal of managed risk.
