**1. Forecast Question**  
Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each horizon: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
Functional normality requires simultaneous, sustained satisfaction of the following observable thresholds for at least 30 consecutive days (or the equivalent continuous period by longer horizons):  

- Direct military activity: No kinetic strikes by the US, Israel, or Iran against each other’s forces, infrastructure, or territory in the Gulf or adjacent waters; no reciprocal retaliatory actions.  
- Proxy activity: Houthi, Iraqi militia, or other proxy attacks on Gulf energy infrastructure, shipping, or territory at or below pre-war baseline rates with no material impact on exports or transit.  
- Shipping security (Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters): No mine, missile, drone, or other attacks on commercial vessels; navigation aids and traffic separation schemes operating without interference; no requirements for special permits or “authorized routes.”  
- Commercial transit levels: AIS-visible and dark/facilitated commercial transits (especially tankers and LNG carriers) at ≥80% of the 2025 pre-conflict daily average (~80–130+ commercial crossings depending on metric), without systematic US naval coordination or shuttle workarounds as the dominant mode.  
- War-risk insurance conditions: Additional war-risk premiums available on standard terms at ≤0.5–1% of hull value per transit (near pre-war ~0.25%), with broad market capacity and no reliance on temporary sovereign facilities; Joint War Committee treatment normalized.  
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf (ex-Iran under any residual sanctions) crude and LNG loadings and loadings through Hormuz at ≥85–90% of pre-war volumes without major pipeline bypass reliance or floating storage accumulation.  
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent risk premium attributable to Gulf disruption ≤$5–8/bbl above fundamental levels; realized volatility near historical non-crisis norms; no persistent physical shortages or force majeure declarations.  
- Force posture of major actors: US naval blockade of Iranian ports lifted or reciprocal Iranian restrictions ended; carrier/strike group presence reduced from wartime surge levels; Iranian coastal missile/drone posture no longer oriented toward commercial interdiction.  
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No active ultimatums, nuclear program red-line violations under active negotiation, or unaddressed incidents that both sides treat as casus belli.  

A temporary ceasefire, oil-price decline, or battlefield pause alone does not satisfy the definition. Commercial and insurance normalization are gating.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  

- 1980s Iran-Iraq Tanker War (1984–1988): Relevant as multi-year Gulf shipping attacks with mines, missiles, and escorts. Never fully closed the strait; oil flows continued with higher costs. Full stabilization followed the 1988 ceasefire. Suggests multi-year disruption possible but commercial adaptation; post-settlement recovery in months. Breaks down: neither side fully dependent on the strait in the same way; no modern insurance market or shadow fleet dynamics.  
- 2019 Hormuz/Abqaiq tanker and Saudi attacks: Brief intensity spikes; insurance and prices elevated for weeks to a few months before partial normalization as attacks subsided. Suggests short-cycle recovery if kinetic activity ends. Breaks down: far smaller scale and no sustained blockade or political preconditions.  
- Red Sea Houthi campaign (2023–2025/26): Prolonged diversion, elevated insurance for 1–2+ years; partial transit recovery lagged security improvements by quarters. Suggests insurance and commercial habits lag military pauses by 3–9 months. Breaks down: bypass (Cape) available; Hormuz has none of comparable scale.  
- 1991 Gulf War: Rapid, decisive; Hormuz largely unaffected and normalization quick post-hostilities. Suggests short recovery after decisive outcome. Breaks down: not a prolonged attritional or proxy contest.  
- Other chokepoint closures (e.g., Suez incidents, temporary mine campaigns): Commercial flows recover in weeks–months after threat removal; insurance lags by additional months.  

Base-rate prior (pre-current-evidence adjustment, weighted toward prolonged unresolved political/military contests with insurance and commercial lags):  
1 week: <5%; 1 month: ~10%; 3 months: ~20%; 6 months: ~35%; 1 year: ~50%; 18 months: ~60–65%.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**  
- Diplomatic channel via Qatar remains open and Gulf states have urged restraint.  
- US post-election signaling of preference for pressure over renewed major combat.  
- Economic pressure on Iran from blockade and export loss creates incentive for a deal.  
- Commercial adaptation (some facilitated/dark traffic, pipeline bypasses) limits total shutdown severity.  

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**  
- Unresolved political preconditions and nuclear issues create high barrier to settlement.  
- Persistent low-level kinetic incidents and mine/threat environment prevent insurance and commercial confidence.  
- Proxy activity (Houthis) and risk of escalation on other fronts.  
- Insurance market lag and risk aversion even after military pauses.  
- Elevated force postures and lack of reciprocal de-escalation.

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Continued low-level incidents and elevated insurance/transit costs through at least early 2027, with intermittent diplomacy. A sequenced limited arrangement (partial blockade relief, Iranian restraint on shipping, nuclear constraints deferred) becomes more plausible in the 6–12 month window under economic pressure and US domestic constraints, enabling gradual commercial recovery thereafter. Full multi-domain normality is more likely after 12 months than before.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Rapid Qatar-brokered sequenced deal within weeks (US lifts key blockade elements; Iran halts interdiction and accepts limited nuclear steps), followed by 30–60 days of verified calm. Insurance capacity returns quickly; traffic recovers to 80%+ within 2–3 months. Probability of this path: ~15–20% by 6 months.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
No durable settlement; periodic limited strikes, mine threats, and proxy activity continue. Insurance remains elevated; commercial traffic stays suppressed with workarounds. Nuclear or regional triggers (Lebanon, Yemen, or Iranian program) periodically raise risk. Probability of functional normality remaining below 40% even at 18 months: ~25–30%.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Strongest case that the forecast overestimates normalization: Iranian leadership may prefer prolonged leverage via Hormuz disruption for domestic and regional reasons; insurance markets and shipowners may treat any residual risk as structural for years; US blockade incentives and nuclear red lines may prove irreconcilable. Strongest case that it underestimates normalization: Economic pain on Iran could force concessions faster than political statements suggest; post-midterm US constraints and Gulf-state pressure could produce a face-saving pause that commercial actors treat as sufficient once incidents drop for 60–90 days. Load-bearing assumptions: that insurance and commercial behavior will lag military pauses by months (supported by Red Sea and Tanker War analogs) and that a full political settlement is required for the highest thresholds.
