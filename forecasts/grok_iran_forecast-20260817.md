**1. Forecast Question**  
Probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War, by horizons of 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months (as of 17 August 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
All of the following must hold concurrently and observably for a sustained period (not a temporary pause or partial rebound):

- Direct military activity: No sustained kinetic exchanges (strikes, missile/drone attacks) between Iran/US/Israel or major Gulf actors inside or immediately adjacent to the Gulf/Hormuz that materially disrupt commercial operations. Isolated incidents do not disqualify if they are rapidly contained and do not restart cycles.
- Proxy activity: No significant activation or expansion of proxy attacks (Houthis, Iraqi militias, Hezbollah maritime/energy targeting) that create parallel disruptions to Gulf shipping or energy infrastructure.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Freedom of navigation restored such that commercial vessels transit without routine Iranian boarding/turn-back demands, mandatory IRGC coordination as a condition of passage, or credible threat of attack. Designated routes (if any) must be internationally accepted and not unilaterally enforced by force.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits (AIS-tracked, two-way) recover to ≥70–80% of pre-war baseline (~100–130 vessels/day; tanker-heavy flows supporting ~15–20 mb/d oil + LNG equivalent). Sustained multi-week averages, not one-off spikes. Backlog of stranded vessels largely cleared.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Hull war-risk premiums return to ≤0.5–1% of hull value (from current ~7.5–10% or 30× peacetime multiples) with broad underwriter capacity restored and Joint War Committee high-risk listing meaningfully narrowed or removed. Cargo war risk similarly normalizes.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf exporters (Saudi, UAE, Kuwait, Qatar, Iraq) resume near-normal loadings and sailings without routine diversions, dark sailings, or port/terminal interruptions. Iranian exports (to the extent permitted) not the sole residual flow.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent and related markers no longer embed a large, persistent Hormuz risk premium driven by closure/attack risk (current levels ~$88 with elevated volatility). Futures curves and implied volatility reflect structural rather than acute geopolitical risk.
- Force posture of major actors: US naval blockade of Iranian ports lifted or rendered non-operative for commercial traffic; Iranian forces not maintaining active interdiction posture; no major new deployments that signal imminent escalation.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No unresolved nuclear “breakout” crisis, major leadership succession shocks, or third-party actions that credibly restart large-scale hostilities within the forecast window.

A temporary ceasefire, partial traffic rebound under Iranian-controlled corridors, lower oil prices from demand destruction, or battlefield pauses do not meet the standard. Commercial normalization lags military pauses.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot (as of ~17 August 2026, ~Day 170)**  
War began late February 2026 with US/Israeli strikes. A June 17 US-Iran MOU produced a brief partial reopening (transits rose to ~40–70+/day briefly, insurance eased temporarily). It collapsed after Iranian attacks on commercial vessels (including Ever Lovely and others), US retaliatory strikes, and Iranian strikes on Gulf bases/shipping. US reimposed naval blockade of Iranian ports; sanctions on Iranian oil sales reinstated.  

Current traffic: Extremely low. IMF PortWatch and tracker data show single-digit daily transits in early-to-mid August (e.g., 1 on 9 Aug; 7-day averages ~3–5% of pre-war ~73–130 baseline). Cumulative post-war crossings far below expected. Hundreds of vessels remain stranded or holding. Iranian crude continues via shadow fleet primarily to China (~1.5 mb/d range in earlier reporting); broader Gulf exports heavily constrained.  

Attacks continue (recent UAV strikes on ADNOC-affiliated tankers; UAE accusations of IRGC piracy/coercion). Iran-Oman talks on new corridors reported in “final stages,” but Iranian officials (Araghchi, SNSC) explicitly state any route agreement does not reopen the strait; reopening requires US concessions (lift blockade, sanctions relief, compensation, force posture changes, end to actions against proxies). US maintains blockade can be sustained indefinitely; recent air campaign pause on Iranian territory but enforcement continues.  

Insurance: War-risk premiums remain extremely elevated (reports of 7.5–10% of hull value or ~30× peacetime; VLCC voyage costs in multi-million range). Brent ~$88 (elevated vs. pre-war, with prior spikes above $100–120). Nuclear facilities damaged but enrichment status and stockpiles remain contested/denied access. Lebanon/Hezbollah and other proxy theaters active. Force posture elevated on all sides.

Evidence base is reasonably dense on shipping metrics and official statements; kinetic details and internal Iranian decision-making have higher uncertainty.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant classes (with breakdowns):

- 1980s Tanker War (Iran-Iraq): Prolonged attacks on shipping in Gulf/Hormuz. Partial commercial adaptation occurred, but full security/insurance normalization required years after major fighting subsided and required clear end to systematic targeting. Suggests multi-year lag for commercial normality even after kinetic intensity drops. Breaks down because it was bilateral Iran-Iraq without direct US kinetic involvement or modern precision drones/missiles at scale.
- 2019–2021 Gulf tanker attacks / Iranian seizures + max-pressure sanctions: Discrete spikes in risk, insurance, and some rerouting; relatively rapid partial recovery once kinetic intensity fell and diplomacy advanced. Timelines measured in months for partial normalization. Breaks down: lower intensity, no full-scale war or prolonged near-closure.
- Red Sea/Houthi disruptions (2023–2025): Persistent asymmetric attacks kept insurance elevated and traffic suppressed for many operators despite naval task forces. Partial adaptation (Cape routing) but no full return to pre-disruption patterns while attacks continued. Suggests insurance and commercial confidence lag kinetic pauses by many months. Breaks down: alternative routes existed; Hormuz has fewer substitutes.
- Other chokepoint or regional war cases (Suez closures, earlier Gulf crises, post-2003 Iraq maritime effects): Full functional recovery of high-volume commercial traffic and risk pricing typically required clear cessation of interdiction capability/intent plus sustained incident-free periods (often 6–18+ months). Nuclear or regime-survival stakes raise the bar further.
- Broader interstate war termination patterns with economic chokepoints: Ceasefires often precede commercial recovery by substantial lags; unresolved control disputes prolong disruption.

Base-rate prior (pre-current-evidence adjustment, treating this as a high-intensity, control-contested, multi-domain Gulf maritime war):  
- 1 week: <5%  
- 1 month: 5–15%  
- 3 months: 15–30%  
- 6 months: 25–45%  
- 1 year: 40–60%  
- 18 months: 55–70%  

These reflect historical lag between kinetic reduction and multi-domain commercial/insurance/force-posture normalization, adjusted upward slightly for modern incentives to restore energy flows and downward for the nuclear/regime-control stakes and Iran’s demonstrated willingness to use Hormuz as leverage.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Mutual economic pain (elevated energy prices, Iranian isolation, Gulf exporter losses, US political pressure on prices) creating incentives for a durable bargain.  
2. Existence of Iran-Oman technical track that could produce an accepted corridor if paired with US concessions.  
3. US demonstrated ability to impose costs and sustain blockade, potentially forcing Iranian prioritization of economic relief.  
4. Historical precedent that commercial actors return once incident rates fall and insurance becomes available.  
5. Damage to Iranian military infrastructure limiting sustained high-intensity interdiction.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Iranian strategic view of Hormuz control as core leverage/regime security asset; explicit rejection of pre-war navigational order.  
2. Unresolved US-Iran core issues (blockade, sanctions, nuclear, force posture, compensation, proxies).  
3. Proven fragility of June MOU—kinetic resumption rapidly reversed partial gains.  
4. Insurance and commercial confidence require sustained incident-free periods; current premiums and carrier advisories show deep risk aversion.  
5. Second-order effects: proxy activation risk, Lebanon theater, potential succession or hardliner dynamics inside Iran, Asian buyer adaptations that reduce urgency.  
6. Force posture inertia and domestic political constraints on both sides against rapid, verifiable drawdowns.

**8. Decomposed Sub-Forecasts** (approximate probabilities that the component is sufficiently normalized to support overall functional normality)

- Major sustained reduction in direct strikes: 1w ~5–10%; 1m 15–25%; 3m 35–50%; 6m 50–65%; 1y 65–75%; 18m 70–80%.  
- Sustained safe commercial transit (volume + security): 1w <5%; 1m 10–20%; 3m 25–40%; 6m 40–55%; 1y 55–70%; 18m 65–75%.  
- Meaningful insurance normalization: lags transit/security by weeks to months; similar but delayed curve.  
- Oil/LNG export recovery (Gulf-wide): tracks transit closely but with some residual shadow/alternative flows.  
- Proxy restraint: lower probability early; improves if main theater stabilizes.  
- Force drawdown / blockade lift: tightly coupled to political deal; low early, rises with deal probability.  
- No new major escalation trigger: declining risk over time if nuclear and leadership issues stabilize, but residual risk persists.

Gating factors are primarily political settlement on control/blockade/sanctions + sustained low incident rate. Military pause alone is insufficient.

**9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon**

**1 Week**  
- Probability: 3%  
- Base-rate anchor: Very low; historical pauses do not produce multi-domain normality in days.  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Down. Traffic near floor, insurance elevated, talks not producing immediate reopening, recent attacks.  
- Core rationale: Physical and commercial systems cannot reconfigure in a week even under ideal politics.  
- Main gating factor: Instantaneous comprehensive political deal + immediate cessation of all interdiction (implausible).  
- What would raise: Sudden high-level US-Iran agreement with immediate verifiable implementation and insurance market response.  
- What would lower: Further attacks or explicit rejection of talks.  
- Confidence: High (near-floor outcome is robust).

**1 Month**  
- Probability: 12%  
- Base-rate anchor: Low teens possible only with rapid diplomatic breakthrough.  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slightly down. Oman track exists but Iran conditions reopening on broader US concessions; June precedent of fragility.  
- Core rationale: Even a signed deal requires demining/clearance, insurance repricing, vessel repositioning, and trust-building.  
- Main gating factor: Binding political agreement that satisfies Iranian leverage demands without triggering US domestic/political collapse, plus initial incident-free window.  
- What would raise: Announced durable deal + US blockade suspension + rapid transit rebound + insurance drop.  
- What would lower: Breakdown of Oman talks, major new attacks, nuclear escalation.  
- Confidence: Medium.

**3 Months**  
- Probability: 28%  
- Base-rate anchor: Mid-20s to low-30s in reference classes for partial-to-functional recovery after intensity drop.  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Neutral to slight down. Economic incentives strong, but control dispute and trust deficit large.  
- Core rationale: Plausible window for a deal + initial commercial recovery if both sides prioritize energy/economic stabilization over maximalist aims. Still requires sustained security.  
- Main gating factor: Verifiable reduction in Iranian interdiction capability/intent + US reciprocal steps + multi-week incident-free transit.  
- What would raise: Successful corridor implementation + sanctions/blockade relief package + insurance capacity return.  
- What would lower: Prolonged low-level attrition, proxy expansion, or hardliner consolidation in Tehran.  
- Confidence: Medium.

**6 Months**  
- Probability: 42%  
- Base-rate anchor: Mid-40s range after accounting for lag.  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight down due to demonstrated resilience of Iranian leverage strategy and US willingness to sustain pressure.  
- Core rationale: Enough time for negotiated outcome, military adaptation, and commercial rebuilding if political will materializes. Still non-trivial chance of frozen low-level conflict.  
- Main gating factor: Durable political settlement that addresses (even imperfectly) control, sanctions, and nuclear issues.  
- What would raise: Clear de-escalatory trajectory with rising transit and falling premiums.  
- What would lower: Entrenched dual-track (Iranian control + residual attacks) or major third-party shock.  
- Confidence: Medium.

**1 Year**  
- Probability: 58%  
- Base-rate anchor: Mid-to-high 50s.  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Modest downward pressure from unresolved structural issues (Iranian strategic culture around Hormuz, nuclear file).  
- Core rationale: Cumulative economic and political costs favor some form of functional arrangement for most actors; commercial systems adapt. Residual risk of incomplete recovery remains.  
- Main gating factor: Whether a political equilibrium emerges that removes routine kinetic risk and restores insurance/commercial confidence.  
- What would raise: Sustained high transit volumes + normalized insurance + reduced force postures.  
- What would lower: Prolonged attrition war or Iranian consolidation of de facto control that keeps premiums elevated.  
- Confidence: Medium-Low (longer horizon increases model uncertainty).

**18 Months**  
- Probability: 67%  
- Base-rate anchor: Mid-to-high 60s.  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight down; structural Iranian incentives and multipolar adaptations (Chinese demand, alternative routes/pipelines) could sustain a “new normal” short of full pre-war functionality.  
- Core rationale: Most reference classes show substantial recovery by this horizon absent permanent regime-level hostility. Definition remains demanding, so probability stays well below certainty.  
- Main gating factor: Long-run political settlement vs. managed low-level contestation.  
- What would raise: Clear end to interdiction campaigns and return of mainstream commercial operators/insurers.  
- What would lower: Institutionalization of Iranian administrative control + persistent high risk pricing.  
- Confidence: Medium-Low.

**10. Most Likely Path**  
Continued low-level kinetic and coercive contestation over Hormuz control for weeks to several months, with intermittent diplomacy (Oman track, indirect US-Iran messaging). Partial technical agreements on routes possible but reopening conditioned on broader package. Gradual, uneven traffic recovery only after a political bargain that includes US reciprocal steps. Insurance and full commercial confidence lag by additional months. By 6–12 months a functional (if imperfect and more securitized) equilibrium becomes the modal outcome; full pre-war normality is not guaranteed even then.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**  
Rapid high-level bargain driven by energy-price political pressure in the US, Iranian economic distress, and Gulf state mediation. Corridor deal + blockade suspension + limited sanctions relief produce incident-free windows. Insurance capacity returns quickly; transits rebound to 60–80% within 1–3 months. Requires Iranian prioritization of economic survival over maximal leverage and US willingness to accept imperfect control arrangements. Probability mass concentrated in the upper tails of the 1–6 month forecasts.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**  
Iran treats Hormuz as permanent strategic asset; hardliners dominate; US maintains maximum pressure/blockade; proxies remain active; nuclear file festers. Result: multi-year low-intensity maritime contest, permanently elevated insurance, structural rerouting, and “managed insecurity.” Traffic recovers only partially and under higher risk premiums. This is the primary downside that keeps longer-horizon probabilities from exceeding ~70%.

**13. Red-Team Critique**  
Possible over-estimation of normalization: Historical Tanker War and Red Sea cases show commercial recovery can be slower and more incomplete than expected when one party retains interdiction capability and views the chokepoint as existential leverage. Iranian statements are consistent and repeated; June MOU collapse is a strong negative update. Insurance markets may reprice structurally higher. Nuclear and succession risks are under-weighted.  

Possible under-estimation: Economic pain is extreme and mutual; modern energy markets and political cycles create stronger incentives for deal-making than 1980s. US military superiority and blockade sustainability give Washington bargaining power. Commercial actors have already demonstrated willingness to move when risk appears contained. Base rates from lower-intensity episodes may be too pessimistic for a high-cost, high-visibility crisis.

Load-bearing assumptions: (1) Iranian leadership ultimately trades some control for economic relief; (2) US domestic politics prioritize energy prices sufficiently to accept imperfect deals; (3) no catastrophic escalation (nuclear use, major regime collapse, great-power direct clash).
