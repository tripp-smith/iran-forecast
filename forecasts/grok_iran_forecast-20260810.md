**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by the horizons of 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months (from the reference date of 10 August 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality is defined as the simultaneous satisfaction of all the following observable, multi-domain conditions for a sustained period of at least 14 consecutive days (to distinguish from temporary pauses):

- Direct military activity: No confirmed US, Israeli, or Iranian strikes on each other’s territory, bases, or major military assets in or adjacent to the Persian Gulf; no large-scale exchanges of missiles/drones between principals.
- Proxy activity: No major Houthi, Iraqi militia, or other Iranian-aligned attacks on commercial shipping, energy infrastructure, or Gulf-state targets that disrupt operations; frequency and severity of residual low-level incidents at or below pre-February 2026 baseline.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Commercial vessels transit without requirement for Iranian Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) coordination/permits, without US blockade enforcement actions (redirects, boardings, disablements), and without kinetic incidents (mines, drones, missiles, small-boat attacks) affecting commercial traffic. Pre-war traffic corridors function without effective dual control or unilateral schemes.
- Commercial transit levels: Average daily vessel transits through the Strait of Hormuz ≥80% of the 2025 pre-war baseline (~110–140 vessels/day, depending on exact metric used by trackers such as Kpler/JMIC/MarineTraffic), sustained.
- War-risk insurance conditions: Additional war-risk premiums for hull and cargo for Hormuz transit ≤1.0% of hull value (pre-war baseline ~0.25%; current elevated range frequently 7.5–10%+), with broad availability of cover from major underwriters and no widespread withdrawal of capacity or Joint War Committee listed-area designation remaining in force at crisis levels.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Combined crude/products exports from Persian Gulf producers (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Kuwait, Iraq, Qatar, Iran where applicable) ≥85% of February 2026 pre-war levels on a multi-week average, with Qatar LNG loadings and Ras Laffan operations not under force majeure or material capacity reduction attributable to the conflict; Iranian exports (if permitted) not subject to active US naval blockade.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent crude futures 30-day realized volatility and risk premium attributable to Gulf disruption not materially elevated relative to non-conflict periods (current Brent ~$84–85); no persistent structural deficit narrative driven by Hormuz risk.
- Force posture of major actors: No sustained elevated US CENTCOM surge presence specifically for Hormuz enforcement beyond routine levels; Iranian coastal missile/drone/small-boat posture not maintained at wartime density for interdiction; no active US naval blockade of Iranian ports.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No new significant kinetic events, leadership assassinations, nuclear threshold crossings, or third-party interventions that reopen direct hostilities.

A temporary ceasefire, partial traffic recovery under Iranian or dual control, lower oil prices from demand destruction or bypass pipelines, or a battlefield pause without commercial and insurance normalization do **not** satisfy the definition.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant reference classes (selected for chokepoint disruption + regional war + maritime insurance/commercial recovery dynamics):

1. **Iran-Iraq Tanker War (1984–1988)**: Sustained attacks on shipping in the Gulf; traffic continued under convoy/escort but never fully “normal” until ceasefire. Partial commercial recovery lagged formal end by months; insurance remained elevated. Suggests 6–18+ months for functional commercial normality after major fighting ends. Breaks down: No full closure of Hormuz; different great-power involvement.
2. **Red Sea/Houthi disruption (late 2023–2025/ongoing elements)**: Proxy attacks caused major re-routing, elevated insurance, and traffic collapse on Bab el-Mandeb. Even after periods of reduced attacks, full traffic/insurance normalization has been slow and incomplete. Suggests 3–12+ months after major de-escalation for meaningful commercial recovery. Breaks down: Not a principal-to-principal war with nuclear/leadership dimensions; alternative routing existed.
3. **Gulf War 1990–91 and post-liberation residual threats**: Rapid conventional military resolution; commercial shipping recovered relatively quickly (weeks to a few months) once kinetic threat removed, though residual mine/insurance issues lingered. Suggests faster track (1–3 months) if decisive military outcome. Breaks down: Clear victory and occupation dynamics absent here; Iran retained significant asymmetric capabilities.
4. **Post-2019 Abqaiq/ tanker attacks and 2019–2020 Iran-US tensions**: Short, sharp spikes in risk premiums and temporary traffic caution; rapid partial recovery once immediate threat receded. Suggests 1–3 months possible for limited normalization if no sustained campaign. Breaks down: Far lower intensity and duration than 2026 war.
5. **Suez Canal blockage (Ever Given 2021) and other temporary chokepoint closures**: Purely operational; full recovery in days–weeks. Not closely analogous but provides lower bound for pure logistics recovery once security restored.
6. **Broader post-major Middle East wars (e.g., 2003 Iraq invasion residual, Lebanon 2006)**: Political/military settlements often lag commercial risk pricing by many months; proxies and residual capabilities prolong elevated risk.

**Base-rate prior (before case-specific updating)**: Probability of meeting the full operational definition by horizon: 1 week ~1–3%; 1 month ~5–10%; 3 months ~15–25%; 6 months ~30–45%; 1 year ~50–65%; 18 months ~60–75%. These reflect historical lag between kinetic reduction and multi-domain commercial/insurance/force-posture normalization in chokepoint conflicts. Current case has higher intensity, dual-control claims, leadership succession effects, and proxy depth, which pull priors downward, especially at shorter horizons.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

Ranked by importance:
1. Mutual exhaustion and domestic political pressure (US gasoline prices, Iranian economic contraction/blockade effects, Gulf state desire for de-escalation).
2. Existence of Oman/Pakistan/Qatar mediation channels that previously produced the June MOU.
3. Pipeline bypass capacity (Saudi East-West, UAE Habshan-Fujairah) already mitigating some volume loss.
4. Trump administration stated preference for rapid end and deal-making style.
5. Historical precedent that chokepoint insurance and traffic can recover once kinetic risk is credibly reduced for several weeks.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

Ranked by importance:
1. Iran’s insistence on formalized control/administrative rights over Hormuz as core leverage and prestige issue post-Khamenei succession.
2. Structural gap between military pause and commercial confidence (insurers require sustained evidence; shipowners re-route or demand escorts).
3. Residual Iranian asymmetric capabilities (mines, drones, anti-ship missiles, small boats) and proxy networks that can be activated at low cost.
4. US blockade and sanctions reimposition create reciprocal escalation incentives.
5. Leadership and factional dynamics in Tehran (hardline military influence visible in recent appointments and maximalist demands).
6. Second-order effects: elevated force postures, trapped vessels, damaged trust among Gulf states, and market memory of repeated ceasefire collapses.

**8. Decomposed Sub-Forecasts**

Approximate probabilities that each major component of the definition is met by horizon (these are not independent):

- Major reduction in direct principal strikes: 1w 15%; 1m 35%; 3m 55%; 6m 70%; 1y 80%; 18m 85%.
- Sustained safe commercial transit (no IRGC mandate, no blockade enforcement, low incident rate): 1w <5%; 1m 10%; 3m 25%; 6m 40%; 1y 55%; 18m 65%.
- Meaningful insurance normalization (≤1% additional): 1w <5%; 1m 8%; 3m 20%; 6m 35%; 1y 50%; 18m 60%.
- Oil/LNG export recovery to ≥85% pre-war sustained: 1w <5%; 1m 15%; 3m 30%; 6m 50%; 1y 65%; 18m 75% (pipelines help, but full Hormuz volume and LNG slower).
- Proxy restraint: 1w 20%; 1m 30%; 3m 45%; 6m 55%; 1y 65%; 18m 70%.
- Force posture drawdown / end of blockade: 1w <5%; 1m 10%; 3m 25%; 6m 40%; 1y 55%; 18m 65%.
- No new major escalation trigger: 1w 70%; 1m 55%; 3m 45%; 6m 40%; 1y 35%; 18m 30% (cumulative risk rises with time under unresolved issues).

Gating factors are primarily safe transit + insurance + force posture; military pause alone is insufficient.

**9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon**

**1 Week**  
- Probability: 2%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~1–3%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight downward (active blockade, suppressed traffic, maximalist Iranian demands, recent proxy activity).  
- Core rationale: No pathway exists for multi-domain normalization in seven days. Even a sudden technical deal would leave traffic, insurance, and posture lagging.  
- Main gating factor: Shipping security and insurance.  
- What would raise this forecast materially: Immediate public US-Iran joint announcement of full unconditional Hormuz opening + verified zero-incident period + insurer statements of rate cuts.  
- What would lower this forecast materially: New major strike or vessel attack.  
- Confidence in this estimate: High  

**1 Month**  
- Probability: 8%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~5–10%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight downward.  
- Core rationale: A deal is possible but historical and current evidence show commercial/insurance recovery requires sustained safe passage evidence that one month is unlikely to fully supply after repeated ceasefire failures.  
- Main gating factor: Credible, verified safe transit under neutral or dual-accepted rules + insurer response.  
- What would raise: Signed limited technical agreement + 2–3 weeks of high transit volume without incident + premium compression.  
- What would lower: Collapse of Oman channel or major proxy escalation.  
- Confidence: Medium-High  

**3 Months**  
- Probability: 22%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~15–25%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Neutral to slight downward.  
- Core rationale: Time allows for a negotiated technical opening and initial traffic recovery, but full definition (insurance ≤1%, force posture normalization, sustained ≥80% transits, proxy restraint) remains demanding given Iran’s control claims and residual capabilities.  
- Main gating factor: Insurance and commercial confidence lag.  
- What would raise: Durable agreement removing blockade and Iranian permit regime + measurable premium drop + traffic rebound.  
- What would lower: Renewed principal strikes or sustained low traffic under Iranian unilateral scheme.  
- Confidence: Medium  

**6 Months**

- Current-evidence adjustment: Slight downward.

- Main gating factor: Enduring removal of dual-control claims and insurance market reset.  
- What would raise: Comprehensive political settlement including sanctions/force posture elements + 1–2 months of normal traffic.  
- What would lower: Entrenchment of Iranian administrative claims or US blockade as new normal.

- Probability: 55%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~50–65%

- Core rationale: Cumulative pressure and historical recovery windows support majority probability, yet the definition remains strict and second-order effects (trust, proxies, force posture) can persist. Long-horizon forecasts carry higher uncertainty.  
- Main gating factor: Political settlement that addresses Hormuz control without creating new triggers.  
- What would raise: Verified multi-month normal traffic + insurance normalization + drawdown.

**18 Months**  
- Probability: 65%  
- Base-rate anchor: ~60–75%  
- Current-evidence adjustment: Neutral to slight downward (persistence risk).  
- Core rationale: Most reference classes eventually see functional commercial recovery, but not always full return to pre-crisis “normality” under the strict multi-domain standard used here. Residual Iranian leverage and market memory reduce the upper tail.  
- Main gating factor: Whether a stable political equilibrium emerges that removes Hormuz as active leverage.  
- What would raise: Clear, durable political resolution + full traffic/insurance recovery observed for quarters.  
- What would lower: Protracted low-intensity contestation or new escalation cycles.  
- Confidence: Medium-Low (inherent long-horizon uncertainty)  

Probabilities are internally consistent (rising with time, concave after 6–12 months) and treat the definition as demanding.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued low-intensity contestation over Hormuz control for weeks to a few months, punctuated by technical mediation via Oman and intermittent kinetic incidents or threats. A limited technical arrangement on routes eventually emerges under exhaustion pressure, allowing partial traffic recovery. Insurance and full commercial confidence lag by additional months. Full multi-domain normality (per definition) most likely materializes in the 9–18 month window if no major new trigger intervenes, but with residual elevated risk versus pure pre-war baseline.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Decisive mutual recognition that continued disruption imposes intolerable costs (US domestic politics, Iranian economic distress under blockade, Gulf state pressure). Rapid mediated deal removes blockade and Iranian permit regime, produces verified high-volume safe transit for 3–4 weeks, triggers insurer rate compression, and leads to force posture adjustments. Probability mass shifts left: 3-month probability could reach 40%+, 6-month 60%+.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Iran successfully entrenches administrative claims over Hormuz as a core post-war prestige and leverage asset; US maintains blockade or intermittent enforcement; proxies remain active; repeated ceasefire attempts fail. Result is a “managed but abnormal” strait with permanently higher insurance, lower effective capacity, and recurring risk spikes. Full definition may not be met even at 18 months (probability of normality <40%).

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Strongest case that the forecast overestimates normalization: The operational definition is extremely demanding (especially simultaneous insurance + transit + posture). Historical chokepoint recoveries often leave a “new normal” of elevated risk that fails the strict multi-domain test. Iranian leadership incentives post-succession favor preserving Hormuz leverage. Trump deal-making can produce headlines without durable commercial effects (as seen with the June MOU collapse).

Strongest case that the forecast underestimates normalization: Economic pain is already severe on both sides and Gulf states; mediation infrastructure exists; pipelines blunt the worst volume losses; markets and shipowners have adapted once before; political will for an exit is publicly signaled. If a credible technical opening holds for even 4–6 weeks, insurance and traffic can move faster than pure historical averages.

Load-bearing assumptions: (1) Iran’s control claim is not easily bargained away; (2) insurers and shipowners require multi-week evidence rather than announcements; (3) proxies can be restrained if principals agree. Evidence that would most rapidly falsify the central view: sustained ≥80–100 daily transits under neutral rules + documented premium collapse to ≤1% within a short window.
