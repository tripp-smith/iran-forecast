I will ground this in current reporting on the 2026 Iran War, Hormuz traffic, insurance, and energy flows, then build the forecast from reference classes outward.The Gulf is not functionally normal on 6 October 2026, day 219–221 of the crisis that began with U.S. and Israeli strikes on 28 February. Oil barrels have partially recovered through dark shipping, escorts, and bypass routes. The operating regime has not.

## 1. Forecast Question

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality, as defined below, by each horizon from 6 October 2026: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months.

## 2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”

All of the following must hold at the horizon date, not merely a ceasefire headline or a lower oil price.

- Direct military activity: no sustained U.S.–Iran or Israel–Iran kinetic exchanges affecting Gulf waters, ports, or energy infrastructure for at least 14 consecutive days, and no active campaign of strikes on shipping.
- Proxy activity: attacks by Iranian-aligned groups on Gulf shipping or energy infrastructure at near-peacetime frequency, not a live Lebanon, Yemen, or Iraqi-militia front that is still hitting Gulf targets.
- Shipping security: transit through Hormuz and nearby waters does not depend on a wartime permit, toll, or convoy regime as the normal mode. Residual naval presence is acceptable; a dual-control system is not.
- Commercial transit: AIS-visible plus dark commercial transits at or above about 70 percent of the pre-crisis baseline (roughly 85 commercial crossings a day on IMF PortWatch-type counts; about 130–140 vessels a day on broader industry counts). Container services largely restored, not still rerouted via the Cape.
- War-risk insurance: mainstream hull and P&I war cover available without blanket exclusions; additional war-risk premium at or below about 0.75 percent of hull value, versus roughly 0.125–0.25 percent before the war and 3–10 percent in 2026.
- Oil and LNG continuity: Gulf crude, products, and Qatari LNG moving on ordinary commercial terms through normal load ports and Hormuz, not primarily via Yanbu, Fujairah workarounds, ship-to-ship transfers, and AIS-dark shuttle tankers. Iranian exports unblocked if the political settlement that defines normality includes an end to the naval blockade.
- Energy-market stress: the wartime risk premium largely gone. A Brent price near $80–100 is not itself evidence of normality if backwardation, freight, and insurance still price a closure option.
- Force posture: U.S. posture reduced from a wartime surge built to force the strait open. A standing carrier-and-blockade regime fails this test.
- Escalation triggers: no fresh major trigger in the prior two weeks — new mine campaign, infrastructure strike series, declared re-closure, or collapse of the political channel into renewed general war.

A temporary ceasefire, a week of escorted tankers, or regional export totals flattered by bypass pipelines do not qualify.

## 3. Current Situation Snapshot

Confirmed or well-supported as of 6 October 2026:

- The war began on 28 February 2026. Iran declared the strait closed to “hostile” shipping in early March. Day count is about 219–221.
- A U.S.–Iran memorandum of understanding was signed around 17–18 June 2026, with a demining window and an intended reopening. It did not hold. Later strikes, tanker attacks, and a U.S. naval blockade of Iranian ports followed. Talks have resumed intermittently; they are not a settlement.
- AIS-visible commercial traffic is about 10 percent of normal: roughly 8.4 crossings a day over the past week versus 84.8 before the crisis. Last-24-hour counts are in the single digits. Status trackers mark the strait “restricted,” threat level severe. Iran claims a permit regime through a Persian Gulf Strait Authority; U.S. Central Command has said the strait remains open for transit. Both descriptions can be true at once: passage is arranged, not free.
- Oil volumes are higher than AIS implies because many tankers run dark and because Saudi Arabia and the UAE are using bypass routes. Kpler put Hormuz oil-and-product clearance near 13 million barrels a day in late September against a 17 million barrel pre-war baseline on its measure, and regional exports at times near or above the pre-war average once Red Sea and Gulf of Oman loadings are included. Other trackers (Vortexa, TankerTrackers) have been lower on strait crude. The gap is disputed and material.
- Attacks have not stopped. The Council on Foreign Relations, citing U.S. Maritime Trade Operations, counts at least seven strikes on ships since 28 September. Times of Israel reporting on 5 October also notes at least seven recent tanker attacks even as some export averages look recovered.
- Insurance has not normalized. Pre-war additional war risk was about 0.125–0.25 percent of hull. 2026 quotes have run from 3 to 10 percent; late-summer and October commentary still puts the multiple near 30 times normal, with claims on the order of $2 billion. Early in the war, International Group P&I clubs cancelled war-risk extensions after reinsurance support was withdrawn. A June consortium improved availability briefly; it did not restore peacetime pricing.
- Container networks remain broken. Major carriers have been rerouting since early March. Xeneta’s summer assessment was that disruption would not reverse quickly even after a political reopening.
- Brent is near $100 on one 6 October tracker, elevated but far below the March spike. Kpler has treated the confrontation as the market’s operating environment at least through year-end.
- Force posture remains wartime: carrier presence and a blockade of Iranian oil are still part of the operating picture. That deployment is expensive and, on the CFR’s reading, not a durable way to keep the strait open without a deal.
- Proxy fronts are not closed. Hezbollah and Israel have continued to trade fire; Houthis remain a live risk to Saudi targets; some Iraqi militias have struck during the war. Soufan Center’s 5 October brief treats Iran’s non-state network as weakened but not retired, with the Houthis still relevant.

Disputed: exact daily oil flow through the strait; whether lanes are fully clear of mines; whether October carrier and Marine movements are a new surge or continuation; how much of the June MOU still binds either side. Belligerent claims of victory or of effective closure should be discounted relative to ship-tracking, insurer behavior, and attack logs.

## 4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior

1. Iran–Iraq Tanker War, 1984–88. Relevant because it is the same waterway, with state attacks on tankers, elevated war risk, and partial continuation of exports under threat. It breaks down because both belligerents wanted oil revenue and outside naval escorts eventually constrained the campaign. Traffic never fully stopped; premiums and risk stayed high until the August 1988 ceasefire, with commercial confidence returning over roughly 6–12 months. Suggests a long tail even after shooting declines.

2. 2019 Gulf tanker and Abqaiq attacks. Relevant for insurance and owner behavior after limited strikes. It breaks down because there was no general war and no closure declaration. Premia rose for weeks and eased over one to three months once the attack tempo stopped. Suggests fast normalization only if the political contest ends.

3. Red Sea and Bab el-Mandeb, late 2023–2025. Relevant because insurers and liner companies rerouted and stayed rerouted while attacks continued, even when the waterway was not legally closed. It breaks down because the Houthis are not a littoral state controlling both shores of Hormuz. Diversions and premia persisted for more than a year. Suggests 6–18 months of commercial abnormality after the political signal turns.

4. Kuwait crisis and Desert Storm, 1990–91. Relevant for oil-shock duration and postwar force posture. Hormuz itself was not the closure point. Price stress faded within months; U.S. posture did not. Suggests energy prices can normalize before the security regime does.

5. Suez closures, 1956 and 1967–75. Relevant because a chokepoint can remain politically closed or semi-closed for months to years when sovereignty is the issue. It breaks down because alternative routes existed and the legal status of Hormuz is different. Suggests that an unresolved control contest dominates the calendar.

6. June 2026 Hormuz false start. Internal reference. Transits recovered toward a fraction of normal within days of the MOU, then reversed when strikes resumed. Insurers reprice in days; owners return only under escort; both reverse on the next incident.

Base-rate prior before this week’s evidence, for this definition: 1 week 3 percent; 1 month 8 percent; 3 months 15 percent; 6 months 28 percent; 1 year 42 percent; 18 months 55 percent. The prior already assumes that chokepoint wars with an open political dispute do not normalize on a ceasefire announcement.

## 6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization

1. Both sides have already accepted, in the June MOU, that reopening Hormuz is negotiable. The channel has not died.
2. Gulf producers have shown they can move large volumes under escort and via bypass. Physical impossibility is not the constraint.
3. Insurers will write the risk when a pause looks real, as they did within days of the June deal. Pricing is reversible.
4. The wartime U.S. naval posture is costly. Washington has a reason to trade force presence for a transit regime.
5. Buyers in Asia have adapted with stocks, dark barrels, and non-Gulf supply. That reduces panic but also reduces pressure for a perfect settlement.

## 7. Key Drivers Against Normalization

1. Iran treats the ability to condition Hormuz as its remaining strategic lever. A permit-and-fee system is a political objective, not an accident.
2. Attack tempo in late September and early October shows the pause is not yet self-sustaining.
3. Insurance and liner behavior ratchet. Cover was withdrawn in 72 hours in March; restoring peacetime terms requires a clean period measured in months, not days.
4. Bypass infrastructure and dark-fleet practices will outlast the war. “Normal” route share may not return even if barrels do.
5. Lebanon, Yemen, and militia theaters can re-trigger Gulf strikes without a new decision in Tehran to reopen general war.
6. No agreed end-state on nuclear limits, sanctions, or the blockade. Force drawdown without that deal is unlikely.

Ranked gating items: attack pause, insurance terms, and the transit regime. Oil volumes are not gating. Prices are not gating.

## 9. Final Probability Forecasts by Horizon

### 1 week
- Probability: 2%
- Base-rate anchor: 3%
- Current-evidence adjustment: −1 point. Attacks this week, AIS near 10 percent, premia still crisis-level.
- Core rationale: Nothing in the current operating picture can flip to the joint definition inside seven days. A ceasefire headline would not qualify.
- Main gating factor: insurance and transit regime.
- What would raise this forecast materially: not applicable inside the window; a total stop in attacks would still leave premia and permits in place.
- What would lower this forecast materially: a new mine-laying claim or a declared re-closure.
- Confidence in this estimate: High

### 1 month
- Probability: 5%
- Base-rate anchor: 8%
- Current-evidence adjustment: −3 points. The June false start showed that a month is enough for a partial traffic bounce and not enough for insurer or owner normalization after fresh strikes.
- Core rationale: Talks can produce another pause. Owners will not treat a permit-and-escort system as normal, and underwriters will not cut premia to under 0.75 percent while UKMTO logs are still active.
- Main gating factor: a clean attack record long enough for reinsurers.
- What would raise this forecast materially: a signed reopening protocol, published mine-clearance completion, and two weeks with zero ship strikes.
- What would lower this forecast materially: carrier strikes on coastal launch sites or another Bahrain/Kuwait-style exchange.
- Confidence in this estimate: High

### 3 months
- Probability: 11%
- Base-rate anchor: 15%
- Current-evidence adjustment: −4 points. Late-September attack tempo and the still-open Lebanon file make a clean quarter unlikely. Bypass economics also reduce urgency.
- Core rationale: By early January a deal could reopen traffic on paper. Historical insurer behavior says premia and liner networks lag the paper by months. Three months is enough for a supervised corridor, not for this definition.
- Main gating factor: political settlement credible enough to end the permit regime.
- What would raise this forecast materially: blockade lifted, Iranian fees withdrawn, and mainstream P&I clubs restoring war extensions.
- What would lower this forecast materially: collapse of the talks channel or a Houthi or militia campaign against Gulf energy assets.
- Confidence in this estimate: Medium

### 6 months
- Probability: 20%
- Base-rate anchor: 28%
- Current-evidence adjustment: −8 points. Half a year is the first horizon at which mine clearance, liner repositioning, and a reinsurer review could coincide, but only if the political file is actually closed. Current evidence does not show that path as the base case.
- Core rationale: Red Sea experience says rerouting sticks for at least two quarters after attacks fade. The Tanker War says risk premia outlast the last shot. Both apply unless a formal transit settlement removes the closure option.
- Main gating factor: durable U.S.–Iran restraint plus insurer re-entry.
- What would raise this forecast materially: 90 days without a ship strike and war-risk quotes under 1 percent.
- What would lower this forecast materially: a decision in Washington to keep the blockade as the strategy through mid-2027, or an Iranian choice to keep fees as the price of passage.
- Confidence in this estimate: Medium

### 1 year
- Probability: 34%
- Base-rate anchor: 42%
- Current-evidence adjustment: −8 points. A year is long enough for fatigue, Asian buyer pressure, and cost of the naval surge to produce a deal. It is also long enough for another round of strikes. The unresolved control contest keeps this below the prior.
- Core rationale: The modal path is a messy supervised regime, not full normality. Full normality requires flag-neutral transit, ordinary insurance, and a reduced force posture. One of those three is likely still missing in October 2027.
- Main gating factor: whether the strait’s political status is settled or merely managed.
- What would raise this forecast materially: a written transit regime with no tolls, sanctions relief tied to compliance, and visible carrier drawdown.
- What would lower this forecast materially: renewed general war or a frozen conflict in which dark convoys become the permanent system.
- Confidence in this estimate: Low

### 18 months
- Probability: 46%
- Base-rate anchor: 55%
- Current-evidence adjustment: −9 points. Time raises the chance of a settlement and of commercial habituation, but the definition excludes habituation to a wartime regime. Suez-length political closures and the Tanker War tail argue against crossing 50 percent without a deal.
- Core rationale: By April 2028 either a settlement has reopened ordinary commerce or a semi-closed, escorted, high-premium system has become the new baseline. Those two outcomes are close. The second fails the definition, so the probability stays under half.
- Main gating factor: settlement versus institutionalized restriction.
- What would raise this forecast materially: a year of peacetime premia and pre-war container service counts.
- What would lower this forecast materially: evidence that Iranian permit authority has been accepted by major flag states, or a second war cycle.
- Confidence in this estimate: Low

Long-horizon figures stay below 80 percent because the definition requires the closure option to be removed, not merely unused. Current evidence does not show that removal as the likely state even at 18 months.

## 10. Most Likely Path

Through late 2026, intermittent talks and intermittent strikes. Oil moves in bursts under escort and via bypass; AIS-visible traffic stays a fraction of normal; premia stay several percent of hull. Container lines do not restore Gulf strings. Into 2027, the modal outcome is a supervised corridor: fewer attacks, more barrels, insurance still several times pre-war, U.S. naval presence still the condition of passage, Iranian political claims over the strait unresolved. That path is partial stabilization. It is not functional normality under the definition used here. A true settlement is plausible inside a year and more plausible by 18 months, but it is not the base case.

## 11. Faster Normalization Case

Washington and Tehran trade sanctions relief and a nuclear pause for an end to the blockade and to transit fees. Mine clearance is certified. Thirty to sixty days pass without a ship strike. Reinsurers reopen war extensions; premia fall under 1 percent; liners announce Gulf strings. Proxies are told to hold. On that path, the definition could be met around the six-month horizon and would likely be met by a year. Probability of this path inside six months is about 15 percent; inside a year about 30 percent. The June bounce shows the commercial system can move quickly once the political signal is believed. It also shows how fast the signal can be revoked.

## 12. Prolonged Instability Case

Talks fail, the blockade stays, and Iran keeps a permit regime backed by sporadic missile and drone attacks. A Lebanon or Yemen flare-up produces another round of coastal strikes. Insurers keep the Gulf in a listed high-risk area. Bypass pipelines and dark fleets become semi-permanent. Premia never sustainably break 2 percent. This path leaves the definition unmet at 18 months. Probability of still failing the definition at 18 months is the complement of the headline forecast, about 54 percent. The load-bearing piece is Iran’s incentive to retain a demonstrated closure option.

## 13. Red-Team Critique

The forecast may be too low if “functional normality” is interpreted by markets as “barrels flow and Brent is under $100.” On that weaker test, late-September export data would already justify much higher probabilities by six months. It may be too high if the control contest is structural: once a state has closed Hormuz and charged for passage, peacetime insurance terms may not return for years, as with prolonged chokepoint politicization. Dark-fleet measurement also cuts both ways. If true commercial transits are already far above AIS, the traffic gap is smaller than the 10 percent figure implies, but the security regime is worse, because ships are transiting only with transponders off. The estimate is most exposed to a single political event, not to a gradual operational improvement.

## 14. Critical Signals to Monitor

- UKMTO and JMIC incident counts, and whether seven-day ship-strike counts go to zero.
- Additional war-risk quotes for a standard VLCC, and whether International Group clubs restore war extensions without carve-outs.
- IMF PortWatch and AIS daily commercial crossings versus the pre-crisis ~85, plus the share of dark tankers.
- Kpler, Vortexa, and TankerTrackers Hormuz clearance versus bypass loadings. Convergence of those series matters more than any one print.
- Formal status of the blockade and of any Iranian fee or permit requirement.
- Carrier and destroyer days in the Gulf and Gulf of Oman.
- Hezbollah, Houthi, and Iraqi-militia attack logs against Gulf targets.
- Qatari LNG liftings through Hormuz rather than swap or shutdown arrangements.
- A written transit annex, not a communiqué. That is the single highest-value update.
