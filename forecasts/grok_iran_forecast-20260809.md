**1. Forecast Question**

Probability that the Persian Gulf returns to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War (commenced 28 February 2026) by horizons of 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months from 9 August 2026.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Functional normality is achieved only when **all** of the following are simultaneously true and sustained for at least 14 consecutive days (to exclude temporary pauses):

- Direct military activity: No major US/Israeli or Iranian airstrikes, ballistic missile barrages, or large-scale drone campaigns against each other’s territory, bases, or critical energy infrastructure in the Gulf theater. Limited defensive intercepts or isolated incidents do not disqualify if they remain below pre-war “gray zone” thresholds.
- Proxy activity: No sustained or coordinated attacks by Iran-aligned groups (Houthis, Iraqi militias, residual Hezbollah maritime elements) on commercial shipping, energy infrastructure, or Gulf state ports that materially disrupt operations.
- Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters: Commercial vessels transit without routine IRGC boarding, warning shots, drone/missile strikes, or mine threats. Both northern and southern corridors are usable without mandatory Iranian permits or coercive routing. No active US naval blockade of Iranian ports.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel transits through Hormuz recover to ≥80% of pre-war baseline (pre-28 Feb 2026 average ~100–135 vessels/day depending on source; target ≥80–110). Tanker and LNG carrier movements resume without systematic diversion or prolonged anchoring.
- War-risk insurance conditions: War-risk premiums for Hormuz transit fall to ≤2× pre-war levels (or equivalent market-clearing rates that do not deter mainstream Western and Asian owners). Major P&I clubs and underwriters resume standard cover without exclusion zones or prohibitive deductibles.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf exporters (Saudi, UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Iraq) restore export volumes to ≥85% of pre-war contractual levels via Hormuz or viable alternatives. No force majeure declarations remaining for major producers due to security. Iranian oil exports stabilize under whatever sanctions regime is then in force without blockade-induced collapse.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent crude sustained volatility (30-day realized) returns to levels consistent with normal geopolitical risk premium (not crisis spikes >$20–30/bbl moves). No persistent 10%+ global oil/LNG supply offline attributable to Gulf disruption.
- Force posture of major actors: US/CENTCOM posture shifts from active campaign/blockade to defensive presence and escort operations only. Iranian forces cease active enforcement of exclusive control claims via kinetic means. No major new forward deployments signaling imminent escalation.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No assassination of senior leaders, nuclear-site strikes that cross red lines, or formal declarations of expanded war aims that restart large-scale hostilities.

A temporary ceasefire, partial reopening under Iranian toll/permit regime, lower oil prices from demand destruction, or battlefield pause alone does **not** meet the definition. Commercial and insurance normalization is required alongside military de-escalation.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant reference classes (3–6):

1. **1980–88 Tanker War (Iran-Iraq)**: Prolonged attacks on Gulf shipping amid wider war. Shipping adapted with escorts, insurance spiked but markets functioned with elevated risk. Full commercial normalization lagged major fighting by months to years. Relevance: direct Hormuz/Gulf chokepoint precedent. Breaks down: conventional interstate war without superpower direct intervention or nuclear shadow; no modern precision drones/missiles at scale. Suggests: partial transit recovery in 3–6 months of reduced intensity; full insurance/commercial normality 12–24+ months.

2. **2019 Gulf tanker attacks / Iran-US tensions (including Abqaiq)**: Gray-zone attacks, temporary spikes in premiums and diversions, rapid partial recovery once kinetic phase ended. Relevance: modern Iranian asymmetric maritime coercion. Breaks down: limited duration, no full war or leadership decapitation. Suggests: 1–3 months for transit recovery if de-escalation holds; insurance slower (3–6 months).

3. **2023–25 Red Sea / Houthi campaign**: Sustained disruption of a major chokepoint by Iranian proxy. Traffic rerouted (Cape), premiums elevated for extended periods; partial recovery only with sustained naval presence and reduced attacks. Relevance: proxy maritime warfare + insurance response. Breaks down: Red Sea vs. Hormuz criticality; different great-power involvement. Suggests: 6–18 months for functional normalization even after peak attacks subside.

4. **1990–91 Gulf War and aftermath**: Rapid coalition military victory followed by residual Iraqi threats and slow full commercial confidence recovery. Relevance: major conventional war involving US and Gulf energy infrastructure. Breaks down: clear military defeat of one side; no ongoing Iranian-style asymmetric hold on chokepoint. Suggests: military pause in weeks–months; commercial/insurance normality 6–12 months.

5. **2024–25 Israel-Hezbollah / limited Iran direct exchanges**: Cycles of escalation and temporary pauses. Relevance: recent Iran-Israel dynamics and proxy restraint thresholds. Breaks down: not centered on Hormuz shipping. Suggests: pauses of weeks to a few months common; durable multi-domain calm rarer without political settlement.

6. **Broader post-major Middle East wars (e.g., 2003 Iraq invasion residual effects on energy confidence)**: Commercial normalization often trails kinetic end by 6–18 months due to residual risk pricing and infrastructure repair.

**Base-rate prior** (before case-specific updating; probability of meeting the full operational definition):

- 1 week: 1–3%
- 1 month: 5–12%
- 3 months: 15–25%
- 6 months: 25–40%
- 1 year: 40–55%
- 18 months: 50–65%

These priors reflect that military pauses are more common than simultaneous shipping/insurance/export normalization, and that chokepoint disruptions historically exhibit long tails.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization** (ranked)

1. Mutual economic pain and US munitions/ political fatigue creating off-ramp pressure (highest weight).
2. Oman mediation channel producing a limited technical transit arrangement.
3. Gulf states’ strong preference for de-escalation and energy revenue recovery.
4. Adaptation already visible (partial alternative routes, Chinese/Iranian-linked traffic continuing).
5. Potential for a narrow, performance-based ceasefire that freezes kinetic activity.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization** (ranked)

1. Iranian insistence on formal recognition of enhanced control/sovereignty over Hormuz plus broader political/economic concessions (core load-bearing obstacle).
2. Residual IRGC operational capacity and willingness to use kinetic enforcement against non-compliant shipping.
3. Proxy networks (Houthis, Iraqi groups) that can independently or on cue disrupt without full Iranian state decision.
4. Trust deficit after multiple collapsed interim deals; each violation raises future insurance and commercial risk pricing.
5. Domestic Iranian politics (succession after Khamenei, hardliner influence) and US domestic/political constraints on concessions.
6. Second-order effects: elevated baseline risk premium, infrastructure damage, and buyer diversification that persist even after kinetic pause.

Probabilities are internally consistent: rising but concave, reflecting both accumulating opportunities for deals and accumulating opportunities for spoilers/new shocks. Shipping/insurance components are the primary brakes relative to pure military pause probabilities.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued “no war, no peace” with intermittent technical progress on routes via Oman, periodic kinetic incidents, depressed but not zero transit (Chinese/Iranian-linked and some risk-tolerant traffic), elevated insurance, and gradual partial recovery of non-Iranian Gulf exports via alternatives or cautious Hormuz use. A narrow freeze becomes more likely in the 3–6 month window; full multi-domain normality remains a minority outcome even at 18 months. Energy markets adapt via higher prices, alternative routes, and demand destruction rather than returning to pre-war risk pricing.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Strongest path: US and Iran reach a performance-based package within weeks (blockade lift for verified free transit + limited sanctions relief + freeze on strikes) that Oman and Gulf states underwrite. Zero major incidents for 30+ days triggers insurer rate cuts. Proxies stand down under Iranian instruction. Probability of this path materializing enough to hit the definition by 3 months is roughly 15–20% conditional; it would raise the 3-month forecast toward 40%+.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Strongest path: Iranian hardliners treat Hormuz control as non-negotiable core interest; technical talks produce only a contested dual-route regime that markets reject. Spoilers (proxies or IRGC elements) conduct periodic attacks. US is constrained by munitions and politics from decisive escalation but maintains blockade pressure. Result: chronic elevated risk, partial Chinese-dominated traffic, persistent high premiums, and Gulf export volumes recovering only slowly via non-Hormuz routes. This path keeps probabilities suppressed across all horizons.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Possible overestimation of normalization: The forecast may underweight the speed of market adaptation and the coercive power of sustained economic pain on Iran (blockade + oil revenue loss). If a technical deal is implemented cleanly, insurance could move faster than historical analogs.  

Possible underestimation of persistence: The forecast may underweight Iranian resolve on sovereignty symbolism after leadership change and the ease with which proxies can re-escalate. Reference classes from the Tanker War and Red Sea suggest commercial tails longer than the 18-month 58% implies. Load-bearing assumption—that economic pressure eventually forces Iranian compromise on control claims—could fail if hardliners prioritize leverage over revenue. Long-horizon numbers carry the usual superforecasting discount for unforeseen shocks.
