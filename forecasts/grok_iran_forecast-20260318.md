**Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
“Return to functional normality” is defined operationally and observably across domains as:  
- **Direct military activity**: Zero confirmed US/Israeli strikes on Iranian targets or Iranian missile/drone/naval attacks on Gulf shipping/assets for ≥30 consecutive days.  
- **Proxy activity**: Hezbollah/Houthi/militia attacks on Gulf or related targets at ≤5 incidents per month (verified via UKMTO/CENTCOM reporting).  
- **Shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters**: Sustained transit with <1 confirmed attack per month and US/coalition naval escort presence at pre-war levels.  
- **Commercial transit levels**: Average daily vessel crossings ≥100 (≥80% of pre-war baseline of ~135–138), sustained for ≥30 days (via AIS/tanker-tracker data).  
- **War-risk insurance conditions**: Premiums ≤0.5% of hull value (renewable without special US backstop), full P&I coverage available without force majeure clauses.  
- **Oil and LNG export continuity**: Gulf exports (Saudi, Qatar, UAE, Kuwait, Iraq) at ≥90% of pre-war volumes (IEA/Kpler data); no force majeure declarations; Iranian exports secondary.  
- **Energy market stress and volatility**: Brent crude volatility index ≤ historical peacetime average (~15–20%); no sustained supply shock premiums.  
- **Force posture of major actors**: US/Israeli/Gulf forces at DEFCON-equivalent 4 or below with partial drawdowns; no heightened alerts or mine-laying.  
- **Absence of major new escalation triggers**: No strikes on Kharg Island oil facilities, Ras Tanura, Ras Laffan, or equivalent for ≥60 days.  

A temporary ceasefire or battlefield pause does not qualify. Lower oil prices alone do not qualify. Commercial normalization must lag military pauses by weeks due to insurer and buyer risk aversion.

**Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Reference classes (outside-in base rates before case-specific adjustment):  

1. **Iran-Iraq Tanker War (1980–88)**: Relevant—prolonged maritime attacks in same chokepoint, insurance spikes, export disruptions. Timeline to functional commercial recovery: 6–18+ months post-ceasefire (full insurance/transit lag). Breaks down: modern US naval backstop and shorter war possible here. Suggests: low short-term probability, gradual long-term.  

2. **2019–2020 Iranian tanker/Abqaiq attacks**: Relevant—targeted Hormuz/Gulf shipping disruptions, brief insurance surge. Recovery: commercial transit/insurance normalized in 1–3 months as tensions de-escalated. Breaks down: lower intensity than full war. Suggests: possible 3–6 month recovery if quick de-escalation.  

3. **Red Sea Houthi crisis (2023–ongoing)**: Relevant—chokepoint rerouting, insurance 1–2% spikes, partial export shifts. Partial normalization: 6–12 months (reroutes stabilized but premiums elevated). Breaks down: smaller scale, no direct state war. Suggests: commercial lag even after military calm.  

4. **1991 Gulf War**: Relevant—rapid military phase, post-ceasefire shipping/insurance recovery. Timeline: 1–3 months to pre-war transit levels. Breaks down: coalition victory vs. current attrition risk. Suggests: faster if decisive end.  

5. **2003 Iraq War initial phase**: Relevant—Gulf disruption from regime change fighting. Recovery: exports/shipping in 2–6 months post-major combat. Breaks down: no Hormuz closure equivalent.  

**Base-rate prior** (timeline-based, pre-evidence):  
- 1 week: ~1%  
- 1 month: ~5%  
- 3 months: ~15–20%  
- 6 months: ~30–40%  
- 1 year: ~50–60%  
- 18 months: ~65–75%  

Longer horizons reflect historical lag between military pause and insurer/buyer confidence.

**Key Drivers Toward Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Decisive degradation of Iranian missile/naval capabilities + regime pressure leading to quick de-escalation or collapse.  
2. Effective US/coalition naval escort regime + insurance backstop restoring limited transit.  
3. Rapid Gulf state diplomatic off-ramps or proxy restraint.  
4. Buyer/insurer confidence rebound if attacks cease for 30+ days.

**Key Drivers Against Normalization** (ranked)  
1. Prolonged attrition war (Iran firing fewer but sustained missiles; US/Israel targeting infrastructure).  
2. Insurance and crew risk aversion lagging any pause by weeks/months.  
3. Proxy/militia activation or mine-laying escalation.  
4. Cascading effects: refinery/port damage, Asian/European buyer shifts to alternatives, Gulf political caution.

- Major reduction in direct strikes: 1 wk ~10%, 1 mo ~25%, 3 mo ~45%, 6 mo ~65%, 1 yr ~80%, 18 mo ~90%.

- Oil/LNG export recovery: gated by Qatar/Saudi restart + transit; similar to transit but +1 month lag.  
- Proxy restraint + force drawdown + no new triggers: aligned with direct strikes but lower short-term due to Hezbollah dynamics.

**Faster Normalization Case** (strongest argument)  
Rapid Iranian military collapse or leadership fracture leads to quick ceasefire (within 1–2 months). US naval escorts + backstop restore transit immediately; Qatar restart in weeks. Insurance normalizes by 3 months; full recovery by 6 months. Probability weight: ~20–25% of scenarios.

**Prolonged Instability Case** (strongest argument)  
Stalemate with Iran conserving missiles, sporadic proxy/mining activity, and repeated low-level strikes. Insurers remain cautious; buyers lock in alternatives. Recovery stretched to 12–18+ months with persistent volatility. Probability weight: ~30–35% of scenarios.

**Red-Team Critique**

Load-bearing assumptions: no major new escalation (e.g., direct Gulf state entry) and insurance lag not exceeding historical 1–2 months. Evidence base thin on exact Iranian remaining capability.
