**1. Forecast Question**

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each of these horizons: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months.

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

“Return to functional normality” requires simultaneous, sustained satisfaction of the following observable criteria for at least 30 consecutive days (longer for insurance and force posture):

- Direct military activity: No sustained US/Israeli or Iranian strikes on territory, major bases, or forces; isolated incidents rare and non-escalatory.
- Proxy activity: Houthis, Iraqi groups, and other Iran-aligned actors not conducting attacks that materially disrupt Gulf shipping, Saudi/UAE energy infrastructure, or partner forces at scale.
- Shipping security (Strait of Hormuz and adjacent waters): ≤1–2 minor incidents per month; no active mine threat or Iranian permit/fee regime enforced against commercial traffic; free transit without coordination with Iranian authorities required for normal commercial vessels.
- Commercial transit levels: Aggregate daily vessel transits (AIS-visible plus credible estimates of dark shipping) ≥70% of pre-war baseline (~90–100+ of ~125–140 vessels/day).
- War-risk insurance: Additional war-risk premiums ≤1% of hull value (or ≤4–5× pre-war ~0.25% levels) with broad private-market capacity available; no sustained reliance on government backstops for routine traffic.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Non-Iranian Gulf crude/products flows (Hormuz + bypasses) ≥90% of pre-war; LNG (especially Qatari) restored toward pre-war levels accounting for any physical damage; Iranian exports not under kinetic blockade.
- Energy market stress and volatility: Brent (and key refined product) prices and volatility not elevated by a sustained Gulf-specific risk premium of >15–20%; markets reflecting fundamentals rather than acute disruption.
- Force posture of major actors: US/partner naval and air surge forces drawn down from wartime levels; no active US naval blockade of Iranian ports; Iran not maintaining an offensive anti-shipping posture in Hormuz.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No fresh large-scale attacks on energy infrastructure, new closure declarations, major proxy activation, or breakdown of any diplomatic channel that restarts kinetic escalation.

A temporary ceasefire, lower oil prices alone, or a battlefield pause without commercial and insurance normalization does not meet the definition.

**3. Current Situation Snapshot**

As of 10–11 October 2026 (roughly Day 225–226 of the conflict that began ~28 February 2026):

- The war continues as a standoff. Major direct US/Israeli strikes on Iranian territory have paused for weeks; Trump has stated no US strikes before the 3 November midterms. Iranian attacks on commercial shipping continue and have surged (11+ incidents in early October, including outside the strait near UAE/Qatar waters). IRGC claims control and requires permits via its “Persian Gulf Strait Authority.”
- US naval blockade of Iranian ports remains in force (reinstated mid-July). US forces are facilitating some “dark” or escorted transits in southern corridors.
- AIS-visible commercial traffic is ~10% of normal (~10 vessels/day vs. pre-war ~85–140). Oil flows have partially recovered via Saudi/UAE pipelines, ship-to-ship transfers, and selective Hormuz crossings (non-Iranian Gulf exports near or approaching pre-war levels excluding Iran in September data from Kpler/Vortexa; recent attacks have reduced throughput). LNG remains heavily constrained; some Qatari cargoes moving but far below normal, with reported damage to export capacity.
- Brent ~$103–105. War-risk insurance is extremely elevated (reports of 7.5–10% of hull value or ~30× normal); capacity has tightened; US-backed schemes exist but are not fully activating for unrestricted traffic.
- Houthis are active against Saudi targets (recent Riyadh airport strikes causing casualties; Saudi-led responses). Other proxy activity is limited but present.
- Diplomacy via intermediaries continues but remains stalled; June MOU collapsed; no deal in sight.
- US force posture is elevated (carriers and amphibious groups in or deploying to the region; blockade enforcement ongoing).

Facts above draw from contemporaneous reporting by Reuters, GlobalSecurity.org daily updates, JINSA, Kpler/Vortexa analyses, UKMTO/JMIC, CENTCOM statements, and maritime trackers. Some transit and oil-volume figures vary by source and include dark shipping estimates; claims of control or exact attack attribution are often contested.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant historical reference classes:

- Tanker War (1984–1988, Iran-Iraq War): Sustained anti-shipping campaign in the Gulf/Hormuz. Relevant for maritime disruption and insurance effects. Strait never fully closed; oil largely continued. Stabilization of shipping required broader war end + US convoy operations (Earnest Will). Suggests multi-year timeline if kinetic war continues; partial adaptation possible within months under protection. Breaks down because today’s conflict involves a US blockade of Iran and Iranian claims of sovereign control.
- 1990–91 Gulf War: Short, decisive conflict. Shipping and markets normalized within weeks to a few months after ceasefire. Relevant for rapid post-kinetic recovery. Breaks down because current war is attritional and unresolved.
- 2019 tanker/Abqaiq incidents: Brief disruptions recovered in days to weeks once kinetic risk subsided. Suggests fast recovery if threat ends. Breaks down due to limited duration and scope versus sustained 2026 campaign.
- Red Sea/Bab el-Mandeb Houthi campaign (2023–2025+): Prolonged disruption; significant traffic diversion, elevated insurance, partial adaptation but no full normality for 1–2+ years. Relevant for proxy-driven chokepoint risk and commercial resilience. Breaks down because Hormuz has fewer easy bypasses and higher strategic stakes.
- Operation Earnest Will (1987–88): US-protected convoys restored Kuwaiti tanker traffic amid ongoing Tanker War. Shows protection can enable partial flows without full political resolution. Breaks down on scale and Iranian willingness to tolerate protected traffic.
- Iran-Iraq War overall (1980–88): Ended only with external pressure and UN resolution; maritime phase tracked the land war. Suggests long duration absent decisive political settlement.

Base-rate prior (before current-case adjustments): Probability of full multi-domain normality rises slowly while kinetic risk and political deadlock persist. Rough prior: 1 week ~1–2%; 1 month ~5%; 3 months ~15%; 6 months ~25–30%; 1 year ~40%; 18 months ~50%. Short horizons are constrained by residual risk and commercial lag; longer horizons depend on political resolution.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

- Mutual interest in limiting further economic damage (Iran’s blockade-induced revenue loss; global energy costs).
- Partial commercial adaptation already visible (pipelines, STS, selective dark/US-facilitated transits for oil).
- Diplomatic channels remain open via intermediaries.
- Trump’s stated pause before midterms reduces near-term major escalation risk.
- Historical pattern that shipping can adapt under protection if major kinetic attacks subside.

Ranked: economic incentives and adaptation > short-term political pause > residual diplomacy.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

- Ongoing Iranian attacks on shipping and assertion of control/permit regime.
- Active US blockade and elevated force posture (carriers, enforcement).
- Proxy activity (Houthis) creating secondary fronts and energy risks.
- Extremely elevated insurance costs and capacity constraints (gating factor for commercial return).
- Physical damage (e.g., Qatari LNG trains) requiring multi-year repair.
- Political deadlock on core issues (Hormuz control, nuclear, sanctions, blockade).
- Risk of post-midterm escalation.

Ranked: maritime kinetic risk + insurance + political deadlock > proxy activity > physical damage.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Continued low-to-medium intensity standoff through the midterms and into early 2027: intermittent shipping attacks, persistent elevated insurance, partial oil adaptation via bypasses/STS/dark traffic, constrained LNG, active blockade, and proxy activity. Gradual commercial and diplomatic progress possible if both sides face sustained economic costs, but full functional normality remains unlikely within 12 months absent a political breakthrough. Markets and shippers adapt imperfectly rather than normalize.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

Strongest case: Post-midterm political deal (or sustained mutual restraint) that ends the blockade, ceases Iranian attacks, and opens a free or lightly regulated corridor. Insurance markets reprice within weeks once attack risk collapses; US forces facilitate then draw down. Oil/LNG rebound quickly (subject to physical repairs). Historical precedent: rapid recovery after short decisive wars or effective protection + ceasefire. Probability of this path materializing within 6 months remains low but non-zero (~15–20% conditional on deal).

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Strongest case: Attritional stalemate persists or escalates after midterms. Iran maintains pressure on shipping to extract concessions; US keeps blockade; Houthis expand operations; insurance capacity stays tight; physical damage (LNG) and new bypass infrastructure lock in a lower equilibrium. Precedent: multi-year Tanker War or Red Sea campaign. Full normality delayed well beyond 18 months. This path has material probability (~30–40%).

**13. Red-Team Critique**

I may be overestimating normalization speed by under-weighting the durability of Iranian maritime leverage and the stickiness of insurance/market risk premia once elevated. Commercial adaptation for oil does not equal full normality; LNG and general cargo lag more severely. I may be underestimating it if economic pressure forces a quicker political deal than reference classes suggest, or if US protection schemes scale faster than expected. Load-bearing assumptions: that major direct strikes stay paused near-term, that insurance is a hard gate, and that Iranian leadership prioritizes regime survival and revenue over maximal disruption. Evidence that would prove the view wrong: a sudden comprehensive deal with verified free transit and premium collapse (faster) or major new escalation with infrastructure destruction (slower).
