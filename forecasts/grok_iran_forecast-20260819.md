**1. Forecast Question**

Probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War (began ~28 February 2026) by each horizon: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 18 months (from ~19 August 2026).

**2. Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**

Observable, multi-domain standard (all must hold simultaneously for a sustained period, not a single-day snapshot):

- Direct military activity: No sustained kinetic exchanges between Iran and US/Israel/Gulf states involving ballistic missiles, drones, or major airstrikes on territory or bases in the Gulf region for ≥14 consecutive days; residual low-level incidents below the threshold that triggers automatic retaliation cycles.
- Proxy activity: No significant activation or escalation by Iran-aligned groups (Houthis, Hezbollah remnants, Iraqi/Syrian militias) that materially disrupts Gulf shipping, energy infrastructure, or US/partner forces.
- Shipping security (Strait of Hormuz and approaches): Commercial vessels transit without systematic attacks, boardings, mine threats, or IRGC “coordination” mandates that effectively control access; UKMTO/JMICS threat level reduced from extreme/high.
- Commercial transit levels: Daily vessel crossings through Hormuz recover to ≥70–80% of pre-war baseline (~130–140 ships/day → ≥90–110), sustained for ≥14 days, with AIS-visible traffic and no large backlog of stranded vessels.
- War-risk insurance: Premiums fall from current extremes (reported ~7.5–10% of hull value or multiples of 20–40× peacetime) to ≤2–3× pre-war levels for standard commercial coverage, enabling normal underwriting.
- Oil and LNG export continuity: Gulf producers (Saudi, UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Iraq) and residual Iranian volumes restore seaborne exports via Hormuz (or fully viable alternatives at scale) without force majeure declarations; Qatar LNG and major crude loadings resume near pre-war rates.
- Energy market stress/volatility: Brent and related benchmarks stabilize without sustained war-risk premium of $10–20+/bbl; volatility returns toward historical Gulf norms (not crisis spikes above $90+ driven by Hormuz risk).
- Force posture of major actors: No major new deployments or escalatory repositioning; US CENTCOM posture remains defensive/enforcement rather than active campaign; Iranian coastal and IRGC naval posture not in active interdiction mode.
- Absence of major new escalation triggers: No nuclear-related moves, mass casualty incidents on commercial shipping, or political shocks that reopen full hostilities.

A temporary ceasefire, partial traffic recovery under Iranian administrative control, or oil price drop from demand destruction alone does **not** meet this standard. Commercial and insurance normalization are gating factors beyond pure military pause.

**4. Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**

Relevant classes (3–6):

1. **Tanker War / Iran-Iraq War maritime phase (1980–88, especially 1984–88)**: Sustained attacks on shipping in the Gulf. Traffic never fully stopped but was severely disrupted for years; insurance soared; US reflagging and escorts eventually stabilized but full commercial normality lagged military de-escalation by months–years. Suggests multi-year partial disruption possible; functional recovery after major external intervention or war end took 6–18+ months post-peak intensity.
2. **1990–91 Gulf War / Iraq invasion of Kuwait and aftermath**: Rapid military resolution (weeks of intense fighting), but residual mines, insurance, and confidence issues delayed full shipping recovery. Post-ceasefire commercial normalization in the northern Gulf took weeks to a few months once major kinetic risk ended.
3. **Red Sea / Houthi disruption (2023–ongoing into 2026 context)**: Prolonged asymmetric attacks on commercial shipping. Traffic diverted around Africa for extended periods; insurance elevated; partial recovery only with sustained naval protection and reduced attacks. Shows that even without full closure, commercial/insurance normality can lag kinetic reduction by many months.
4. **Strait of Hormuz crises / Iranian threats and limited seizures (multiple, including 2019)**: Short-lived spikes; traffic recovered within days–weeks once acute threat subsided, because underlying state capacity and insurer confidence remained intact. Breaks down here because the 2026 case involves sustained near-closure, regime survival after leadership decapitation, and mutual blockade/interdiction.
5. **Post-ceasefire Gulf or Levantine commercial recovery (e.g., after 2006 Lebanon or earlier tanker phases)**: Military pause often precedes commercial recovery by 1–6 months due to insurer caution, charterer risk aversion, and residual proxy risk.
6. **Broader chokepoint disruptions (Suez 2021 blockage analog limited; more usefully prolonged asymmetric maritime campaigns)**: Commercial actors require sustained low-risk signals, not just declarations.

Base-rate prior (pre-current-evidence adjustment, for meeting the full operational definition):  
- 1 week: 1–3%  
- 1 month: 5–10%  
- 3 months: 15–25%  
- 6 months: 30–45%  
- 1 year: 45–60%  
- 18 months: 55–70%  

These reflect historical lag between military de-escalation and commercial/insurance recovery in Gulf/maritime conflicts, plus the high bar of the multi-domain definition. The current war’s intensity, mutual blockades, and leadership changes pull the prior downward relative to short-crisis cases.

**6. Key Drivers Toward Normalization**

Ranked: (1) Mutual economic exhaustion and Gulf state pressure for reopening (energy revenues, reconstruction). (2) Sustained US military pause + credible enforcement reducing Iranian attack capacity. (3) Successful Iran-Oman (or broader) technical route mechanism that insurers and charterers accept. (4) Iranian internal pragmatic faction gaining enough space for limited compromise on administration without full sovereignty claim. (5) Alternative pipeline/export capacity (ADCOP, East-West, Jask) partially offsetting Hormuz dependence, reducing urgency for full reopening but also reducing leverage for prolonged closure.

**7. Key Drivers Against Normalization**

Ranked: (1) Core disagreement over who controls/administers Hormuz (Iran sovereignty claims vs. freedom of navigation + US blockade). (2) Residual Iranian missile/drone/coastal interdiction capacity and willingness to use calibrated attacks. (3) Insurer and charterer risk aversion requiring prolonged clean record, not declarations. (4) Proxy and horizontal escalation options (Houthis, others). (5) Domestic political incentives on both sides (US midterms context; Iranian hardline consolidation post-leadership losses). (6) Nuclear file linkage making pure shipping deals unstable.

**10. Most Likely Path**

Protracted low-to-medium intensity stalemate: intermittent calibrated attacks and enforcement actions, partial technical arrangements via Oman or others that allow limited traffic under constrained conditions, elevated but gradually declining insurance, and slow volume recovery. Full multi-domain normality remains elusive until a more durable political understanding or decisive shift in capabilities/incentives. Commercial actors treat the Gulf as high-risk for an extended period.

**11. Faster Normalization Case**

A pragmatic Iranian leadership prioritizes economic survival, accepts a non-sovereignty administrative formula (joint or Omani-led with Iranian consultation), US lifts or relaxes blockade in tandem, 3–4 weeks of clean transits occur, insurers rapidly reprice, and Gulf states underwrite residual risk. Probability of this path is modest but non-zero within 3–6 months if domestic Iranian pressures intensify.

**12. Prolonged Instability Case**

Hardline consolidation, continued use of Hormuz as leverage, proxy activation, mutual red-line testing, and insurer/charterer permanent risk premium. Functional normality delayed beyond 18 months or achieved only at lower traffic/insurance levels that still fail the operational definition. Supported by current leadership signals and historical tanker-war duration.

**13. Red-Team Critique**

Strongest case I am overestimating normalization: The operational definition is demanding; commercial and insurance recovery historically requires longer clean records than military pauses deliver. Iranian capacity for calibrated, deniable disruption remains high; US domestic politics and midterm pressures could produce inconsistent posture. Base rates from shorter crises may be too optimistic.

Strongest case I am underestimating: Economic pain on both sides (Iranian oil revenues, Gulf damage, global energy costs, US political costs) could force faster pragmatic deals than reference classes suggest. Alternative export routes + SPR releases + demand responses already mitigate some pressure, potentially accelerating acceptance of imperfect arrangements. US military degradation of Iranian coastal assets may be more lasting than visible.

Load-bearing assumptions: (1) Insurers remain conservative; (2) Iranian control claims are non-negotiable in the near term; (3) No sudden regime-altering internal event.
