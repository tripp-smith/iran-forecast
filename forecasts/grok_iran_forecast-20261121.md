**Forecast Question**  
Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each horizon: 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 18 months.

**Operational Definition of “Return to Functional Normality”**  
Functional normality requires simultaneous, sustained (at least 2–4 weeks of observed stability) satisfaction of the following observable thresholds. A temporary ceasefire, oil-price decline, or battlefield pause alone does not qualify.

- **Direct military activity**: No sustained US–Iran exchanges of strikes (airstrikes, drones, missiles) for ≥14 consecutive days; residual incidents limited to isolated, non-escalatory events.
- **Proxy activity**: Houthis and other Iran-linked proxies cease major attacks on Saudi critical infrastructure, Gulf shipping, or bases; no new proxy fronts opened.
- **Shipping security (Hormuz and nearby waters)**: Threat level reduced from “severe” to moderate/low; daily or near-daily projectile/mine attacks cease; residual risk does not require routine naval escorts for most commercial traffic.
- **Commercial transit levels**: AIS-visible plus dark/US-facilitated Hormuz transits recover to ≥70% of pre-war baseline (~55–65+ ships/day vs. ~78–90/day pre-war). Most vessels operate with AIS on and without mandatory coordination.
- **War-risk insurance**: Premiums fall to ≤1–2% of hull value (from current 6–10% range) and become routinely available without extreme costs or short cancellation windows.
- **Oil and LNG export continuity**: Total Gulf oil flows (including Iranian crude) at or above ~95% of pre-war levels without major STS workarounds or pipeline dependence; Qatar LNG recovers toward pre-war volumes (currently well below due to damage and transit constraints).
- **Energy market stress and volatility**: No ongoing strategic reserve releases driven by Gulf disruption; Brent war premium largely eliminated; freight rates normalize.
- **Force posture**: US surge forces (multiple carriers, elevated destroyer presence) drawn down toward pre-crisis levels; Iran does not maintain active blockade enforcement posture.
- **Absence of major new escalation triggers**: No nuclear breakout moves, large-scale Israeli re-entry into strikes, or fresh attacks that reopen broad conflict.

**Current Situation Snapshot** (as of ~21 November 2026, day ~267 of the conflict)  
War began 28 February 2026 with US/Israeli strikes. After a June MOU that lapsed, the situation settled into a “no war, no peace” stalemate with intermittent limited strikes. Recent days: US strikes on Qeshm Island IRGC sites; Iranian drone/missile attacks on US-related sites in Bahrain and Kuwait (intercepted, limited damage); ongoing Iranian attacks on commercial shipping in/near Hormuz (multiple tankers hit in recent weeks, including ADNOC-related vessel with casualties).  

Hormuz is severely restricted. AIS-visible commercial traffic is roughly 7–10% of pre-war (~7–10/day vs. ~78–90/day baseline). US-facilitated and dark transits raise the effective total (UKMTO-style counts sometimes in the 30–50/day range in better periods), but volume remains far below normal. US naval blockade prevents meaningful Iranian crude exports (no confirmed successful breach of blockade zone by laden Iranian crude tankers since mid-July). Non-Iranian Gulf oil exports have largely recovered via pipelines (Saudi East-West, UAE) and limited Hormuz/STS, approaching or matching pre-war levels excluding Iran. LNG remains impaired (Qatar ~30% of prior volumes).  

War-risk insurance is extreme (6–10% of hull value; one US Navy official cited $64 million per transit, though this figure is disputed/contextual). Brent is ~$91–93. US maintains a large naval presence (three carriers reported recently). Houthis are actively attacking Saudi targets (missiles toward Riyadh, infrastructure). Negotiations are stalled primarily over nuclear sequencing (60% stockpile, enrichment), blockade/Hormuz control, and sanctions relief. Both sides appear to calculate that time favors them; post-US midterms dynamics have not produced a breakthrough.

**Reference Classes and Base-Rate Prior**  
Relevant classes (with relevance, limitations, and suggested timelines):

1. **1980s Tanker War (Iran-Iraq)**: Prolonged attacks on shipping in Gulf/Hormuz with high insurance and escorts; oil largely continued to flow. Relevance: same chokepoint and actor. Breakdown: no US blockade of Iranian exports and no modern drone/missile density. Suggests 12–36 months for functional commercial recovery after ceasefire.
2. **2019–2020 Gulf tanker attacks and tensions**: Short spikes in attacks, temporary insurance spikes, rapid recovery once direct incidents stopped. Relevance: Hormuz vulnerability. Breakdown: far lower intensity and duration. Suggests weeks to 2–3 months once military activity ceases.
3. **Red Sea/Houthi campaign (2023–2025)**: Sustained shipping disruption, high war-risk rates, rerouting; partial normalization only after military degradation or deals. Relevance: proxy maritime campaign linked to Iran. Breakdown: different chokepoint, no full blockade. Suggests 6–18 months for insurance and volume recovery.
4. **Post-ceasefire commercial lag in regional conflicts (e.g., after major Gulf flare-ups or limited wars)**: Military pause often precedes commercial confidence by 3–9 months due to insurance, mine risk, and residual posture. Relevance: gap between kinetic stop and normality. Suggests military de-escalation necessary but not sufficient.
5. **Chokepoint closures with infrastructure damage (Suez historical cases, LNG facility hits)**: Physical damage and confidence loss extend recovery. Relevance: Qatar LNG damage reported. Suggests 6–18+ months for full export restoration.

**Base-rate prior** (before current-case adjustment): 1 week ~5%; 1 month ~12%; 3 months ~30%; 6 months ~50%; 1 year ~65%; 18 months ~75–80%. These reflect that commercial and insurance normalization typically lags kinetic pauses by months in chokepoint cases, with full multi-domain recovery taking 6–18+ months even in favorable paths.

**Key Drivers Toward Normalization**  
1. Mutual reluctance for full-scale war resumption (both sides have absorbed costs; Trump signals preference for economic pressure).  
2. Partial oil export recovery via alternatives demonstrates resilience and reduces market panic.  
3. Economic pain on Iran from blockade (inflation, currency, inability to export).  
4. Mediator interest (Qatar, Oman, others) and periodic diplomatic contact.  
5. US domestic political constraints post-midterms limiting open-ended commitment.

**Key Drivers Against Normalization**  
1. Persistent kinetic activity and shipping attacks (gating for insurance and transit confidence).  
2. Active Houthi-Saudi front (proxy restraint failure).  
3. Stalled core issues (nuclear stockpile/enrichment, Hormuz control vs. blockade).  
4. Extreme insurance costs and residual mine/threat perception.  
5. Force postures and blockade remain in place; no drawdown signal.  
6. Infrastructure damage (especially Qatar LNG) and second-order buyer caution.

Ranked by importance: shipping security and insurance first (they gate commercial normality); then proxy restraint and kinetic pause; nuclear/political settlement last for full durability.

**Synthesis logic**: Shipping security + insurance are the primary gating factors for functional normality. Military pauses and oil-flow resilience can occur earlier, but commercial and insurance normalization lag. Proxy activity and force posture are reinforcing constraints. Final probabilities are not mechanical averages; they track the slower of the gating components (transit + insurance), adjusted upward modestly when other conditions improve and downward for residual escalation risk. Long-horizon figures remain below 80% because the definition is demanding and residual mine/threat/insurance confidence issues persist even after kinetic stops in historical cases.

**Most Likely Path**  
Continued “managed stalemate” with intermittent low-level strikes and shipping harassment through early 2027, gradual partial recovery in non-Iranian oil and some LNG, but insurance and full transit volumes lagging. A limited deal or de-facto quiet becomes more probable in the 6–12 month window under economic pressure, enabling functional normality closer to the 12–18 month horizon. Full multi-domain normality (including Iranian oil and normalized insurance) is unlikely before then.

**Faster Normalization Case**  
Mutual exhaustion plus successful mediation produces a sequenced deal (Hormuz reopening + partial sanctions relief in exchange for nuclear steps) within 1–3 months. Shipping attacks cease immediately; US blockade eases; insurance markets reprice downward within weeks of sustained quiet. Houthi front is separately contained. Probability mass shifts earlier by 15–25 points at 3- and 6-month horizons.

**Prolonged Instability Case**  
Nuclear gap remains unbridgeable; Israel or US resumes larger strikes; Iran intensifies shipping campaign or succeeds in partial blockade runs that trigger escalation; Houthis continue or expand attacks. Insurance stays elevated, transit remains suppressed, and force postures stay high through 2027. Probability of normality at 12–18 months falls toward 30–40%.

**Red-Team Critique**  
Strongest case that I overestimate normalization: The nuclear issue and mutual distrust are deeper than economic pressure can overcome; residual mine threats and Houthi independence keep insurance and commercial caution elevated even after a kinetic pause. Strongest case that I underestimate it: Both sides face real domestic and economic costs; a quiet de-facto arrangement (no formal deal) could allow commercial recovery faster than historical full-settlement cases once attacks drop. Load-bearing assumptions: that insurance and transit confidence require weeks-to-months of verified quiet, and that the Houthi front is not fully controllable by Tehran.
