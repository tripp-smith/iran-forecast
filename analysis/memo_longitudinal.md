# Persian Gulf normality odds stay below March after a late-November rebound

> **18-month odds: 80% → 68% (−12 pts).** Medium and long horizons are still lower than 18 March. A mid-November trough (18-month 62% on 15 Nov) partially reversed by 21 November. Commercial normality remains gated by insurance and transit—not by the next kinetic pause.

From 18 March to 21 November 2026, daily Grok superforecasts of a return to **functional normality** in the Persian Gulf after the 2026 Iran War end lower on every horizon except 1 week (2%→3%). The steepest first-to-latest cuts are still at longer ranges: 1 year 65%→55% (−10); 18 months 80%→68% (−12); 6 months 45%→38% (−7). Near-term odds stayed near the floor for most of the series.

The week after 15 November was a **partial rebound from the trough**, not a new March-level regime. From 15 to 21 November, 3-month odds rose 12→22, 6-month 28→38, 1-year 48→55, and 18-month 62→68. 20 November briefly priced 18-month odds at 78% before the 21 November run retraced to 68%.

Audience: policymakers, shipping-risk analysts, and energy traders reading the series cold. The operational bar is demanding—military pause alone does not count. Commercial transit, war-risk insurance, export continuity, force posture, and the absence of new escalation triggers must clear together.

## Long horizons absorbed most of the decline from March

The series is still a **downward translation of the medium and long curve** relative to March, with a late rebound that does not erase the net cut. Near-term odds never really left the bottom; the model kept treating the next few weeks as almost closed while revising how far a durable commercial reset could get within a year to a year and a half.

Spring lifted odds into late April (still with a formal blockade and deeply depressed transit). The mid-year memorandum window raised medium-horizon odds again in June. By early September those gains had largely reversed. Mid-November marked the softest multi-horizon reading in the series; the following six days clawed back part of that loss without restoring March’s long-horizon levels.

Treat large day-to-day moves (including the 19–20 November spike and 21 November retracement) as **high variance around a lower regime**, not as proof that commercial normality is imminent.

## Evidence shifted from early-war shock to failed settlement and commercial lag

Across 1,524 parsed evidence rows on 166 dates, the recurring nouns are transit, attacks/strikes, Hormuz, blockade, Houthi activity, oil, and memorandum/MOU language. Kinetic pauses appear; then **insurance, volume, and political terms** fail to clear the bar.

- **March — Opening shock.** Operation Epic Fury; active barrages; commercial and insurance normalization expected to trail any military pause by months.
- **Late April — Passive blockade.** Brief transit uptick around a 28 April meeting; still ~86% below baseline volumes; Ras Laffan / LNG damage a hard constraint in earlier runs.
- **June — Memorandum window.** Islamabad/MOU ceasefire narrative; forecasts briefly price incremental reopening if the memorandum holds.
- **September–November — Fragile pauses, then managed stalemate.** A June MOU that lapsed without a broader deal; US blockade plus selective facilitation; low-intensity harassment and elevated war-risk pricing as the working baseline. By late November the latest run still describes intermittent strikes, AIS-visible Hormuz traffic near ~9% of baseline, war-risk premiums in the 6–10% hull range, active Houthi attacks on Saudi targets, and stalled nuclear/blockade talks.

## Drivers reordered from “war ending soon” to settlement quality and insurance lag

Early toward-drivers emphasize coalition escorts, inventory attrition, and Omani/Qatari diplomatic channels. Against-drivers emphasize proxy cycles, insurer aversion, and mine/coastal reactivation risk.

By late April the toward-list is dominated by **meeting outcomes and short attack-free windows**; the against-list is still the **formal blockade**, collapsed transit volumes, and Iranian “insufficient” framing of talks.

By late November, toward-drivers lean on mutual reluctance for full-scale resumption, partial non-Iranian oil recovery via pipelines/STS, Iranian economic pain under blockade, and mediator contact. Against-drivers still lead: persistent shipping attacks, the Houthi–Saudi front, stalled nuclear/Hormuz terms, extreme insurance, and force-posture inertia. The narrative path remains low-intensity attrition into 2027: partial oil at elevated cost, deniable harassment, and commercial catch-up only after a political settlement that removes the incentive to tax the Strait.

## Prolonged instability stays competitive where measured

Structured scenario weights parse cleanly for only the early window (about 45 of 248 days). Where both weights exist, prolonged instability often sits at or above the faster-normalization weight (for example ~33% prolonged vs ~22% faster on 19 March). Later prose continues to treat a messy modus vivendi and a Red Sea–like coercion equilibrium as live branches even when numeric weights are missing. Do not over-read the sparse numeric scenario file as a full time series.

## Insurance and transit bind the aggregate forecast

Where decomposed sub-forecasts parse (most early dates; thinner later), **meaningful insurance normalization** and **sustained safe commercial transit** sit well below “major reduction in direct strikes.” On 18 March, strike reduction at 6 months was already ~65% while insurance was ~35% and high-volume transit ~40%. The 21 November decomposition keeps the same hierarchy: strike reduction at 6 months ~65% versus transit ~35% and insurance ~30%. A strike pause is necessary and not sufficient; insurers and carriers are the slow variables.

## Two calibration risks remain in the latest view

Relative to the evidence path, the **21 November** forecast (3% / 8% / 22% / 38% / 55% / 68%) is coherent with eight months of incomplete settlements and depressed transit, plus a modest late rebound from the mid-November trough.

1. **Underestimation of workaround capacity** if dark/escorted flows and partial oil recovery already deliver more system function than AIS-visible “normality” admits.
2. **Overestimation of long-horizon recovery** if intermittent taxation of Hormuz becomes a durable operating system rather than a bridge to a deal—especially if day-to-day rebound spikes are mistaken for a structural turn.

## Bottom line

Over 248 daily runs from 18 March to 21 November 2026, Persian Gulf functional normality **ends below the March starting point on every horizon except 1 week**, with the steepest net cuts at 6–18 months. Temporary lifts from spring diplomacy and a mid-year memorandum did not stick. A mid-November trough partially reversed in the following week; it did not restore March’s long-horizon odds. The latest path is managed stalemate and expensive partial flows—with commercial normality still gated by settlement quality and insurance, not by the next ceasefire headline alone.
