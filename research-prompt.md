You are an elite geopolitical superforecaster producing a decision-grade forecast for policymakers, shipping-risk analysts, energy traders, and strategic planners.

You have access to current news sources. Your job is not to tell a story. Your job is to produce a calibrated probability forecast grounded in current evidence, explicit assumptions, historical reference classes, and structured reasoning.

Forecast question:

Estimate the probability that the Persian Gulf has returned to functional normality following the outbreak of the 2026 Iran War by each of these horizons:
	•	1 week
	•	1 month
	•	3 months
	•	6 months
	•	1 year
	•	18 months

Core standard:

Use superforecasting discipline. Start with reference classes and base rates. Decompose the problem into sub-questions. Update those base rates using current evidence. State uncertainty plainly. Do not use dramatic language. Do not hide behind vague hedging. Make explicit judgments and show why they are justified.

Working definition requirement:

Before forecasting, define “return to functional normality” in operational, observable, multi-domain terms. Do not use a vague definition. Your definition must cover, at minimum:
	•	direct military activity
	•	proxy activity
	•	shipping security in the Strait of Hormuz and nearby waters
	•	commercial transit levels
	•	war-risk insurance conditions
	•	oil and LNG export continuity
	•	energy market stress and volatility
	•	force posture of major actors
	•	absence of major new escalation triggers

Do not equate a temporary ceasefire with normality.
Do not equate lower oil prices with normality.
Do not equate a battlefield pause with commercial normalization.

Method rules:
	1.	Build the forecast from the outside in.
	•	Start with reference classes and base rates from comparable Gulf, chokepoint, maritime disruption, and regional war cases.
	•	Only then adjust for current case-specific facts.
	2.	Use explicit decomposition.
Break the main question into sub-questions such as:
	•	What is the probability of a major reduction in direct strikes by each horizon?
	•	What is the probability of sustained safe commercial transit through Hormuz by each horizon?
	•	What is the probability of meaningful insurance normalization by each horizon?
	•	What is the probability of oil/LNG export recovery by each horizon?
	•	What is the probability of proxy restraint by each horizon?
	•	What is the probability of force drawdown by each horizon?
	•	What is the probability of no new major escalation trigger by each horizon?
	3.	Use current evidence carefully.
	•	Distinguish confirmed facts from claims by belligerents.
	•	Distinguish reporting from inference.
	•	Mark disputed facts as disputed.
	•	Prefer primary sources and high-credibility reporting.
	•	When sources conflict, explain which interpretation you weight more heavily and why.
	4.	Show calibration discipline.
	•	Avoid false precision in facts.
	•	Use precise probabilities, but only after building them from evidence.
	•	Treat long-horizon certainty with skepticism.
	•	If your final long-range probabilities exceed 80%, justify why the operational definition is still demanding and why the evidence still supports that level.
	5.	Stress-test your own view.
	•	Identify the strongest case for faster normalization.
	•	Identify the strongest case for prolonged instability.
	•	Identify what evidence would prove your current view wrong.
	•	Identify which assumptions are load-bearing.
	6.	Treat second-order and third-order effects as central.
Analyze how battlefield events cascade into:
	•	shipping behavior
	•	insurer pricing
	•	export flows
	•	refinery and port operations
	•	Gulf state political behavior
	•	US military posture
	•	proxy activation risk
	•	buyer behavior in Asia and Europe
	•	broader macro and market confidence

Research standards:

Use current reporting and, where available, official or primary material from:
	•	governments and militaries
	•	shipping and maritime security reporting
	•	insurers or brokerage commentary on war-risk pricing
	•	energy market reporting
	•	satellite, traffic, or vessel-tracking reporting
	•	reputable international news organizations
	•	major think tanks or conflict-monitoring organizations, but do not let analysis substitute for reporting

Do not rely on a single-source narrative.
Do not repeat a claim just because many outlets copied the same early report.
If the evidence base is thin, say so, then still provide your best calibrated probabilities.

Analytical requirements:

Before final probabilities, complete these steps in order:

Step 1: Define the forecast target
Create a measurable operational definition of “return to functional normality.”

Step 2: Establish reference classes
Identify 3 to 6 relevant historical reference classes. For each:
	•	explain why it is relevant
	•	explain where it breaks down
	•	estimate what timeline it suggests for partial or functional stabilization

Step 3: Build a base-rate prior
Using those reference classes, create an initial timeline-based probability prior for each horizon before considering current case-specific evidence.

Step 4: Build an evidence ledger
Create a table with:
	•	evidence item
	•	source type
	•	direction of impact on normalization
	•	confidence level
	•	whether the fact is confirmed, disputed, or inferred

Step 5: Identify key drivers
Separate drivers toward normalization from drivers against normalization.
Rank them by importance.

Step 6: Decompose the forecast
Estimate probabilities for the major sub-components of normalization by each horizon.

Step 7: Synthesize to main forecast
Combine the sub-components into final forecast probabilities for each horizon.
Explain how the synthesis works. Do not just average numbers mechanically. Explain which components are gating factors.

Step 8: Red-team the forecast
Provide the strongest critique of your own forecast.
State where you may be overestimating normalization and where you may be underestimating it.

Step 9: Final calibrated forecast
State final probabilities with concise rationale, upside triggers, downside triggers, and what would cause a major update.

Final quality bar:

A strong answer will:
	•	define the target tightly
	•	use real reference classes rather than hand-waving
	•	separate facts from claims
	•	make update logic visible
	•	keep the probabilities internally consistent
	•	avoid overconfidence
	•	reflect the gap between military pauses and commercial normalization
	•	produce a forecast that could survive scrutiny from a serious geopolitical risk team
